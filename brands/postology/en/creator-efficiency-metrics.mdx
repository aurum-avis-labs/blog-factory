**Creator efficiency** is the cost of staying on a posting rhythm. It is not your follower count. Followers say something about the audience. They say nothing about how many minutes, apps, and missed slots it took to publish the last ten posts.

This page is for a solo creator or a small brand that already posts every week and wants a few numbers that fit on a notepad. It is not a studio dashboard and it is not a growth score. If you are still deciding whether to post at all, these metrics will not help you yet.

## What to count, and what to leave

Count the work that repeats. Leave the vanity numbers in the app where they already live.

<ul class="blog-facts">
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Minutes per post</span><strong>Same type, timed</strong><p>Clock one carousel the way you clock the next one. Mixing a Reel with a text post makes the average useless.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Handoffs</span><strong>Apps opened</strong><p>Write the number of tools you touched for that post, including the chat where the caption started.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Reuse</span><strong>One brief, several posts</strong><p>A brief that becomes a feed post and a second cut is reuse. Writing a new idea from zero each time is not.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Kept slots</span><strong>Planned vs live</strong><p>Of the posts you put on the week, how many actually went out. A full calendar with a quiet feed is a miss, not a style choice.</p></li>
</ul>

Four numbers are enough. A fifth is usually a ratio you will not update. There is no universal "good" minute count here. Your baseline is the next five posts of one type, written down by you. Borrowed benchmarks from someone else's studio describe their niche, their editor, and their tools. They do not describe your Tuesday.

## The mistake: reading the audience as the workflow

The common mistake is to treat likes, reach, or follower change as the efficiency report. Those can fall while the workflow gets faster, and they can rise while you spend a full day on a single post. Use them to judge the post. Use minutes, handoffs, reuse, and kept slots to judge the system that makes posts.

A short example, made up so the columns are obvious. A creator times five feed posts: 42, 38, 55, 36, and 40 minutes. The 55-minute one included a hunt for a file that was named poorly. Handoffs sit around five apps. One brief became two posts, so reuse is thin. Four of five planned slots went live. The useful change is a file name and one fewer paste, then another five posts. It is not a new follower goal.

## Who should skip this sheet

Skip it if you do not have a rhythm yet. You cannot efficiency-score a habit that is not there. Also skip it if a client already demands a reporting deck about reach. That deck answers a different question. Keep the four workflow numbers in a note that is not the client report, or the client numbers will crowd them out within two weeks.

## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should creator efficiency include follower growth?</h3>
  <p>No. Keep audience numbers for judging posts. Efficiency is minutes, handoffs, reuse, and whether the planned slot went live.</p>
</div>
<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How many posts do I need before the numbers mean anything?</h3>
  <p>Five of the same type. One hero launch will dominate an average and tell you nothing about the ordinary week.</p>
</div>
<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is a low minute count always better?</h3>
  <p>Only if the posts still ship and you can stand behind them. A fast post you would not publish is not efficiency. It is a draft you refused.</p>
</div>
<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>What do I change first?</h3>
  <p>The step that shows up on every timed post. Often that is a file hunt or a second paste, not the writing itself.</p>
</div>
