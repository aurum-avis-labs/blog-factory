If you asked a chatbot for a week in Barcelona and got a restaurant that **does not exist**, a museum **closed on the day you arrive**, and a **30-minute transfer** that actually takes ninety with luggage, you already met the core **AI trip planning limitations**. The problem is not that AI is useless—it is that text models **sound authoritative** while they may be **out of date, incomplete, or invented**.

This article is for skeptical planners who want a **specific** fix list, not a rant. Use AI for speed; use human verification for anything that costs money, time, or safety.

## Limitation 1: Hallucinated or renamed venues

Models combine training snippets and guess plausible names. You may see:

- Restaurants that closed years ago
- “New” galleries that never opened
- Street addresses that are close but wrong

**Verify:** Official website, Google Maps pin (check recent reviews), or the venue’s Instagram within the last few months.

**Workflow habit:** Maintain a “confirmed” section in your doc. Nothing moves from AI draft to confirmed until a link checks out.

## Limitation 2: Outdated hours and seasonal closures

Hours change for holidays, renovations, and staffing. AI rarely knows **this Tuesday** or **shoulder season** rules.

**Verify:** Museum and attraction sites; note **closed days** (many European museums close Monday or Tuesday).

**Workflow habit:** Batch-verify anchors one week before departure, not months ahead when hours may still shift.

## Limitation 3: Transit gaps and fantasy timing

AI itineraries often assume:

- Flat 15-minute hops between distant neighborhoods
- Empty subway lines at midnight
- No security lines, no elevator outages, no strikes

**Verify:** Map apps with **departure time** set to your travel date; airport and inter-city rail sites for real schedules.

**Workflow habit:** Add **30–50% buffer** around every transfer you have not done before in that city.

## Limitation 4: Taste and crowd blindness

“Best viewpoint” lists ignore your fear of heights, your kid’s stroller, or your dislike of packed Instagram spots.

**Verify:** Recent traveler reviews for **wait times** and **accessibility**; swap items that do not match your party.

**Workflow habit:** Tag each AI suggestion as **try / skip / replace** in a shared doc so the group sees decisions.

## Limitation 5: Booking and price blind spots

Most AI planners do not hold live inventory. They may suggest **sold-out** exhibitions or **peak-season** hotel zones without flagging cost.

**Verify:** Booking sites and official ticket portals; set price alerts yourself.

**Workflow habit:** Book **non-refundable anchors** first, then ask AI to replan around fixed times.

## A hybrid human + AI workflow that works

| Step | Who | Output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Constraints | You | Dates, city, pace, must-dos |
| 2. First draft | AI | Day-by-day skeleton |
| 3. Map pass | You | Cluster fixes, cut 20% |
| 4. Anchor verify | You | Confirmed links + bookings |
| 5. Re-prompt | AI | Rain backups, meal gaps near confirmed pins |
| 6. Offline copy | You | PDF or notes on phone |

This is the same spine described in [how to plan a trip itinerary with AI](/blog/how-to-plan-a-trip-itinerary-with-ai)—limitations are why the verify steps exist, not optional extras.

## Prompt tricks that reduce bad drafts

Ask the model to:

- **Cite uncertainty:** “Mark anything you are not sure exists with [verify].”
- **Separate tiers:** Must-do vs optional vs rain backup
- **Respect closed days:** “Assume museums may be closed Monday; avoid scheduling then unless I confirm.”
- **Show transit mode:** Walk vs metro vs taxi for each leg

No prompt eliminates hallucinations. They reduce cleanup time.

## When AI is still worth it

AI trip planning shines when you need:

- **First-pass structure** in minutes
- **Neighborhood grouping** you had not considered
- **Alternate day plans** after weather changes
- **Shareable narrative** for friends reviewing the trip

Know the ceiling, use the strengths. For vocabulary and scope of AI itineraries, see [what is an AI travel itinerary](/blog/what-is-an-ai-travel-itinerary).

## Frequently asked questions

### Should I stop using AI after one bad plan?

No—change how you use it. Treat every venue as unconfirmed until you verify.

### Is paid AI travel software safer?

Paid tools may connect to live data for some features, but hallucinations and stale content still happen. Read what each product actually integrates.

### How long should verification take?

For a week in one city, budget **30–60 minutes** for top anchors; more for multi-city legs.

### Can I sue or get refunds if AI was wrong?

Generally you are responsible for bookings you make. AI output is advice, not a contract.

### Do I need a spreadsheet too?

Many travelers use AI for narrative days and a spreadsheet for costs and confirmation numbers—that hybrid is normal.

## Your next step

Take your last AI draft and highlight every **named venue**. Spend one session confirming or replacing them on official sites and maps. Delete unverified items from the “confirmed” calendar. That single pass teaches you more about AI trip planning limitations than any generic warning.
