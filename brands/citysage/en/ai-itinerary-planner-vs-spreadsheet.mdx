## AI itinerary planner vs spreadsheet

The **AI itinerary planner vs spreadsheet** question comes down to the job in front of you. An AI itinerary planner, or an AI trip planner in a chat, writes a readable first pass of the days from a city, dates, and a few interests. A spreadsheet keeps running totals, who paid, and the confirmation codes you already hold. On a normal city break you will use both, planner first, sheet for anything you would hate to get wrong.

This is for someone planning a trip with friends, a partner, or for a social post, and comparing a blank grid with a generated day plan. It is a poor fit for company expense rules.

If you want the drafting steps on their own, start with [how to plan a trip itinerary with AI](/blog/how-to-plan-a-trip-itinerary-with-ai). The comparison below is where the sheet still earns a tab.

An AI itinerary planner is good at a story: how Thursday connects to Friday, which neighborhood is the base, what to swap if it rains. Plain-language edits work ("slow Tuesday down", "keep one museum"). Some tools also give you a link friends can open, which a raw grid rarely does.

A spreadsheet is good at columns you invent. Budget totals, a currency column, per-person splits, loyalty numbers, packing flags, "not booked yet". Sort unpaid items. Filter every train. The file works in whatever sheet app you already trust, and a copy on your phone still opens when the login page does not.

## Side by side

| Job | AI itinerary planner | Spreadsheet |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time to a first day plan | Fast once you can describe the trip | Slow until a template exists |
| Neighborhood order | Suggests a flow you still check on a map | You decide the order yourself |
| Money, splits, exchange rates | Treat figures as placeholders | Formulas you can audit |
| Confirmation codes | Easy to lose inside a paragraph | A column |
| What friends will actually read | A short narrative or a share link | A grid, which finance-minded friends already know |
| Offline on the day | Only if you exported a copy | A file you saved |
| Who holds the file | The vendor account | You |

No row is a universal winner. The useful **AI itinerary planner vs spreadsheet** setup is a sequence: draft, check, then log the facts that must stay true.

## When the spreadsheet still wins

Open the sheet when money is the argument. A running total, an exchange rate, and a column for who already paid for the apartment will stay honest in cells. Paragraphs will not.

Open it when the trip is already a pile of inventory: flight segments, a rail pass, voucher codes, a museum ticket with a name on it. One row per booking. A source column (confirmed email, your note, AI draft) keeps estimates from sitting next to paid amounts as if they were the same kind of fact.

Open it when you repeat the trip shape. A family reunion or a festival weekend often has the same columns every year. The template is the value. Generating a fresh essay each time throws that away.

Open it when your connection is unreliable. A downloaded sheet is the plan you can still scroll on a bus. A draft that lives only behind a login is not.

Packing tied to activities belongs here too. A cold-morning row can point at a gear cell. A prose list can name the jacket, but it cannot give you a checkbox.

Skip the sheet as your only plan when you are still staring at an empty grid in a city you do not know. You will spend the evening building columns and still have no Thursday.

## When the AI itinerary planner wins

Use the planner when you need a readable first week and you hate the blank page. Ask for days, a base neighborhood, meal ideas, and one indoor swap. You get something you can argue with, which is the point of a first draft.

Use it when the group wants options in sentences. "Rain: museum. Clear: the long walk." People answer that in a chat. They do not answer a color scale in column H.

Use it when you are weighing pace. Two neighborhoods a day versus one base and a single outing is a writing problem, not a money one. The sheet can store the result after you decide.

The planner is the wrong place for a price you are about to pay. Put a number in the sheet only after you have seen it on the operator's own page.

## The limit you should not skip

Read that as a habit, not as a reason to avoid a first draft. The day shape can be useful and the venue name can still be invented. Before you send anyone across town, check the place on a map and on the venue's own site. Check walking time between pins instead of trusting the word "nearby". Check prices with the operator, especially if the date, the ticket class, or the season can move them.

Hours, closures, and transit lines go stale. If being wrong costs a booking, the sheet (or the confirmation email) is the record. The chat is the sketch.

## A hybrid that does not fight itself

1. Draft the days in the planner: anchors, neighborhoods, a rain swap, and the meals you care about.
2. Cut about a fifth of the stops after you look at a map. Distance and closing days are the usual reason.
3. Move costs, codes, and splits into the sheet. One row per booking. Keep estimates in a different column from amounts you have paid.
4. Send friends the narrative and keep the budget tab to yourself. A [shareable travel itinerary](/blog/shareable-travel-itinerary-for-friends) is the page they will open. Leave passport numbers, card details, and medical notes out of that link.
5. On the day, use the sheet for "what is booked", a map for "how we walk", and the short narrative for "what today is for".

A made-up example: four friends, four nights in Porto. One person holds a sheet with the apartment total, the airport train, and who has already transferred their share. Another holds an AI draft that puts Ribeira on the arrival afternoon, a Gaia walk the next morning, and a rain swap to a museum. Museum order stays in the draft. Apartment math stays in the sheet. Both links sit in the chat.

[CitySage](https://citysage.ch) is one way to get a visual AI itinerary you can share with friends or post to social. It does not replace the sheet when the open question is money or a code you must not lose. Export or retype the costs either way.

## What to keep off the public link

The sheet inherits the sharing settings of whatever drive it lives in. An AI planner stores the prompt on someone else's servers. Read that vendor's policy before you paste an itinerary, and do not paste passport numbers, card numbers, or passwords into a prompt at all.

A practical split: public day plans in a view-only link, money and identity in a sheet with a short list of people. If the trip is leisure and the account belongs to your employer, use a personal file.

Phone screens change the choice too. A narrative is easier to read on a train. A wide grid is easier on a laptop. If the group mixes both, open the actual link on the smallest phone before you call the format done.

## Common mixes that waste an evening

Using the planner as the accounts book means the totals drift every time someone regenerates the days. Using the sheet as the map means you will not notice two "quick" stops that are an hour apart. Two files with no owner means Monday's museum exists in the draft and not in the sheet, or the reverse.

Regenerating the whole plan after a map check throws away the cuts you already made. Edit the working draft. And if you share anything, split the public days from the finance tab before the link goes into the group chat.

## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Can an AI itinerary planner replace a spreadsheet?</h3>
  <p>Rarely, if the trip has shared costs or booking codes. The planner drafts the days. The sheet is where verified numbers and codes should live. A nice export still needs that cleanup.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is Google Sheets enough for trip planning?</h3>
  <p>Enough for logistics and money, once you are willing to type the research in. It will not propose a neighborhood order. Pair it with a map list or an AI draft for the flow of the days.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Which is better for a group trip?</h3>
  <p>Friends read a short narrative or a share link. The person tracking the apartment and the trains uses a sheet. Pin both. The group chat is a poor archive for either.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should I trust prices in an AI itinerary?</h3>
  <p>No. In the InsureMyTrip test, prices for major experiences came in low, and some recommended places did not exist as described. Copy a price into the sheet only after the operator's page shows it.</p>
</div>

## What to do for the next trip

List the next three questions you actually have. If two of them are amounts or codes, duplicate last year's sheet and fill rows. If two of them are "what are we doing on Thursday?", run one AI draft, then check it on a map before anyone books.
