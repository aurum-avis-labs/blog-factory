## Multi-city trip itinerary order

<p class="blog-lede">The best multi-city trip itinerary order is the one that matches your flights: arrive at one end of the route, move in one direction, and fly home from the other. Most bad trips come from too many one-night stops or a return leg that undoes a day of train time.</p>

This guide is about route logic, pacing, and luggage friction. It is not a booking tutorial. Check every train time, fare, and strike notice on official operator sites before you pay.

## How should cities order on a map?

Draw your stops on a map and read the line you would travel. A clean multi-city route looks like a gentle arc or a straight shot, not a star with you crossing the same corridor twice. If two cities sit on the same rail line, put them back to back. If one city is a dead end, it usually belongs at the start or end near the airport you use, not in the middle unless the flight savings are large.

Order cities **before** you fill hour-by-hour plans. Changing sequence after you booked non-refundable trains is expensive. A spreadsheet or an [AI itinerary planner vs spreadsheet](/blog/ai-itinerary-planner-vs-spreadsheet) pass helps compare two orders side by side, but the map still wins when geography is obvious.

## Start from flights and work backward

Unless you already hold an open-jaw ticket, shape the city list around **arrival and departure airports**:

1. List candidate cities and rough nights per stop
2. Price **open-jaw** or multi-city flights (often cheaper than round-trip to one hub plus backtracking)
3. Draw the route on a map and reject zig-zags unless the savings are large
4. Insert **transfer days** when any leg exceeds about three hours door to door

AI can propose city order in minutes. You still validate flight economics and whether a "cheap" hub forces a four-hour backtrack on the ground.

## Open-jaw tickets and when order saves money

An open-jaw flight lands in city A and leaves from city B. That pattern matches a one-way move along the route. Round-trip into the same hub tempts people to return to A just to catch the flight home, which burns a day.

Compare **total trip cost**, not only the flight screen:

| You compare | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Open-jaw into A, out of B | Often matches the map you actually want |
| Round-trip to A plus return train from B | Can look cheap until you add the backtrack leg |
| Extra city to use a flight deal | Sometimes worth it; sometimes one night you will not enjoy |

If a fare forces an awkward order, swap which city gets the long flight or drop a stop. A shorter trip in the right direction beats a longer trip that chases a fare glitch.

## Minimum nights: a practical rule

| Stop type | Suggested minimum |
| --- | --- |
| Major capital or multi-neighborhood city | 3 nights |
| Smaller city or day-trip base | 2 nights |
| Pass-through you might skip | 0 to 1 night (usually skip) |

**Why:** First and last nights in a city are often **partial** (travel, check-in, packing). Two nights sometimes means **one full day**. Three nights usually gives **two full days** plus arrival slack.

Inside a single city, depth is a different question. For a one-city week, see pacing in [one-week city itinerary planning](/blog/one-week-city-itinerary-how-many-neighborhoods) and keep multi-city order separate so you do not mix "how many neighborhoods" with "how many countries."

## How many city changes per week is too many?

A useful ceiling for many travelers is **one city change every three to four nights**. More than that and you live in checkout lines. A group trip needs even fewer moves: every change is a group decision about bags, meeting points, and who missed the train announcement. Label transfer days on the itinerary. If half the group thinks it is a sightseeing day and half thinks it is travel, you get fights at the station.

## First stop or last stop for the long flight?

Jet lag hits hardest in the first days on the ground. Put the lighter city first if you need slow mornings, or the anchor sights first if the group arrives rested. Write the rule on the itinerary header and match hotel check-in times.

## Luggage friction: the silent itinerary killer

Every city change costs:

- Packing and checkout time
- Storage locker hunts if check-in is late
- Staircases in older stations
- Risk of left items on trains

Reduce friction:

- **Pack for carry-on** if you will change cities more than twice
- **Ship a box home** on long trips instead of dragging souvenirs city to city
- **Stay near main stations** on transfer days only. Sacrifice charm for logistics once.
- **Book morning departures** when possible so delays do not erase your afternoon

Put **transfer days** in the itinerary as their own label, not disguised as sightseeing days.

## Sample two-city EU pattern (generic)

This pattern is illustrative, not a live timetable. Substitute cities that fit your flights.

**Trip length:** 8 nights  
**Cities:** City A (north) → City B (south)  
**Flight:** Open-jaw into A, out of B  

| Nights | Base | Focus |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 to 4 | City A | Museums, core districts, one day trip optional |
| 4 | Travel morning | Train A to B; check-in afternoon |
| 5 to 8 | City B | Food neighborhood, waterfront or old town, slow last day |

**Order logic:** Fly into A, train south to B, fly home from B. No return to A.  
**Minimum nights:** Four in A, four in B (each includes partial travel days).  
**Verify:** Rail operator sites for seat reservations and strike notices.

Adjust nights if City B is smaller. Steal a night from B only if A has more anchors you care about.

## When to add a third city

Add a third stop only if:

- Transfer times stay **under three hours** reliably
- You have **at least two nights** per stop
- Someone in the group accepts lighter pacing

Otherwise you are collecting passport stamps, not experiences. Keep a third city when it sits on the line between A and B and you have a fixed reason to stop there, such as family, a festival, or one museum. Cut it when it forces a triangle backtrack or leaves everyone with one rushed afternoon.

## When to cut a city from the list

Cut a city when the map says so, not when FOMO says so. Drop a stop if adding it forces a one-night stay, a midnight transfer, or a return to an airport you already left. A [group trip itinerary without endless group chat](/blog/group-trip-itinerary-without-endless-group-chat) works better with two bases and honest "free afternoon" blocks than with four cities on a seven-night trip.

## AI prompts for multi-city order

Include:

- **Fixed flight cities and dates**
- **Maximum transfers** you tolerate per week
- **Minimum nights per stop**
- **Luggage note:** "We have two large bags. Minimize station changes."

Ask for **two order options** with pros and cons (cost, time, backtracking). Then run the map pass yourself. If you use AI to draft days, follow the verify steps in [how to plan a trip itinerary with AI](/blog/how-to-plan-a-trip-itinerary-with-ai).

## Common ordering mistakes

- **Circular routes** to reuse a cheap flight hub
- **One-night stops** to "fit everything"
- **Scheduling heavy sights** on transfer days
- **Ignoring check-out and check-in times** (11 a.m. out, 3 p.m. in leaves a dead zone)
- **Assuming the last train** exists after a late concert in another city

## Frequently asked questions

### Is it better to train or fly between cities?

Trains win downtown to downtown under about four hours in many European corridors. Flying can win on longer legs if you include airport time honestly.

### Should I book hotels before or after fixing city order?

Fix order first, then book flexible stays on anchor nights. Non-refundable trains in the wrong direction cost more than a hotel swap.

### Should I store luggage at the station?

Lockers help on long transfer days. Verify size limits and hours. Some hotels store bags if you booked both ends through them.

### How do I plan multi-city with kids?

Fewer bases, longer minimum nights, and afternoon quiet time beat aggressive city hopping.

### Can AI book trains for me?

Most AI tools only suggest routes. You purchase on official sites or apps.

### What if prices jump when I reorder cities?

Sometimes a slightly worse geographic order saves a lot on flights. Compare total trip cost, not just rail time.

## Your next step

List your cities on a map, assign **minimum nights**, and count **door-to-door transfer hours** between each pair. If any stop has fewer than two full days or any transfer exceeds half a day, cut a city or extend the trip. That pass sets a multi-city trip itinerary order you can actually enjoy.

When the sequence is stable, sketch days in one shareable plan so partners see the same transfer labels.
