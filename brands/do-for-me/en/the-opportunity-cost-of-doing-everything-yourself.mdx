The opportunity cost of doing everything yourself is the hour you never got back: the lecture you revised badly, the Saturday with your kid that became an errand run, the meal you meant to cook and replaced with whatever was still open. If you spend too much time on chores, you are probably counting the chores and missing what they replaced.

This is for people in Zürich whose days are already full. The tasks are ordinary. The cost shows up somewhere else.


## Opportunity cost is the thing you did not do

<Image src={img2} alt="Checklist of daily chores" width={500} quality={80} class="w-full" />

Each item on its own feels cheap:

- a quick errand,
- clearing up after the day,
- carrying a few boxes,
- walking the dog,
- sitting with homework for twenty minutes that become an hour.

Add them and the week has no spare edge. People reach Friday wondering why the errands they actually wanted still sit on the list, and why the evening is already spoken for. That is the cost. Money may be involved later. The first loss is the thing the hour was going to be.

## Adding the hours is the easy part

<Image src={img3} alt="Person missing out on family time" width={500} quality={80} class="w-full" />

Time spent on repeat chores is time not spent on focused work, study, rest, or people you live with. That sentence is obvious. It still fails as a decision tool, because the chores look too small to trade.

Try one week in two columns, on paper or in your notes. Left: the errand. Right: what you dropped to do it. A parcel pickup that "took fifteen minutes" may have cost the start of a focused block you never restarted. A Saturday run to buy one shelf can eat the lunch you promised someone. You do not need a rate in francs for this. You need to see the swap.

The imbalance feels like being busy without getting anywhere that matters. That is the usual shape, not a personal failure.


## The fix people try first, and why it stalls

<Image src={img4} alt="Person hesitating to ask for help" width={500} quality={80} class="w-full" />

Three reactions show up again and again:

- The task feels too small to hand off.
- Hiring help sounds like a company, a contract, or a bill that does not match a one-hour job.
- Asking a friend feels like a favour you will owe back on a weekend you also do not have.

So people keep every task, including ones they already know they need help with. The usual productivity fix, "block an hour for admin", assumes an hour exists and the places are on one route. In Zürich the route is often two trams and a shop that shut while you were still at the first stop.

A local student who can do one described job is a different shape from a service contract. You are trading a clearly bounded task for the block it was going to consume. You are not outsourcing your life.


## Who feels this in Zürich

The pattern is common in a few situations.

A student with a part-time job and a seminar on the other side of the city. The flat still needs a food shop and a bag taken to the station. Neither job is hard. Both land in the same ninety minutes.

A parent whose afternoon is already a pickup. Any extra stop turns dinner into a late scramble. The "quick" errand is the one that slides for a week.

Someone new to the city, without parents around the corner. There is no default second pair of hands for a move, a heavy delivery, or a dog that cannot wait until 20:00.

In each case the expensive part is the lost block, not the difficulty of the task.


## When doing it yourself is still the right call

Keep the task if:

- you are the only one who can judge it (a decision, a private message, money that is not yours to hand over),
- you like doing it and it actually rests you,
- it needs a licensed trade,
- or explaining it would take longer than doing it.

Hand it off if it is simple, repeats, does not need your expertise, and keeps blocking something you would rather protect. At that point doing it yourself can cost more than getting help, even before you talk about money.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Write the task in one sentence.</li>
  <li>Write what you will do with the hour if you do not spend it here.</li>
  <li>If the second line matters more, and a stranger could follow the first line, the task is a candidate.</li>
</ul>

Modern ways to get local help exist for that case: short, practical, no long contract. How the options differ is covered in [what your options are when you need help with small tasks](/en/blog/what-are-your-options-when-you-need-help-with-small-tasks).

<div class="blog-faq">
  <p class="blog-faq-q">Is opportunity cost only about wages?</p>
  <p>No. For most households it is the evening, the study block, or the family time that moved aside. A wage comparison is optional. The swapped hour is not.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <p class="blog-faq-q">Does asking for help mean I am behind?</p>
  <p>It means the day contains more tasks than one person can place without dropping something else. That is a common week in a city, not a character flaw.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
