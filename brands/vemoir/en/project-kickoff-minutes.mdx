**Project kickoff meeting minutes** are the page everyone will quote three months later, when someone asks whether the mobile app was ever part of the deal. Good kickoff minutes record a goal with a date, what is in scope and what is out, the milestones, roles with real names, risks with a measure and an owner, how the team will work together, and the first actions with an owner and a due date.

Write them so a person who missed the kickoff can run the project from them. The free [project kickoff minutes template](/tools/minutes-sheet/project-kickoff) has these sections ready, with a filled example you can overwrite. Below is what goes into each part, who should write it, and a worked example from a CRM rollout.

## What project kickoff meeting minutes are for

Most meeting minutes are read once and filed. Kickoff minutes are different: they are the first written agreement between the sponsor, the project lead and the people who will do the work. Later disputes about scope, dates and responsibility usually go back to this meeting, and the minutes are the only version that does not depend on memory.

That changes how you write them. A regular team sync can live with "discussed the timeline." A kickoff cannot. Every line should still make sense to a reader in six months who was not in the room and does not know the context.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Goal in one or two sentences, with a date</li>
  <li>In scope and out of scope, as two separate lists</li>
  <li>Milestones with dates</li>
  <li>One name per role: who decides, who delivers, who is informed</li>
  <li>Risks with a measure and an owner</li>
  <li>Working agreements: steering cadence and where documents live</li>
  <li>First actions with owner and due date</li>
</ul>

## Goal, scope and milestones

**The goal** needs a date and something you can check. "Introduce a CRM" is a direction. "By March 31, 2027, Sales and Customer Service work in one shared CRM, and customer data from Excel and Outlook has been migrated" is a goal: on April 1, anyone can say whether it was met.

**In scope** lists what the project delivers. Keep it to what people will actually ask about later: the system, the data migration, the training and for how many people.

**Out of scope** is the section that saves the most arguments. Write down what comes later (phase 2) and what does not come at all. If the sponsor said "no mobile app this year," that sentence belongs in the minutes, not in someone's head. When a request arrives in January, the project lead can point to a line everyone saw on the day of the kickoff.

**Milestones** are the three to five dates the steering committee will check: vendor selected, test system ready, go-live. Each one needs a date, not a month. A milestone without a date turns into a discussion at every status meeting.

## Roles with names, not departments

"IT will handle access" is how actions get lost. Write one name per role. A useful split for a kickoff has three questions:

- **Who decides?** Usually the sponsor, who owns the budget and the goal. Write the person, for example Michael Grant, Head of Sales.
- **Who delivers?** The project lead and the people who do the work: key users, IT, an external vendor contact.
- **Who needs to be informed?** People who are affected but do not decide or deliver, such as line managers whose staff will be in training.

If a department really does own something, still name the person who answers for it. When that person changes, update the role list in the project documents and say so in the next minutes.

## Risks, working agreements and first actions

**Risks** need three columns: the risk, the measure and the owner. "Data quality might be poor" is a worry. "Inconsistent Excel lists; clean up before migration, two days planned; owner: Head of Sales" is a risk someone is handling. Two to four real risks are better than a list of ten that nobody reads again.

**Working agreements** are the decisions about how the project runs. Two are almost always needed:

- **Steering cadence:** how often the steering committee meets, and the date of the first meeting.
- **Document location:** one folder where all project documents live, named exactly as it appears, for example the SharePoint folder "CRM 2027".

Add others only if they were actually agreed: who approves change requests, which channel is used for quick questions.

**First actions** turn the kickoff into work. Each action gets one owner and a due date within the next two or three weeks. If you want a deeper look at writing actions people actually finish, see [action items from meetings](/blog/action-items-from-meetings). The rule is the same here: a verb, one person, one date.

## Who takes the minutes and when they go out

The project lead should not take the minutes. They are running the meeting, steering the discussion and answering questions; writing at the same time means either the meeting or the minutes suffers. Ask a team member, often a key user or a project assistant, and agree on it before the kickoff starts.

Send the minutes the same day. The next morning, people already remember the discussion differently, and the first actions may be due within a week. A short note with the minutes helps: "Please send corrections by Friday. After that, this version is the reference." Then file the version in the document location the kickoff just agreed on.

If you record the kickoff to check details later, tell everyone at the start and respect a no. The person taking the minutes still reads the draft, corrects names and dates, and decides what goes out.

## A worked example from a CRM rollout

This is the filled example from the [kickoff template](/tools/minutes-sheet/project-kickoff), with each role marked as deciding or delivering. The project is a CRM rollout at Brightline Supply, a fictional company.

```text
Project kickoff minutes
Project: CRM rollout at Brightline Supply
Date: 2026-10-14, 1:30-4:00 PM
Project manager: Priya Shah | Minutes: Lucas Moreau

Attendees and roles
- Michael Grant: Sponsor (Head of Sales), decides
- Priya Shah: Project Manager, delivers
- Lucas Moreau: Key user, Customer Service, delivers
- Sofia Alvarez: IT, delivers

Project goal
By Mar 31, 2027, Sales and Customer Service work in one shared CRM.
Customer data from Excel and Outlook has been migrated.

In scope
- Selecting and setting up the CRM
- Migrating data from Excel and Outlook
- Training 25 employees

Out of scope
- Integration with accounting (phase 2)
- Mobile app

Milestones
- Vendor selected: Nov 15, 2026
- Test system set up: Jan 15, 2027
- Go-live: Mar 1, 2027

Risks (risk / mitigation / owner)
- Inconsistent Excel lists / clean up before migration, 2 days planned / Head of Sales
- Key users barely available in December / move training to January / Project Manager

Decisions
- The steering committee meets monthly. First meeting on Nov 4.
- All project documents live in the SharePoint folder "CRM 2027".

Next steps (what / owner / due)
- Send requirements list to three vendors / Priya Shah / Oct 24
- Merge Excel customer lists / Lucas Moreau / Nov 7
- Sort out access to the test system / Sofia Alvarez / Oct 31
```

Notice three things. The minutes taker is Lucas, not Priya, who chaired. The out-of-scope list is as concrete as the in-scope list. And the first steering meeting on November 4 already has its own template: the [steering committee minutes](/tools/minutes-sheet/steering-committee) pick up where this page stops.

## What to leave out

Kickoff minutes get long when they try to record the whole conversation. Leave out:

- **The discussion that led to each decision.** Record the result. If an alternative was rejected for a reason people will ask about again, one sentence is enough.
- **Introductions and small talk.** The attendee table already says who was there and in which role.
- **The slide deck.** Name it with its title and date and store it in the project folder. Do not copy slides into the minutes.
- **Opinions attributed to people.** "Sofia was worried about access" helps nobody. "Access to the test system: Sofia Alvarez, Oct 31" does.
- **Open questions without an owner.** Either give each one a name and a date, or leave it for the next meeting's agenda.

A good test: the minutes fit on one or two pages, and each line is either a fact, a decision or an action.

## Frequently asked questions

### Who approves the kickoff minutes?

The project lead checks the draft before it goes out, and the sponsor confirms it. Many teams send the minutes with a deadline for corrections and then treat that version as the reference.

### What if someone disagrees with a point after the minutes are sent?

Ask for the correction in writing, check it against your notes or the recording, and send a corrected version that says what changed. Do not edit the filed version silently.

### Do kickoff minutes need to be signed?

Usually not for an internal project. A written confirmation from the sponsor by email is often enough, unless a contract or your project handbook asks for more.

### How long should project kickoff minutes be?

One to two pages for most projects. If they run longer, the minutes probably contain discussion that belongs in other documents.

### Are kickoff minutes the same as a project charter?

No. A charter or project mandate is the document that authorizes the project. The minutes record what the kickoff agreed and can point to the charter by title and date.

### Should we record the kickoff meeting?

Only if everyone knows and agrees at the start. A recording helps the minutes taker check names and dates, but the written minutes remain the reference.
