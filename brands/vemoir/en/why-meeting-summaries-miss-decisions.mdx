Meeting summaries miss decisions because a summary is built to sound finished. A decision is often a short sentence in the middle of a hedge: "we can, if legal signs, and not before the 12th." The model, or a tired note-taker, writes "The team agreed to proceed." The condition is gone. The date is gone. The person who must tell legal is gone. The paragraph reads well, and it is the wrong record.

This is not a claim that summaries are useless. A recap helps someone who missed the topic list. It is a poor container for commitments. The container for commitments is a decision line and an action line. The blank place to write the outcome is the [minutes sheet](/tools/minutes-sheet). ## What a summary is optimized to do

A summary compresses. It prefers the center of the discussion over the one sentence where the room closed, because the center is longer. It prefers confident prose over "maybe" and "if," because confident prose looks like a recap. It groups speakers into "the team" because names make sentences clumsy.

Those habits help a newsletter. They hurt a protocol. A board, a client, or next week's you needs the decision, the condition, and the action with one owner and a date. A transcript keeps every hedge and never labels the decision. You can search it and still miss the point if you do not mark the line. The summary and the transcript fail in different directions. Neither one is the minutes.

## The shapes of decisions that disappear

Listen for these, and expect a summary to sand them down.

**The conditional yes.** "Yes, once finance confirms the cap." "Approved" without the condition is a different decision.

**The narrow yes.** "We do the April date for the Swiss client only." "We launch in April" drops scope.

**The explicit no or deferral.** "We are not deciding today" is a decision about timing. A summary often picks a side from the longer argument.

**The owner who refused.** "I can't take it, ask Priya" is not "the team will handle it" if Priya did not accept.

A joke, a rejected draft, and a loud "exactly" mid-sentence can also become "decisions" when the model rewards a decisive tone.

## Why meeting summaries miss decisions in practice

Three ordinary workflows produce the miss.

You paste a VTT into a chat box and ask for "key decisions." The model supplies an owner if the prose feels unfinished. It drops "not" in a double negative. It merges two speakers who disagreed into "the group leaned toward."

The meeting app's recap is written for the host's inbox. "Budget" is a theme. "Cap the pilot at CHF 8,000 until the May review" is a decision. If the recap gives you themes, you still write the lines.

A shared notes doc drifts. Three people edit the same paragraph. The decision becomes the last editor's version. A structured line with a date is harder to silently rewrite. On a Mac, a generated decision can stay tied to a transcript passage so you can open the words before you share. The browser [minutes sheet](/tools/minutes-sheet) gives you fields: decisions, actions with owner and due date, open questions. You type what you are willing to stand behind.

## A decision line that can be checked

Use a line, not a paragraph.

```text
Decision: Pilot stays capped until the May review.
Responsible: Nora
Date decided: 18 October 2026
Condition: Finance confirms the cap in writing.
Action: Nora asks finance by 21 October.
```

If there is no condition, write "none" so a later reader knows you looked. Do not invent Nora.

On the Mac, decisions stay separate from actions and from your private notes typed during the call. That separation is what a single summary paragraph cannot do. Rejected options belong in the decision wording or in one extra line, "Not doing: a second vendor this quarter."

## How to review a summary before you send it

1. List every decision you remember in the line format above, before you read the summary.
2. Search the transcript for "if", "unless", "not", "only", and the names in the room.
3. For every owner in the summary, find the acceptance. No acceptance, no owner.
4. Move the checked lines to the minutes. Send the summary only if it matches those lines.
5. Keep the transcript for the dispute. Do not edit it so it matches the mail.

[Meeting notes from a transcript](/tools/meeting-notes-from-transcript) can draft decisions from a paste. Read the draft with the rules above. Neither a browser draft nor a model recap may invent a speaker the file did not have.

## The same call, paragraph and line

A twelve-minute call is enough as an example. Someone says the revision goes out Thursday if clause four stays unchanged. You say you will ask legal on Tuesday. The room says "agreed," then the next topic starts. Minutes earlier, another clause was rejected.

The model paragraph often reads: the team agreed on a Thursday revision. The condition is gone. Tuesday is gone. The rejected clause is back, or gone. Who reads the mail acts on Thursday without clause four.

The minutes line reads differently. Decision: revision Thursday, only if clause four stays. Action: you ask legal Tuesday. The final "agreed" does not get its own line because it adds no new content.

Put both versions side by side and you see where the summary worked. Cut from the paragraph what the line does not carry, or replace the paragraph with the lines. Keep the transcript if someone later claims the "if" was never said. The recording stays on the Mac if you captured there. The mail carries the lines, not the audio file.

For memory, the round paragraph is fine. In the minutes, Thursday, the clause, and the person must stand when someone asks again. If one of the three was missing in the call, write the gap. Do not fill it so the paragraph sounds more decided. When line and paragraph disagree, the line wins.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are meeting summaries the same as minutes?

No. A summary compresses themes or tone. Minutes are the record: decisions, actions, owners, dates, open questions.

### Why do AI summaries sound more decisive than the call?

They are prompted to produce a clear recap. Clarity hides conditions until you find them in the transcript.

### Should I stop using summaries?

No. Use them to remember what was discussed. Put decisions in their own lines and check those lines.

### What if the room never made a decision?

Write that. "No decision. Revisit on 2 November" is a useful record.

### Can a transcript replace the decision log?

It can prove a line. It does not mark the line. You still extract the decision.

### How many decisions does a normal meeting have?

Often one or two, plus a handful of actions. A summary that lists twelve "decisions" has usually promoted topics and comments.

### Do I need speaker names to record a decision?

You need them when the decision is "Alex committed." Do not invent Alex from a nameless file.

### Where should the checked lines live?

On the minutes you share with people who must act, and in the tracker you already use, with the same owner and date.
