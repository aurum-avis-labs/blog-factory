Meeting summaries miss decisions because a summary is built to sound finished. A decision is often a short, ugly sentence in the middle of a hedge: "we can, if legal signs, and not before the 12th." The model, or a tired note-taker, writes "The team agreed to proceed." The condition is gone. The date is gone. The person who must tell legal is gone. The paragraph reads well, and it is the wrong record.

This is not a claim that summaries are useless. A recap helps someone who missed the topic list. It is a bad container for commitments. The container for commitments is a decision line and an action line: the words of the call on one side, the outcome on the other. The blank place to write the outcome is the [minutes sheet](/tools/minutes-sheet). A copyable layout is the [meeting minutes template](/blog/meeting-minutes-template).

## What a summary is optimized to do

A summary compresses. It prefers the center of the discussion over the one sentence where the room closed, because the center is longer. It prefers confident prose over "maybe" and "if," because confident prose looks like a recap. It groups speakers into "the team" because names make sentences clumsy.


Those habits help a newsletter. They hurt a protocol. A board, a client, or next week's you needs three things a summary treats as clutter:

- The decision, including what was refused.
- The condition, if one was stated.
- The action that makes the decision real, with one owner and a date.

A transcript has the opposite problem. It keeps every hedge and never labels the decision. You can search it, and you can still miss the point if you do not mark the line. The summary and the transcript fail in different directions. Neither one is the minutes.

## The shapes of decisions that disappear

Listen for these, and expect a summary to sand them down.


**The conditional yes.** "Yes, once finance confirms the cap." A summary that says "Approved" has changed the decision. The honest line is the yes plus the condition, and an action for the person who will ask finance.

**The narrow yes.** "We do the April date for the Swiss client only." "We launch in April" is a different decision. Scope is part of the wording.

**The explicit no.** People remember the option that was discussed and forget the rejection. "We will not bring a bot into the client call" is a decision. A summary of "tools were discussed" drops it.

**The deferred decision.** "We are not deciding today" is a decision about timing. A summary often writes "alignment pending" or, worse, picks a side from the longer argument.

**The corrected number.** The first figure was wrong. The second figure is the decision. A summary sometimes averages them, or keeps the first because it appeared in the "key moment" near the start.

**The owner who refused.** "I can't take it, ask Priya" is not "the team will handle it." If Priya did not accept, you have an open question, not an action.

None of this requires a perfect transcript. It requires that you do not let a paragraph be the only place the decision lives.

## Why meeting summaries miss decisions in practice

Four ordinary workflows produce the miss.

You paste a VTT into a chat box and ask for "key decisions." The model has seen a million recaps that end with momentum. It will supply an owner if the prose feels unfinished. It will drop "not" in a double negative. It will merge two speakers who disagreed into "the group leaned toward." You did not ask for fiction. Compression plus a bias toward closure produces it anyway.

A person writes the summary from memory the next morning. They remember the topic they cared about and the decision they wanted. The quiet reversal at minute 40 is gone. The transcript, if you kept one, is how you catch that. The summary from memory has no passage to point at.

The meeting app's own recap is written for the host's inbox. It is often a list of themes. Themes are not decisions. "Budget" is a theme. "Cap the pilot at CHF 8,000 until the May review" is a decision. If the recap gives you themes, you still have to write the lines.

A shared notes doc drifts. Three people edit the same paragraph. The decision becomes the last editor's version. A structured line with a date is harder to silently rewrite, and on a Mac the generated decision can stay tied to a transcript passage so you can open the words before you share. That check is the point of evidence, not a decoration. The browser [minutes sheet](/tools/minutes-sheet) will not do the check for you. It gives you fields: decisions, actions with owner and due date, open questions. You type what you are willing to stand behind.

## A decision line that can be checked

Use a line, not a paragraph.

```text
Decision: Pilot stays capped until the May review.
Responsible: Nora
Date decided: 18 October 2026
Condition: Finance confirms the cap in writing.
Action: Nora asks finance by 21 October.
```

If there is no condition, write "none" so a later reader knows you looked. If there is no owner for the action, the decision may still be real and the action may be an open question. Do not invent Nora.

On the Mac, decisions are stored as wording, a responsible person, and a date, separate from actions. You can delete one without deleting the transcript. You can jump from the decision to the passage. Your own notes typed during the call stay marked as yours, so a thought you had is not presented as something the room said. That separation is what a single summary paragraph cannot do. It is also more than a browser form. The form is enough when the meeting was one export and you will file Markdown.

Rejected options belong in the decision wording or in one extra line, "Not doing: a second vendor this quarter." Otherwise the next meeting restarts the argument as if it were new.

## How to review a summary before you send it

1. List every decision you remember in the line format above, before you read the summary. Then read the summary and see what it added or dropped.
2. Search the transcript, if you have one, for "if", "unless", "not", "only", and the names of the people in the room. Each hit is a candidate the summary may have smoothed.
3. For every owner in the summary, find the acceptance. No acceptance, no owner.
4. For every date, write the calendar day. "Next Thursday" rots.
5. Move the checked lines to the minutes. Leave the summary in the mail only if it matches those lines. If it does not, send the lines.
6. Keep the transcript for the dispute. Do not edit it so it matches the mail.

A model draft can be the first pass of step 1 only when you already have the text in front of you. [Meeting notes from a transcript](/tools/meeting-notes-from-transcript) will draft decisions and actions from a paste. Read them with the rules above. Cleaning a VTT first does not spend a run. The notes draft does. Neither one is allowed to invent a speaker the file did not have.

If you do not have text, do not ask for a decision list from a vibe. A 15-minute file can be transcribed in the browser without upload. Longer calls and live capture stay on the Mac. None of that makes the recording lawful. You still told people, or you did not.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are meeting summaries the same as minutes?

No. A summary is a compression of themes or of tone. Minutes are the record: decisions, actions, owners, dates, open questions. A good summary can be rewritten into minutes. A pretty summary is not already minutes.

### Why do AI summaries sound more decisive than the call?

They are trained and prompted to produce a clear recap. Clarity hides conditions. The decisive sentence is a hypothesis until you find it in the transcript.

### Should I stop using summaries?

No. Use them to remember what was discussed. Do not use them as the only copy of what was decided. Put the decisions in their own lines and check those lines.

### What if the room never made a decision?

Write that. "No decision. Revisit on 2 November" is a useful record. A summary that picks a winner because the agenda had a recommendation is not.

### Can a transcript replace the decision log?

It can prove a line. It does not mark the line. You still extract the decision, or the next reader has to replay the meeting.

### How many decisions does a normal meeting have?

Often one or two, plus a handful of actions. A summary that lists twelve "decisions" has usually promoted topics and comments. Cut until each line would change someone's work.

### Do I need speaker names to record a decision?

You need them when the decision is "Alex committed." You do not need a full diarization to write "the room deferred the vendor choice" if you were there and the transcript shows the deferral. Do not invent Alex from a nameless file.

### Where should the checked lines live?

On the minutes you share with the people who must act, and in whatever tracker you already use, with the same owner and date. The summary can sit on top only if it does not contradict the lines.
