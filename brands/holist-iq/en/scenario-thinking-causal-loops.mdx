## Scenario thinking with causal loops

<p class="blog-lede">Scenario thinking with causal loops stress-tests one intervention on one map. You keep the same variables and the same loops. You change only the assumptions on the link you are about to pull, then walk both stories before you commit budget or political capital.</p>

This is for teams who already have a causal loop diagram and a decision date. It is not a workshop for inventing four unrelated futures. If the map does not exist yet, build that first. A scenario on a blank wall is just two opinions.

## How this differs from classic scenario planning

Classic scenario planning often builds a few worlds that differ in many drivers at once: regulation, demand, technology, politics. That work is useful when the question is what the environment might do to you. It is a poor fit when the question is what happens if you change this one rule.

Loop scenarios keep the diagram fixed. The two stories differ in polarity, delay, or strength on the intervention link, and in how the loops that share that link respond. People can point at the same arrow and disagree. That disagreement is the scenario.

If you draw a fresh diagram for the bad world, you lose the comparison. Executives cannot tell which assumption produced which outcome.

<dl class="blog-terms">
  <div>
    <dt>Polarity</dt>
    <dd>Whether the link helps the goal or feeds the loop you meant to weaken.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Delay</dt>
    <dd>How many turns of the clock pass before the effect shows up.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Falsifier</dt>
    <dd>Early evidence that ends one of the two stories before the deadline.</dd>
  </div>
</dl>

## Pick one intervention link

Choose a lever the organization can actually pull within a quarter. Good candidates are a delay you can shorten, a rule you can change, or an information flow you can open. Poor candidates are culture, trust, or the market, which are outcomes of several links, not a single pull.

Write the link as a sentence: if this variable moves, that variable moves, in this direction, on this clock. If you cannot write that sentence, you do not have an intervention yet. You have a wish.

Stay on one link even if the room wants a program of twelve actions. Extra actions belong in a later round, after this one has a pass or fail signal.

## Define two assumption sets on the same arrow

Use two sets on the same link: one where the intended effect arrives as hoped, one where a known side path dominates. Typical knobs:

- **Polarity.** The link helps the goal, or it feeds the loop you were trying to weaken.
- **Delay.** The effect shows up in weeks, or only after a hiring, procurement, or trust lag.
- **Strength.** The link moves the next variable a little, or it saturates because a stock or a capacity limit is already binding.

Do not draw a second diagram. Annotate the same arrows. Dotted lines are enough for the pessimistic polarity. A second diagram invites people to smuggle in new variables so their story wins.

  <li data-step="1"><h3>Name the link</h3><p>One sentence, one direction, one clock.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Write the optimistic walk</h3><p>Intended effect arrives, and the balancing loop you rely on still holds.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Write the pessimistic walk</h3><p>Same link, with the side path or the longer delay that the room already suspects.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Name the falsifier</h3><p>Evidence that would kill the optimistic path before the deadline, and who reads it.</p></li>
</ol>

## Trace metrics on two clocks

Every serious map has a fast variable and a slow one. Cycle time, ticket volume, and complaint counts often move in weeks. Trust, skill, installed base, and political support move in quarters.

Scenarios fail when everyone watches only the slow metric. The optimistic story looks true until the quarter closes, because the reinforcing damage was already in the fast variable. Write both clocks on the slide. Assign a person to each.

Ask, out loud: if the pessimistic path is real, what number moves first, and how many weeks before the headline KPI notices?

## Decide signals that falsify each scenario

A scenario without a kill-switch is a vibe. For the optimistic path, write the evidence that would prove it wrong before the decision deadline. For the pessimistic path, write the evidence that would show the side path did not fire.

Keep the signal leading, cheap, and owned. A six-month lagging financial result cannot falsify a choice you will not reverse in six months. Prefer a count you already collect: defect escapes, wait time, overtime hours, appeal volume, supplier expedites.

Agree the threshold in the room. Worse is not a threshold. Defect escapes on the first release above the last four-week median, checked in week three, is a threshold.

## A made-up example: dropping an approval step

Use this only as a structure to copy. It is not a report of a client result.

A team wants to drop an approval step to shorten cycle time. The intervention is one link: time spent waiting for approval.

Optimistic walk: the delay shortens, throughput rises, and the balancing loop that catches defects before release still holds. The fast metric (cycle time) and the slower one (customer-visible defects) both improve, with defects checked before the quarterly chart is printed.

Pessimistic walk: the same delay shortens, more defects escape, rework climbs, and a reinforcing loop of overtime and further defects takes over within weeks. The quarterly throughput chart still looks acceptable because rework is booked later, on a different team.

Falsifier for the optimistic path: defect rate on the first release after the change, read in week three by the quality owner, against the recent median. If it fires, you restore the step or add a different check before the quarter review congratulates the wrong metric.

The approval step itself is interchangeable with a reporting rule, a price change, or a hiring freeze. What matters is the pair of clocks and the early signal.

## When not to scenario-test

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>Skip the exercise when the decision is reversible and cheap, or when no leading indicator exists. A walk with no falsifier is theatre.</p>
</aside>

Also skip when you have no agreed map. Building the diagram and stress-testing it in the same hour produces a story that matches the loudest person. And skip when the two scenarios are really two different goals. That fight belongs in the leverage conversation, not in assumption labels.

## Tabletop one lever in twenty minutes

On a whiteboard copy of the map, tell the optimistic story once, finger on the arrows, including the delay marks. Then tell the pessimistic story on the same arrows. Write the falsifier and the owner in the corner. Stop. If the room wants a third world, ask which assumption on the same link it changes. If the answer is everything, you have left the method.

## What to do with the result

If both stories survive and the falsifier is cheap, run the intervention with the signal in place. If the pessimistic walk is obvious and the signal would arrive too late, do not launch. Change the link you test, or move to a deeper lever. ## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is scenario thinking with causal loops a full simulation?</h3>
  <p>No. A qualitative walk on one link catches many surprises that show up in steering meetings. If the decision needs levels, rates, and units over time, move the quantified slice to a stock-and-flow model and keep the variable names aligned.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How many scenarios should we write?</h3>
  <p>Two on the same link. Add a third only when it changes one assumption you can name, such as delay length. Four themed worlds belong to a different method.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>What if the room disagrees on polarity?</h3>
  <p>Keep both as dotted and solid versions of the same arrow. The falsifier is how you stop arguing. Do not average the two stories into a mushy third.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Do I need a finished causal loop diagram first?</h3>
  <p>Yes. Without shared variable names and loops, the stress test is only discussion. The map can be rough. Everyone in the room still has to recognise it as the same map.</p>
</div>
