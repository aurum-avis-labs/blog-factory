## What **operational firefighting feedback loops** explain

Ops teams clear the queue, get thanked, and find a similar queue the next morning. Urgent work is visible, so it gets recognition and staff time. Prevention is quiet, so it loses the same hour. The result is a team that stays busy and a recurrence that stays funded by accident.

This page is for operations leads who already run incidents, tickets, or production escapes, and who have tried "better prioritization" without a change in what repeats. If you searched **why ops teams stay busy**, the answer is rarely missing discipline. It is usually a loop that makes saves visible and does not count incidents that never happened. The same-problem pattern in other functions is in [why the same business problems keep coming back](/blog/why-the-same-business-problems-keep-coming-back).

## Firefighting is not the same as incident management

Incident management answers: what broke, who fixes it, when is service green again? **Operational firefighting** is the mode where the same incident class returns week after week and every clear looks like the job. A good IM tool makes the clear faster. It does not change anything when the class is structural and prevention keeps getting displaced.

If your review only tracks time-to-clear and open tickets, you are optimising the visible side of the loop. Add: which classes were closed before, who got credit for what, and how many hours went to a link that stops the class next quarter.

## The reinforcing loop that rewards the save

Read it as a story, not as a slogan. An incident is visible. The person who clears it is rewarded, formally or by reputation. Time for prevention is what gets cut, because the next page, ticket, or customer is already waiting. With less prevention, similar incidents become more likely. Those incidents are visible again, and the reward shifts further toward the save.

Nothing in that loop requires a careless team. It requires a clock and a reputation system that can see the rescue and cannot see the incident that did not happen. Blame aimed at "hero culture" usually lands on the people the loop is already paying to be heroes. The loop is the incentive path: visibility, reward, less prevention time, more recurrence, more visibility.

Name it on a sketch if you have one. R1: firefighting spiral. The variables that belong on it are specific enough to argue about: urgent hours, recognition for urgent clears, hours left for prevention, and the rate of repeat incident classes. "Busyness" is not a variable. It is the feeling the loop produces.

## The balancing loop that looks like progress

Backlog rises. Leadership adds overtime, a surge hire, or a weekend war room. Throughput rises. The backlog falls. The surge ends, because the visible pain ended. If the reinforcing loop is untouched, the inflow of repeat work returns and the backlog rebuilds.

That balancing loop is real, and it is why firefighting feels effective. The team did change the number everyone watches. The change expires when the surge expires. Calling the surge a success and the return a new failure misses that they are one structure on two clocks.

A map helps here only if both loops are on it. A picture of the backlog alone will recommend another surge. A picture of recognition and prevention time will ask which link the surge never touched.

## Delays hide the prevention payoff

Prevention metrics move slowly: repeat rate inside a class, change failure on a known cause, time-to-detect on a class you instrumented. Firefighting metrics move daily. Attention follows the fast clock unless you put a leading indicator on the slow one.

The mismatch is the same mechanism as other [delays in feedback loops](/blog/delays-in-feedback-loops). A prevention block can be working while this week's incident count is still ugly, because the incidents already in flight were created earlier. If you judge the block on this week's count alone, you will reassign the people and erase the test. Write the delay next to the prevention link before the first review.

## What to change in the loop

Shift a piece of recognition toward prevented recurrence, in a form the team can see. That might be credit in the review for a class of incident that stayed down, not a speech about ownership. Fund a small prevention slice and protect it from redeployment when the next urgent day arrives. If the slice is borrowable, it is not a slice. It is spare capacity for the reinforcing loop.

Map one structural link per quarter and give it an owner. One link is enough: the handoff that recreates the ticket, the missing check that lets the same defect escape, the alert that pages people who cannot fix the class.

## When firefighting is the right mode

A genuine emergency, a safety incident, or a novel failure with people at risk needs surge mode. Take the surge. The structural problem is chronic recurrence of a known class, not the occasional spike. If you cannot separate those two in the weekly review, you will either under-react to danger or over-react to the familiar queue. Split the board: novel and safety items in one lane, repeat classes in another, with the prevention time attached only to the second.

Also leave firefighting in place when the volume is truly one-off, such as a migration weekend that will not recur. Mapping a loop for a single event adds ceremony. Map it when the same class has already returned.

## Count last week before you redesign the team

Estimate hours on repeat classes versus hours on novel work. You do not need a precise study. A rough ratio, said out loud in the ops review, changes the conversation from who stayed late to which loop got the time. If almost all hours sat on repeats, another hiring surge without a protected prevention block extends the balancing loop and leaves R1 in place. If the hours sat on novel safety work, the firefighting story is the wrong diagnosis this month.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is operational firefighting a culture problem?</h3>
  <p>Culture follows what the loops reward. If only visible saves are recognized, people will keep producing visible saves. Change the metric you review and protect the prevention time, and the norms have something to attach to.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Will more headcount break the loop?</h3>
  <p>Only if some of the new time stays on prevention after the backlog drops. If new people are pulled into the same urgent queue, you have lengthened the surge. The reinforcing loop is unchanged.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How do we tell a real emergency from the spiral?</h3>
  <p>Novel harm and safety incidents get surge mode. A class you have already cleared before is the spiral. Separate them in the review so one does not borrow the other's rules.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How is the firefighting spiral different from "recurring problems"?</h3>
  <p>Recurring problems is the symptom in leadership language. The spiral is the mechanism: reward for saves, less prevention, more of the same class. Without a map, teams argue about blame. With R1 and B1, they argue about links and delays.</p>
</div>
