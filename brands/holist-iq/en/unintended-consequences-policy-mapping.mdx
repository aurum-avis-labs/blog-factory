**Unintended consequences** in policy are usually adaptations. A rule changes what pays, what is risky, or what takes time, and actors move along paths the memo did not draw. **Policy mapping** with causal loops makes those paths discussable before rollout, while you can still change the instrument or the monitoring.

This is for policy teams, regulators, and program owners who need a structure stakeholders can read. It does not claim to predict every outcome. It claims you can name the adaptations you already know how to look for, and attach a delay and a watch metric to each.

If you need the wider case for this kind of design, start with [systems thinking for public policy](/blog/systems-thinking-for-public-policy-designing-better-political-decisions-in-compl). When you are choosing how deep the instrument should sit, use [leverage points in systems thinking](/blog/leverage-points-systems-thinking).

## Map actors who can adapt, not only the headline metric

List who can change behavior when the lever moves: firms, households, intermediaries, clinicians, landlords, enforcement staff, the contractors who implement the rule. Draw links from the lever to their constraints. Time, cash, legal risk, and spare capacity matter more than a slogan about compliance.

A map that only connects the lever to the KPI in the press line will show success by construction. The unintended path lives in the actors who were left off because they are not the official target.

For each actor, one sentence is enough: when this constraint tightens or loosens, they do this next. If you cannot write the sentence, you do not yet have a link. You have a worry.

## Trace second-order paths on purpose

For each first-order gain, ask two questions. Who gains time to adapt before enforcement or reporting catches up? Where does the activity go if the official channel gets more expensive?

Mark delays on enforcement, appeals, reporting, and procurement. Those delays are the window in which the adaptation happens. A rule that looks tight on day one and leaky in month six is often a delay problem, not a mystery.

**Illustration, not a recorded case.** Stricter reporting aimed at large firms raises their cost of a contract. Work shifts toward smaller entities with thinner oversight. The official metric on large-firm reporting improves. A later gap shows up in the smaller population, which the dashboard was not built to see. The map's job is to put that second population on the page before rollout, with a delay on the oversight link.

Use the same shape for other instruments. A subsidy can raise demand faster than the stock of people who can deliver, so wait times grow and trust in the program falls. You do not need a named jurisdiction to ask whether your delivery stock is a variable or an assumption of instant capacity.

## Run a pre-mortem on the links

Ask stakeholders to mark the links they do not believe. Capture the alternate polarity as a dotted hypothesis with their name on the doubt. Hour one is for disagreement you can see. Arguing "the fact" before the link is named just sorts the room by rank.

A useful pre-mortem question: if this policy is described as a failure in two years, which arrow in today's map will people say we refused to draw? Draw it now as a hypothesis. You can still reject it after the evidence rule is set.

## Write the boundary so critics have something real to attack

State what the map excludes: cross-border spillovers, the informal economy, a population you cannot measure, a security limit on what you will publish. Critics should be able to say "your boundary is wrong" instead of "you are naive." A map without omissions is advertising.

Keep the boundary on the same page as the loops. A footnote nobody reads will be treated as if the omission were covered.

## Watch the second hop, not only the target

Choose one or two variables that sit on the adaptation path, downstream of the official KPI. Reporting completeness in the population the activity might flee to. Wait time. Exemption rate. Appeals. Price in the adjacent market. Assign an owner and a date before rollout, not after the first bad headline.

If that variable has no data, the map has told you the gap. Either build a thin measure, or accept that you will learn about the adaptation from outside the program. Do not pretend the official KPI includes the hop. It does not.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Actor list includes intermediaries and the people who enforce the rule</li>
  <li>Each first-order gain has a "where does it migrate" sentence</li>
  <li>At least one delay is written in months, not as "later"</li>
  <li>Disputed signs are dotted, with a name attached</li>
  <li>The boundary says what you left out</li>
  <li>A second-hop metric has an owner and a date</li>
</ul>

## When not to publish the map

Do not release the working map when security, an active investigation, or a live negotiation would be harmed by showing the adaptation path in public. Internal use can still discipline the instrument. A public diagram that teaches people how to slip the rule is not a transparency win. Publish the boundary and the monitoring commitment instead, and keep the sensitive links in the working file.

## One page on the next memo

On the next policy memo, add three adaptation links and one delay before the steering group meets. If the group only discusses the target KPI, the page has not been read. Ask which of the three links they want dotted. That question is the whole method in miniature.

## Keep the map through consultation

Consultation changes links. A file that cannot show what moved, and why, will be redrawn from memory after each meeting. ## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is this partisan fact checking?</h3>
  <p>No. The work is structural: which feedback and which adaptation the headline design leaves out. You can do it for a proposal you support. The test is whether the omitted path is on the page.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Do we need a quantitative simulation first?</h3>
  <p>Not always. Qualitative loops plus monitoring on second-hop variables reduce surprise for many instruments. Simulate when the decision depends on levels, rates, and units. Then keep the variable names tied to the loop map so the two views can correct each other.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How is this different from an impact assessment template?</h3>
  <p>Templates list categories: environment, distribution, small firms. Loops force a directed story between categories, and a delay. A category with no arrow is a heading, not a consequence.</p>
</div>
