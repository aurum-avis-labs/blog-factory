Executives rarely need the full wall of notes. They need an **executive brief from a causal loop map**: one page, the loops that dominate the decision, the delay that sets the clock, the trade-offs among options, and a test with an owner. The map stays attached. The brief is what gets read before the decision date.

This workflow is for someone who already has a map and a decision. If you are still building the first loops, start with the [Holist-IQ tutorial](/blog/holist-iq-tutorial). If you are still checking whether a loop map is the right product for the team, use the [Holist-IQ overview](/blog/holist-iq).

## What goes on the single page

Five blocks are enough. More than that becomes a second map in prose.

1. Outcome and decision date. State the metric and the day the choice will be made. If there is no date, you are writing a briefing note for later, and you should say so.
2. Dominant reinforcing and balancing loops in plain language. Two stories, each a few lines, each traceable on the map. Name them R1 and B1 so a question in the meeting can point at a loop.
3. The longest delay on the critical path. A range is fine. This is the sentence that stops the room from judging a quarterly mechanism on a monthly chart.
4. Options written as loop effects. For each option, say which link it moves, what happens to the named loops, and which side effect you will watch. Skip feature lists and vendor language.
5. One recommended test. Owner, leading indicator, window, and the result that would make you drop the recommendation.

A reader who only has eight minutes should be able to repeat those five blocks without the diagram. If they cannot, the brief is still a tour of the whiteboard.

## Keep the structure, move the notation

Plus and minus signs help the people who built the map. They slow everyone else. Put a short link table in an appendix if the decision needs an audit trail, and keep the loop names in the body. The body sentence is "overtime rises, review time falls, escapes rise later." The appendix can carry the signs.

Do not "simplify" by dropping the loop boundary. A paragraph of drivers with no return path is a list, and lists are how the same argument returns next quarter. Leadership can handle two loops. They should not have to reconstruct them from bullet fragments.

## Evidence, dissent, and what you are not claiming

Attach the evidence you actually have, and label its limit. A single quarter of movement is not confirmation of a loop. A workshop agreement is not a measurement. Write "agreed in the session" and "observed in the incident log" as different kinds of support.

Keep the alternate link that skeptics proposed, drawn as a dotted or clearly marked alternative on the map and named in one line on the brief. Executives decide with visible uncertainty. A false consensus forces the dissent into the hallway, where it becomes a reason to ignore the brief later. If you cannot state the strongest objection, the page is unfinished.

Also say what the brief does not decide. Scope limits prevent the meeting from treating a local operations loop as a group strategy. One sentence is enough: the map covers this product line, this year, this metric.

## Shrink a map you already have

Take a current diagram and force it into the five blocks, on paper, before you open a template. If you cannot name two loops without looking at every node, the map is not ready to brief. If you can name them and cannot name a delay, write "delay unknown" in block three and make that the first question, not a hidden gap. If every option moves the same link, you do not have options. You have one bet with several labels.

## When a one-page brief is the wrong artifact

Skip it when the room is still deciding what the outcome is. A brief will freeze a slogan. Skip it when the only audience is the working team and they need the live map, not a compression. Skip it when the decision is a single calculated stock and the argument is numeric. In that case the loop brief can be a preface, and the model output is the decision page.

Also skip the Holist-IQ path when you need a governed financial model or a live operational dashboard as the primary artifact. The brief does not replace those. It tells leadership which loop they are about to spend money on.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How long should the brief be?</h3>
  <p>One page, plus an optional appendix for the link table and the alternate hypotheses. If the story needs a third page, you still have two decisions inside one document.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Who writes it?</h3>
  <p>The person who can trace the loops, with the decision owner named on the page. A communications pass for clarity is useful. A pass that removes the delay, the dissent, or the loop names is not.</p>
</div>
