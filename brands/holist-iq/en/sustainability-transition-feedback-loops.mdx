## Read lock-in in sustainability transitions as structure

<p class="blog-lede">Feedback loops explain why a run of incremental wins can stall: stocks, skills and contracts reinforce the current option while price or rule pressure often arrives on a slower clock.</p>

Installed assets, skills, contracts and political support keep the existing option in play. Balancing pressure from a price, a rule or a new alternative often reaches the same stock years later. If you only watch the quarterly efficiency number, lock-in looks like inertia. It is a structure you can name on half a page before the next "transition" project starts.

This is for people who own a transition portfolio: facilities, product lines, public programmes or a council that declares a quick win and then sees the same pattern return. It is a problem description with a mappable exercise, not a technology catalogue.

## What reinforcing lock-in looks like

A reinforcing loop here is ordinary. The existing option has a stock of infrastructure. Using it builds operating skill and supplier relationships. Scale lowers unit cost relative to the alternative. Those advantages attract political and customer support for staying. Support defers the retrofit, so the stock of old assets remains. The loop needs no villain. It needs time and a stock that is expensive to strand.

Read it as variables:

<ul class="blog-facts">
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Stock</span><strong>Assets in use</strong><p>Compatible with today's infrastructure, not only "more efficient".</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Cost</span><strong>Unit cost and familiarity</strong><p>Relative to the alternative, including training and spares.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Politics</span><strong>Contracts and subsidy logic</strong><p>What props up the status quo and pushes replacement out.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Investment</span><strong>Diverted capital</strong><p>Budget that does not flow into replacement.</p></li>
</ul>

Each step makes the next more reasonable. A single efficiency project inside this loop can be real and still leave the loop intact: lower emissions per unit on the asset you already have, and a cheaper cost to keep that same asset running.

## Balancing loops that arrive late

A carbon price, a performance standard or a procurement rule is often meant as a balancing loop: pressure rises with the gap, actors invest in the alternative, the gap shrinks. The loop is only as fast as the stocks it has to move. Capital cycles, permits, supplier retooling and retraining are delays. If the balancing signal stays weaker than the reinforcing return on the old asset for several years, the transition story and the capital budget will disagree, and the budget will win even when the slides say ambition.

Name the delay on the map. "Later" is not a delay. "Next replacement window for this asset class, in years" is a delay. [Delays in feedback loops](/blog/delays-in-feedback-loops) helps when a council treats a slow loop as failed policy.

## Map physical variables and belief variables together

Emissions, fuel use and tonnes sit next to operations. Perceived feasibility, career risk and "this will not get permitted" sit next to adoption. Drop either side and the map lies in a predictable way. A physical-only map sends you to another efficiency project on the locked-in asset. A belief-only map sends you to a campaign that does not change the stock.

On one page, keep both, and mark which arrows are material flows and which are expectations. If the decision later needs a carbon or energy balance, that slice belongs in a stock-and-flow model. The loop map is still the place where politics and expectations stay visible. The comparison is in [stock and flow vs causal loops](/blog/stock-and-flow-vs-causal-loops).

## Map one loop in four steps

Pick one product, facility or programme. The exercise takes about an hour with two roles: someone who knows the stock and someone who knows the budget or contracts.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Fix the stock and the boundary</h3><p>What counts as stock (assets, contracts, certified capacity), and what sits outside the map because it will leave soon?</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Sketch the reinforcing loop</h3><p>Three or four variables, arrows labelled "more of" or "less of". Each arrow needs one sentence on why it holds in your organisation.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Add the balancing lever and the delay</h3><p>Which rule or price should press on the stock, and in which year does it first touch it in practice? Write the delay in years or budget cycles, not as "medium term".</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Read it back with finance or procurement</h3><p>Ask which budget line extends the stock if you call this project a transition. Argument at step 3 or 4 is often the real work.</p></li>
</ol>

If nobody can name the stock in step 1, the portfolio is still a project list. If the delay line starts an argument, you have found what quick wins skip.

## Check whether the balancing lever is strong enough

A qualitative map is enough for the first question, structural or just a project. For the second question, whether your lever is enough, you do not need a simulation yet. You need three explicit comparisons on the same page:

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>How much the reinforcing loop tends to grow the stock per year (units or capex, roughly)</li>
  <li>How strong the balancing signal is per year (price, quota, procurement share), also roughly</li>
  <li>Delay until the lever touches the stock in reality, not only in the statute</li>
</ul>

If the balancing signal stays smaller than the reinforcing return on the old asset across that delay, the transition story is a narrative. That is not a moral verdict. It points to where capital, rules or procurement need sharpening before you measure efficiency on the locked-in asset again.

## What better looks like before the next quick win

Better is not a longer strategy deck. It is a named lock-in loop, a named balancing loop and a delay you refuse to average away. Then one conversation about where a small shift could sit before the next capital cycle.

Useful questions in that conversation:

- Which stock are we extending when we call a project a transition?
- Which balancing lever is funded, and in which year does it first touch that stock?
- Who gains from the current unit-cost advantage, and is that gain on the map or only in the appendix?

If you cannot answer the first question, you are describing a project. The map makes the gap visible.

## Honest limits

A causal loop diagram clarifies the story and the disagreement. It does not produce a budget, a life-cycle inventory or a forecast of tonnes. Do not present a qualitative loop as if it were a mitigation pathway. Do not skip the loop and treat a single coefficient as the transition. Teams that need market reactions around prices and investment can add context from [economic systems modeling](/blog/economic-systems-modeling-mapping-policies-interest-rates-and-market-feedback-lo), still without pretending one diagram is the whole economy.

  <span class="blog-kicker">Note</span>
  <p>This framing is not an argument against technology. It asks where a technology has to outrun reinforcing lock-in, including skill and politics, rather than only beating last year's efficiency on the incumbent asset.</p>
</aside>

## When a quick win is still the right call

Some assets will turn over soon anyway. A short-payback change on a site you are about to exit does not need a lock-in workshop. Use the loop map when the same "win" has been booked before and the stock of the old option is still growing, or when several functions celebrate different metrics from the same project.

Loop types, if the labels R and B are new in the room, are covered in [reinforcing vs balancing loops](/blog/reinforcing-vs-balancing-loops).

## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is mapping lock-in an argument against incremental efficiency?</h3>
  <p>No. Incremental work can be the right move on an asset that is leaving the stock soon. The map shows when that work also deepens the case for keeping a stock you said you were retiring.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Do sustainability transitions always need a simulation?</h3>
  <p>Not for the first argument about structure. You need a simulation, or at least a stock-and-flow balance, when the claim is about tonnes, energy or money over time. The loop map does not replace that claim.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Who should be in the room when the map goes to the council?</h3>
  <p>At least someone who knows the physical stock and someone who releases capital or signs contracts. Without the second person, the delay line stays a hope.</p>
</div>
