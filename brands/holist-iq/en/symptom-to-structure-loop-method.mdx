## What symptom-to-structure elicitation is for

When a team keeps treating the latest spike as a fresh incident, **symptom to structure** moves the conversation to a loop you can test. Five steps keep blame out of variable names and put delays where people already feel them. The method is for recurring metrics. A one-off accident belongs in an incident review, not here.

The output is a small, named structure: an outcome that returned, the last fixes, one or two loops that could explain the return, and one link you will measure. It is not a wall of every factor anyone can imagine.

## Step 1: Anchor the recurring metric

Write the outcome in the words the metric already uses: backlog over a threshold, the same defect class, a budget line that reopens, a complaint code that returns after every campaign. Note the last three times it came back, and what "back" means in numbers or in a date.

If it never returned, stop. You do not have a structural question yet. Brainstorming tools and an ordinary after-action review are enough. Forcing a loop onto a single event produces a diagram that cannot be falsified, because nothing is expected to happen again.

Also write the quick fixes you already tried and what each one targeted. Three is enough. The list is evidence of the level you have been pulling, not a confession.

## Step 2: Symptom wall, then neutral rename

List the pains people actually say. Rename each one before it becomes a node. "They don't care" becomes something you could see: time from alert to response, share of tickets reopened, fraction of checks skipped on a deadline. "The policy failed" becomes a gap you can point at: reported volume, exemption rate, waiting time.

Neutral names are how other functions stay in the room. A name that accuses a group ends the method in the first ten minutes. If a pain cannot be renamed, park it as context, not as a variable.

## Step 3: Story links before polarity

Draw arrows only after someone tells the story in one or two sentences: when this moved, that moved, and we saw it in this place. Then mark plus or minus. Polarity without a story is how maps become spaghetti.

Disagreement stays on the map as a dotted alternative, with the doubter's name on it. Do not vote a sign into existence. The later test is what the sign was for. Before you add arrows, check that every link has a speaker and a place it was observed. Missing polarity and unnamed arrows are the usual reasons a busy map explains nothing.

## Step 4: Close loops and mark delays

Follow arrows until you return to the metric. Give the loop a plain name the team would use in a hallway: the overtime loop, the exemption loop, the reopen loop. Mark reinforcing or balancing only after you can count the negative links. If you cannot close a loop, say so. An open chain is a hypothesis about sequence, not a structure that maintains itself.

Add a delay note on the longest administrative, hiring, or trust link. That note is often the reason the last fix looked good on the chart inside the delay window, then the loop restored the metric. If every link is drawn as instant, the map will recommend another instant fix.

## Step 5: Choose one link to instrument

Pick one link whose sign or delay would change the next action. Attach a leading indicator you can read before the metric "returns" again, an owner, and a window of about six weeks. Write what you will do if the indicator supports the link, and what you will do if it does not.

Do not instrument the whole map. A dashboard of fifteen new measures is a second project. One link teaches the team whether the structure is real enough to matter.

## Where the method usually fails

People skip the recurrence check and map a crisis that will not repeat. They collect thirty variables before any arrow has a story. They open a software trial while the names are still accusations. Any one of these produces a picture and no next measurement.

A shorter failure: the facilitator closes a loop that the operators do not recognize, because the close makes the diagram look finished. If the people who live in the metric will not repeat the loop name, it is not closed. It is decorated.

## When to stop early

Stop after step 1 if there is no recurrence. Stop after step 3 if you cannot get a story for the arrows without inventing actors who are not in the room. Stop after step 5 once the test is running. More loops can wait until the first link has been looked at. A second workshop in the same week mostly adds variables.

## Retro one incident only as practice

If the team is new to the sequence, run steps 1 to 3 on a past outage or a past policy surprise that did return. Thirty minutes. The goal is the rename and one story-arrow, not a publishable map. Then do the live metric with the full five steps.

## Frequently asked questions

### How is this different from root cause analysis?

Root cause analysis is built for an incident: find a cause, contain it, prevent that path. This method is built for a metric that comes back, including the way people adapt to the last fix. Use both when a single event also sits on a pattern you have seen before.

### Do we need the whole organization in the room?

No. You need people who can tell the arrow stories and someone who can read the metric. A crowd adds names faster than stories.

### What if two loops both look plausible?

Keep both. Instrument the link that would tell them apart inside the six-week window. Do not merge them into one unnamed tangle.

### Can we run this without software?

Yes. Paper and a wall work for steps 1 to 4. Software helps when you need version history and shared edits after the room. Holist-IQ fits that post-meeting edit pass; it does not replace the rename conversation.
