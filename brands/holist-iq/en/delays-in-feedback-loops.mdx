**Delays in feedback loops** are why sound plans get cancelled early. A balancing response can be working while the metric on the agenda still worsens. A reinforcing problem can look flat until it crosses a threshold and then seems sudden. The usual fix, a tighter review cadence or a new owner, treats the lag as stubbornness. It often punishes the intervention that needed a longer clock.

This page is for executives and operators who have already stopped a pilot, or who are about to. It covers how to mark a delay, which metric sits on which clock, and when to extend a test. Recurring patterns around the same outcome are in [why the same business problems keep coming back](/blog/why-the-same-business-problems-keep-coming-back). Delay marks on a diagram are in [how to read a causal loop diagram](/blog/how-to-read-a-causal-loop-diagram).

## Why delays break ordinary judgment

Attention follows the report people already have: weekly incidents, monthly revenue, a quarterly board pack. The system often moves on a slower stock. Hiring, training, trust, regulation, physical inventory, and installed equipment do not update on the dashboard's clock. When those clocks differ, the room blames the owner of the metric. The structure would have predicted the lag if someone had written it down.

Tighter reviews, a new owner, or a second initiative piled on the first all leave that lag in place. Mark the delay on the map, name one leading variable on the delayed path, and give the test a window tied to that delay. Stop early only when the leading variable moves the wrong way.

## Patterns worth marking

| Pattern | Example | What to watch first |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity | Hiring, training, certification | Pipeline and skill coverage, before headcount shows up in throughput |
| Trust | Brand, safety reputation, compliance confidence | Complaints, near-misses, or audit findings, before revenue or incident totals |
| Physical stock | Inventory, maintenance backlog, infrastructure | Age of the stock and the inflow, before a rupture or a stockout |
| Policy and budget | Legislative or annual funding cycles | What was adopted, before outcomes that wait on the next cycle |

The last column is the variable that can move while the official KPI is still quiet. If you cannot name it, you cannot manage the delay yet.

## Mark the delay where someone will see it

Put a delay mark on the link, or a side table with a duration range tied to named variables. State which variable moves first and which variable the board already tracks. Ranges are more honest than a false point estimate. "One to two quarters" is usable. "Soon" will be read as "this month" by the person who owns the target.

**Worked sketch.** A plant wants fewer recordable incidents. The proposed link is: safety training up, incidents down. Certification for the people who do the risky work finishes about two quarters after the budget is spent. Finance watches monthly training spend and monthly incident counts. In months one and two, spend is up and incidents are flat or worse, because the people not yet certified are still on the job and because attention on reporting can raise the count. The leading variables are certification completions and the share of risky tasks done by certified staff. If those move as expected, the incident series is not yet the verdict.

That sketch is a reading example, not a measured program. Your durations will differ. The habit is the same: write the slow link, name the fast number people will misuse, and name the earlier number that belongs to the delay.

## Test across two clocks

Pick one leading variable on the delayed path and decide the direction you expect. Review on that variable's clock. If it moves as expected while the lagging KPI is still flat, extend the test to the end of the delay you wrote down. If you swap strategy at the first flat month, you never observe the loop you funded.

Also decide what "wrong way" means before the pilot starts. A leading variable that stalls is different from one that reverses. Stall can be an execution problem. Reversal is evidence against the link. Write that distinction in the same note as the delay so a later reader does not renegotiate it under pressure.

Pilots should run at least as long as the longest delay on the critical path you identified, unless the leading indicator fails first or a safety limit is hit. Open-ended tests are how delays become excuses. A named window is what makes the delay operational.

## When waiting is the wrong call

Stop when leading indicators move the wrong way, when costs or harms are irreversible, or when a safety or ethical limit is already breached. Delay language is not cover for a red flag the room can already see. Also stop if you cannot name any leading variable. Waiting on the lagging KPI alone, with no earlier signal, is patience without a map.

Some delays are what keep a balancing loop from jerking between extremes. If [reinforcing and balancing loops](/blog/reinforcing-vs-balancing-loops) are new, read the behavior before you try to delete every lag.

## Annotate one project you already called a failure

Choose an initiative leadership stopped early. Add delay marks after the fact. Ask whether a leading metric you already had was moving in the predicted direction. If it was, the cancellation measured the fast clock. If it was not, the lesson is the link. Either way you see the pattern before the next early stop. The same lag is how [second-order effects](/blog/second-order-effects-in-decisions) get missed: the first effect is still in transit.

Delays shift when scale shifts. A training lag at one site is not the lag after a national rollout. Keep the note next to the loop and revise it when the boundary changes. The [systems thinking tool checklist](/blog/systems-thinking-tool-checklist) is the right filter if those notes must survive a change of owner. Holist-IQ keeps delay notes on the loop map itself, so the timing assumption travels with the structure. Business settings for the same habit are in [systems thinking in business](/blog/systems-thinking-in-business).
