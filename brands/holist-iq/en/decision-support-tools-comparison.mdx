## Decision support tools compared: match the artifact to the question

<p class="blog-lede">Spreadsheets, dashboards, and causal loop maps fail when each one is asked to answer every question in the same meeting.</p>

**Decision support tools** only work when you match the artifact to the decision clock and to who must agree. Spreadsheets carry numbers and scenarios. Dashboards show what moved. Causal loop maps show how those numbers feed each other and come back. Stock-and-flow models add accumulation when a quantity must be calculated. Whiteboards carry the first hour of talk. Used as a set, they cover a recurring decision. Used as substitutes, they hide the feedback that made the decision recur.

## Which question are you actually asking?

Three questions get mixed in the same meeting. "What is the number?" belongs in a spreadsheet or the system of record. "What moved since yesterday?" belongs on a dashboard. "Why does this metric return after we fix it?" belongs on a causal loop map. A fourth question, "how much accumulates if this rate holds?", belongs in a stock-and-flow model once the loops are agreed.

If you cannot say which question next Thursday's meeting must answer, do not buy another license. Name the metric, the last time it returned, and the decision date. Then the rows below are comparable.

## Spreadsheets: precision on a slice

Excel and Google Sheets are the right home for budgets, scenario tables, and models with a named owner. They are strong when the decision is reversible, the inputs are quantitative, and the argument is about values rather than about structure. A shared sheet also leaves an audit trail if people version it with discipline.

They weakly show feedback. A circular reference is an error, not a reinforcing loop. You can design loops in a sheet, with lagged columns and careful notes, and some finance teams should. Most operational sheets do not. The reader sees this month's variance and has to remember why last quarter's fix fed this month's spike.

Stay with a spreadsheet when the job is a number the organization already trusts, on a clock the sheet can update. Pass on it as the only decision artifact when the same variance keeps returning and the comments in column Z disagree about why. Add a loop map beside the sheet. Do not throw the sheet away.

## Dashboards: movement without the story

BI dashboards such as Looker, Power BI, and Tableau are built to show movement: a KPI crossed a threshold, a segment diverged, a queue grew. That is the right tool for daily or weekly operational monitoring, where someone must notice and route.

They rarely show why two metrics influence each other, or how long the influence takes. A chart of incidents and a chart of overtime can sit on one page and still leave the loop unspoken. Filters answer "where did it move?" They do not answer "which loop dominates this month?"

Use a dashboard when the question is detection. Skip it as the explanation layer when leadership already sees the spike and still argues about the cause. Pair the alert with a loop map that names the path from the spike back to the work that creates the next spike. The dashboard remains the sensor. The map remains the argument.

## Causal loop maps: structure for a recurring decision

Holist-IQ, kumu, and other causal loop diagram tools put loops, polarity, and shared assumptions on the page. The job is structural. Which links reinforce, which links balance, where a delay sits, and which link a team can actually move before the decision date.

They are the right tool when a metric returns despite fixes, when functions disagree about the mechanism, and when the decision needs a story more than another chart. Holist-IQ is aimed at maintained loop maps that start from plain language, so the map can stay a decision-support artifact rather than a one-off drawing. It is a poor fit when you only need a budget total or a live operational screen.

Skip a loop-first tool when the situation has no recurrence, when nobody can name variables yet, or when the next step is a governed calculation of stocks. In that last case, agree the loops first if the story is still contested, then move the agreed structure into a quantified model. Buying the map tool to avoid a facilitation problem does not fix the facilitation problem.

## Stock-and-flow tools: when the quantity must be calculated

Stella-class and Vensim-class tools add stocks, flows, and equations. Use them when accumulation itself is the decision: inventory, cash, capacity, workforce, or a pollutant stock, and when you must compare numeric scenarios after the causal story is stable enough to encode.

They are heavier to learn and easier to overfit. A precise chart from an unstated loop still hides the disagreement. People who need the equation should use this class. People who still argue about the story should not start here. The boundary between the two artifacts is covered in [stock and flow vs causal loops](/blog/stock-and-flow-vs-causal-loops).

Stay on a causal loop map when stakeholders cannot yet agree the links. Move to stocks and flows when the remaining argument is about rates, capacities, and timing in numbers.

## Whiteboards: speed, then a handoff

Miro, Lucid, and a wall with pens are the fastest way to get contested links into the room. They are a good first hour. Loop notation stays consistent only if someone enforces polarity, names, and delays. Without that discipline, the board becomes a mind map with arrows.

Pen and paper remains valid for discovery. It is a weak system of record. Photos lose alternate links, and remote readers cannot see which arrow was contested. Plan the handoff before the workshop ends: a variable list, two loop names, one delay, one test. If the map must survive the next owner, move it into a tool that keeps those fields. See [causal loop diagram software](/blog/causal-loop-diagram-software) for that choice.

## What these tools cost you (beyond the license)

Vendor list prices change by seat and region, so this section stays qualitative. A spreadsheet costs mostly reviewer time when ownership is clear. A dashboard costs data plumbing and someone who answers pages. A maintained loop map costs facilitation and an owner who updates polarity when the story shifts. A stock-and-flow model costs modeler time and governance so the equations stay tied to the agreed loops.

The expensive mistake is paying for overlap without a job split. Three subscriptions and no artifact assigned to Thursday's decision is still a process problem.

## A selection matrix you can reuse

| Question you must answer | Lean toward | Leave it when |
|---|---|---|
| A metric returns after fixes | Causal loop map | There is no recurrence to explain |
| Monthly budget or scenario values | Spreadsheet | The fight is about structure, not the cells |
| Daily operational monitoring | Dashboard | You already see the spike and still disagree on cause |
| Quantified accumulation | Stella or Vensim class | The causal story is still contested |
| Cross-functional agreement on why | Holist-IQ or another CLD-first tool | You only need a one-off sketch |
| First-hour discovery | Whiteboard or paper | The map must outlive the meeting |

The middle column is a lean, not a mandate. Many decisions need two rows: a dashboard to notice, a loop map to explain, a spreadsheet to cost the test.

## When another license will not help

If the last three tools failed because nobody facilitated the conversation, another subscription repeats the failure. Fix the workshop cadence, the owner, and the decision date before you add software. Also skip a purchase when the group has not named the outcome metric. Tools do not supply the variable list.

A second failure mode is overlap without a job split. The sheet, the dashboard, and the map all get updated by different people, and none of them is the artifact for Thursday's decision. Assign one artifact to the decision, and let the others feed it.

## Label the last three decisions

Write down the last three recurring decisions, the tool you used, and one loop the tool did not show. If the missing loop would have changed the option you picked, that decision wanted a causal map beside the number. If the missing piece was a quantity, you wanted a stock-and-flow pass or a better sheet. This half hour is more informative than a feature grid, and it uses your own history.

Use the [systems thinking tool checklist](/blog/systems-thinking-tool-checklist) when the missing piece is a maintained map and you need buying criteria. Holist-IQ belongs in the loop-map row: plain-language input, named feedback, and a map you keep as the decision changes. It does not replace the spreadsheet that holds the budget or the dashboard that pages the on-call lead.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Can a dashboard replace a causal loop diagram?</h3>
  <p>Dashboards tell you something moved. A causal loop diagram shows the feedback structure you are willing to argue about. Keep both when the metric is operational and recurring.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should we start with a quantified model?</h3>
  <p>Start with quantities when the stock and the rates are already agreed. Start with a causal loop map when people still propose different mechanisms. Encoding a disputed story in equations makes the chart look finished.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is a whiteboard enough?</h3>
  <p>Enough for the first hour and for a decision that will be taken before the ink dries. Not enough if a later owner must see polarity, delays, and the link you chose not to test.</p>
</div>
