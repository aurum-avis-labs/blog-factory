## What a causal loop diagram is

A **causal loop diagram** is a map of how factors in a situation change one another: variables, arrows, and closed paths. Use one when a straight story ("the backlog caused the complaints") hides the feedback that keeps producing the same result.

You can read one without a simulation background. You need shared labels, a polarity rule, and a walk around each loop. This page is that notation. Templates, makers, and buyer criteria are in the [causal loop diagram software guide](/blog/causal-loop-diagram-software).

It helps executives, policy teams, and analysts agree on structure before they spend money or reputation. It is a weak fit for a one-off task with no later effect, and for a workshop that only wants a wall of sticky notes.

## The four words on the map

<dl class="blog-terms">
  <div>
    <dt>Variable</dt>
    <dd>A factor that can rise or fall, named so a stranger could measure it in principle.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Link</dt>
    <dd>An arrow from A to B: a change in A tends to change B, other things equal.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Polarity</dt>
    <dd>Whether B ends higher or lower than it would have been.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Loop</dt>
    <dd>A path of arrows that returns to the variable you started from.</dd>
  </div>
</dl>

Prefer neutral names: hiring pace, open tickets, trust in the regulator. "Bad culture" is a verdict, and it ends the conversation the map was meant to open. Keep the first map small enough to trace aloud, often under a dozen variables.

Each link is a hypothesis about direction. It says nothing about size or timing. Write it as a sentence beside the arrow ("if A rises, B ends higher, other things equal"). Without that sentence, the diagram is a poster.

## What plus and minus mean

Polarity is relative to what would have happened otherwise. In *Business Dynamics* (2000), John Sterman defines a positive link this way: if the cause increases, the effect is higher than it would otherwise have been, and if the cause decreases, the effect is lower than it would otherwise have been. A negative link runs the other way. Burns and Musa quote that definition in an open [System Dynamics Society paper (2001)](https://proceedings.systemdynamics.org/2001/papers/Burns_1.pdf).

"Both move up" fails on stocks. Births are a positive link into population. If deaths are larger, population still falls. The plus sign only says population is higher than it would have been without those births.

Some facilitators write **s** (same direction) and **o** (opposite) instead of **+** and **−**. System dynamics keeps plus and minus, because "same direction" does not describe a flow adding into a stock. [The Systems Thinker](https://thesystemsthinker.com/fine-tuning-your-causal-loop-diagrams-part-i/) sets out that argument, and the safer way to name a loop.

<aside class="blog-callout">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Correction</span>
  <p>A plus link means the effect ends higher than it would have been. A minus link means it ends lower. Neither says the effect is rising or falling. A spiral downward, where a fall leads to a further fall, is still reinforcing.</p>
</aside>

Mark every link before anyone debates an intervention. If one arrow needs both signs, the variable is doing two jobs and should split.

## Reinforcing and balancing loops

A path that returns to its start is a feedback loop. **Reinforcing** (R) sends the change back as more of the same. **Balancing** (B) sends it back opposed. Name the loop in a sentence. "R1" alone will not survive the next meeting.

Assume one variable rises, and say each link aloud until you return. A further rise means reinforcing. A fall means balancing. Counting minus links is the fast check: even, zero included, is reinforcing; odd is balancing. One wrong sign ruins the count, so the trace stays the check.

Write "delay" on a link, or draw two short strokes across it, when the lag would change the decision. A hire approved today and a hire who can take tickets in four months are different links. Without the mark, the room expects the balancing loop to win this week.

For how these loops show up in operating decisions, see [systems thinking in business](/blog/systems-thinking-in-business).

## How to read a causal loop diagram

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Write the question</h3><p>One decision or one tension above the canvas. If the map cannot answer it, the variables belong in a different meeting.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Read the names aloud</h3><p>A label that needs a paragraph is not a variable yet. Rename it.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Say one link at a time</h3><p>"If overtime rises, reply quality ends lower than it would have, other things equal."</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Trace each loop</h3><p>Walk a rise around the path. Mark R if it comes back amplified, B if it comes back opposed.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Mark the delays</h3><p>Put them on the links that make a fix look successful before it has worked.</p></li>
  <li data-step="6"><h3>Pick a link to probe</h3><p>Which arrow, if you changed its strength, would change what the loop does? That is the next research question.</p></li>
</ol>

## A made-up example

Take a support team with too many open tickets.

One loop: more open tickets lead to more overtime. More overtime leaves reply quality lower than it would have been. Lower quality leaves trust lower. Lower trust leaves more repeat contacts, which leaves more open tickets. Walk a rise in tickets around that path and you finish with still more tickets. That is reinforcing. The short-term push, overtime, feeds the queue it was meant to clear. Two minus signs on the path do not make it balancing. An even count of minus links comes back the same way.

A second loop: more open tickets lead to more approved headcount, and after a delay to more people who can take tickets, which leaves open tickets lower than they would have been. A rise in tickets comes back as a fall. That is balancing, and it is late. The delay is why the team feels the overtime loop this month.

The map does not choose the staffing plan. It shows why "work harder this week" strengthens the reinforcing loop while the balancing loop is still on its delay. If that pattern is familiar, start from [why the same business problems keep coming back](/blog/why-the-same-business-problems-keep-coming-back).

## When to skip a causal loop diagram

Skip it for a purchase with no later effect, for a room that will only accept blame labels, or when you need a number tomorrow. The diagram has no stocks, no units, and no time step. Quantities over time need a stock-and-flow model. The loops show which quantities matter before you build that.

Paper is enough for a first hour. Software matters when the diagram has to survive later meetings. The comparison is the [software guide](/blog/causal-loop-diagram-software). The buying questions are the [systems thinking tool checklist](/blog/systems-thinking-tool-checklist).

A flowchart orders steps ("and then"). Here the nodes are quantities. A mind map collects associations and says nothing about whether one factor ends higher or lower than it would have been.

## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is a causal loop diagram the same as a flowchart?</h3>
  <p>No. A flowchart orders steps. A causal loop diagram says how factors move each other, including paths that return to an earlier factor.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Does a plus sign mean both numbers rise?</h3>
  <p>It means the effect is higher than it would have been without that cause. Population can still fall while births are a plus link into it, if deaths are larger.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>What is the difference between polarity and loop type?</h3>
  <p>Polarity sits on one link. Reinforcing or balancing is what you get after you trace every link back to the start.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Do I need simulation software?</h3>
  <p>You can agree the structure without one. Add a simulation when you need sizes and time steps, after you can say the loops aloud.</p>
</div>
