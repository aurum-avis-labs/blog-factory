Misalignment is often incompatible stories, not incompatible people. A **shared causal map** gives stakeholders one diagram, shared variable names, and visible loops so the debate moves from anecdotes to structure. You can see which claim is about a link, which is about a delay, and which is a different goal wearing the costume of a fact.

This is for cross-functional decisions: product and operations, a ministry and its delivery agency, a research team and the people who live with the finding. It assumes you will meet more than once. A single poster that nobody may edit is a communications piece, not alignment.

## What the map has to satisfy

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>One outcome sentence everyone can point at, with a date for the decision</li>
  <li>Variable names that describe a quantity or a condition, not a villain</li>
  <li>At least two named loops, one reinforcing and one balancing if both are in play</li>
  <li>Delay notes on the links people already fight about</li>
  <li>One test or decision attached, with an owner</li>
</ul>

If any line fails, stop the drawing and fix the contract. Teams that skip the outcome sentence produce a diagram that proves everyone's prior story. Teams that allow blame labels ("lazy suppliers," "hostile press") turn the session into a trial. Rename to something you could measure or observe: supplier lead time, coverage volume, appeal rate.

Two named loops are the minimum because a single chain is an anecdote. The second loop is usually where the other function's objection lives.

## Facilitation when seniority is mixed

Executives define the outcome and the decision date. They do not draw every arrow. Operators and analysts supply the links, because they see the delays. Skeptics mark dotted alternate polarities instead of being asked to "get on board."

A workable sequence:

1. Read the outcome sentence. Strike words that smuggle a solution ("by automating," "by tightening").
2. Each function adds variables in its own language. The facilitator merges duplicates in the room, out loud.
3. Draw links only where someone can tell the story of the arrow. No story, no arrow.
4. Close loops and name them in plain language the operators would repeat.
5. Park disputed polarities as dotted hypotheses with a name on the doubt.

Do not let the most senior person narrate the whole map. Their story will be coherent and missing the delays that sit in the floor process. Do not let the session become a software demo. The tool comes after the contract holds.

## Governance: who may edit what

Alignment dies when the map changes overnight and nobody knows why. Assign three roles before you leave the room:

- **Map owner.** One person who publishes the current version and refuses unnamed edits.
- **Link owners.** For the few edges that would change the decision if the sign flipped. They bring evidence, not vibes.
- **A version note** whenever incentives, legislation, or the org chart change. One line: what moved, and which loop it touches.

Everyone else may comment. Comments are not arrows. If a comment deserves an arrow, the map owner adds it in the next session with the story attached.

## What a shared map will not do

Maps do not replace politics, authority, or a bad goal. They make the trade-off speakable: which loop each stakeholder is protecting, and what happens to the other loop if that one wins.

If two parties want different outcomes, a diagram of "the system" will launder the conflict. Name both outcomes. Decide which one this map serves. The other can have its own map, later.

If someone can veto the conclusion but will not sit in the variable list, you do not have a shared map. You have a briefing they may ignore. Either they send a delegate who can mark dotted links, or you shrink the decision to the group that will actually use the diagram.

## The pre-read that saves the meeting

Send a one-page variable list two days before the session. Ask each function to veto names that accuse a person or a group, and to add omissions. Collect this in writing. In the room, start from the revised list. You will spend the hour on links, which is the part that does not survive email.

If the list comes back as solutions ("install tool X") rather than factors ("time to reopen a ticket"), send it back. You are not in the mapping session yet.

## After the map holds

A shared diagram that nobody takes to the decision is a social event. When the names are stable and the dotted links have owners, turn it into an [executive brief from a causal loop map](/blog/executive-brief-from-causal-loop-map): outcome, dominating loop, the lever you will test, the signal, the date. Leave the full arrow set in the working file.

## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <p class="blog-faq-q">How is a shared causal map different from a RACI?</p>
  <p>A RACI assigns tasks and roles. A causal map records claims about why metrics move, including delays and loops. You can have both. The RACI does not tell you which story of the system you are executing.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <p class="blog-faq-q">How many people should be in the session?</p>
  <p>Small enough that every function can narrate one link. If you need more than about eight voices, split by function for the variable list, then merge with one delegate each. A large room produces slogans.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <p class="blog-faq-q">What if a powerful stakeholder rejects the map?</p>
  <p>Ask which link or outcome they reject. A rejected link becomes a dotted hypothesis with their name on the doubt. A rejected outcome means you do not share a decision. Drawing more arrows will not fix that.</p>
</div>
