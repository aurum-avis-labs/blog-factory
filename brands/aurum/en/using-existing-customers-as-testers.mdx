## Using existing customers to test a product

Using existing customers to test a product works when you need real workflows, honest friction, and a short path to the first session. You pick a narrow slice of people who already trust you, give them a clear job to finish in the prototype, and record what they do, not what they say to be kind. That sequence can show whether the core path is usable and whether the problem still hurts enough to change behaviour. It does not show whether strangers will sign up, pay, or stay without your name on the invoice.

The reader here is a Swiss SME or an innovation lead inside a firm that already serves clients and wants to try a new app, portal, or add-on without pretending the whole base is a representative sample. If you are validating entirely outside your current market, start with cold outreach or paid tests instead. For the internal politics of a new idea, see [validate a product idea inside a company](/blog/validate-a-product-idea-inside-a-company). For how a gate-heavy process compares with faster loops, see [stage-gate vs lean startup](/blog/stage-gate-vs-lean-startup).

## What to ask customers to do

Treat the test like a task, not a survey about feelings. Write one sentence: "Finish X without calling us." Everything else is optional until that path works.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Define the cohort</h3><p>Choose 8 to 15 accounts that look like the future buyer, not the loudest fans. Mix sizes if you sell B2B. Exclude anyone who owes you a favour this quarter.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Separate the experience</h3><p>Use a subdomain, a test login, or a build that cannot break live data. Say plainly that features may disappear.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Book a short kick-off</h3><p>Twenty minutes on Teams is enough: show the job, agree how they report bugs, and when the window closes.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Observe behaviour</h3><p>Session recordings and a simple event list beat a long questionnaire. Note where people stop, not only what they praise.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Close with a decision</h3><p>After the window, rank blockers. One pivot or one cut is a valid outcome. A list of "nice ideas" without a ranked blocker is not.</p></li>
</ol>

## What existing customers can prove

They can show whether the workflow matches how they already work, whether wording in German or French confuses them, and whether the first version is safe to put beside live operations with guardrails. They often surface integration points you forgot: export formats, approval steps, or who actually clicks the button.

They can also tell you early if the idea should stay internal. If nobody schedules the second session unless you chase, that is data.

## What polite feedback does not prove

Customers who already pay you have reasons to be gentle. They may not tell you the price is wrong, that a competitor is simpler, or that they only joined because of a relationship discount. A successful beta with ten friendly accounts does not replace a test where people discover you without an introduction.

  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>A customer beta shows usability and fit with people who already know you. It does not prove market size, repeatable acquisition, or willingness to pay at a cold price. Plan a second test with strangers or paid traffic before you treat the result as a board-ready Go.</p>
</aside>

## A made-up example: a supplier portal on a subdomain

A made-up example: a trading firm with 40 active buyers, not a client. Leadership wants a self-service portal for repeat orders. They invite nine buyers who place orders at least monthly, plus three who stopped ordering last year. The test runs on `portal-test.example.ch` for three weeks. Kick-off calls happen in week one. Support stays available by email, but nobody walks through the click path live unless a session fails in the recording.

By week three, six of the nine active buyers complete a repeat order without email. Two stall on address validation. The three lapsed buyers never log in. The team drops the address step to a manual confirm for v1 and stops calling the lapsed group "validation". They schedule a small Google Ads test to strangers for the same job before they ask the board for a full build budget.

The example is invented. The lesson is behavioural: completion among willing accounts, silence among others, and a named next test that is not more of the same cohort.

## Contracts, data, and the main product

Even a beta touches personal data if logins identify people. Use the same retention and access rules you would use in production, or looser only if your adviser agrees in writing. Do not paste production customer records into a sandbox without a purpose and a delete date.

If staff build the prototype during paid hours, employer rules on software ownership still apply in Switzerland. If an agency ships the code, you want assignment in the contract before testers arrive. Ideas themselves are not protectable; execution and brand are. That is why a controlled beta is useful, not because secrecy replaces a second test.

## When to bring in a build partner

Customer sessions expose whether the path is right. They do not remove the need for a deployable app, analytics from day one, and a plan for what happens after the beta window. If your team cannot ship an isolated build in weeks, or if every change needs a committee, outsourcing the first slice is a partner question, not a favour from IT. The checklist for that decision is in [getting an MVP built for you](/blog/getting-an-mvp-built-for-you).

## When MVP validation fits

[MVP validation](/services/mvp-validation) fits when you already know which customer job you want to observe and you need a real web app, measurement, and a structured Go, Pivot, or Stop recommendation after paid tests, not only friendly sessions. It is a poor fit if you only want opinions from accounts you already serve and you are not willing to run a second loop with colder traffic. Book a discovery conversation when the beta proved friction but not whether strangers will pay.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How many customers are enough for a first test?</h3>
  <p>Eight to fifteen active accounts who match the target role is a workable band. Fewer than five and one vocal person steers the roadmap. More than twenty without analytics and you drown in anecdote.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should you pay customers to test?</h3>
  <p>A small thank-you is fine when it is declared and does not depend on positive wording. Paying for praise corrupts the signal. Better: early access they actually need, or a credit on the live product once it ships.</p>
</div>
