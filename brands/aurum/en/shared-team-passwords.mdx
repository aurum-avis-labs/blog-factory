## Shared team passwords: why one login hurts Swiss SMEs

A shared team password for the shop till, the info@ mailbox, or the Instagram business account means you cannot tell who changed a setting, who sent a mail, or who clicked a link. After someone leaves, the secret often lives on a sticky note and in several personal password managers. That weakens accountability and organisational measures under [Art. 8 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de).

This page is for owners and team leads in Swiss firms with about 20 to 250 staff who know "everyone uses the same login" but have not fixed it yet. A full password policy template is a different article ([password policy for a small business](/security/blog/password-policy-for-a-small-business)); here the focus is the shared secret and what you can do this week.

## What "shared team password" usually means

It is one login several people know and use: a POS user at the till, admin access to the web shop, the owner password for social media, or the info@ password instead of delegated access. A shared mailbox opened with personal accounts is not the same as "Marketing2024" in a Teams chat. Both can hurt, but the fix differs.

## Where sharing sneaks in

Retail keeps one POS user because shift change is fast. Marketing shares social credentials "just for the campaign". Support shares info@ because licensing felt expensive. A family firm gives several people the admin password "so IT is simple". Each shortcut removes logs tied to a person.

## Accountability, investigations and insurance

When finance asks "who approved this payment mail", shared logins produce silence. Insurers and customer audits ask the same after incidents. Individual accounts or delegated access with logging make answers possible without blaming the wrong person. That is not legal advice, but it saves hours when you must document an incident.

## Personal data in a shared mailbox

A shared mailbox holds customer mail. If someone forwards a thread to the wrong client, you cannot trace who did it. That matters for breach documentation and for reporting high-risk incidents "as soon as possible" to the FDPIC. [Microsoft 365](/security/blog/microsoft-365-and-swiss-data-protection) shared mailboxes with personal admin accounts are the usual pattern instead of one shared password.

## Shared password or company vault?

A [password manager for the business](/security/blog/password-manager-for-a-business) with roles beats a secret in chat, but it does not replace individual application accounts everywhere. The target remains: each person has their own login, MFA where possible, and only documented exceptions with an expiry date. The vault is the bridge when the app cannot do multi-user.

## What to do instead without a big project

Use individual accounts with shared mailboxes or aliases where the platform allows. For social tools, use business-manager roles, not one password in chat. For legacy apps with no roles, rotate after departures, store secrets in a company vault, not WhatsApp. Write exceptions down: owner, reason, review date.

## Phishing, forwarding and US tools

One click on info@ affects everyone who knows the password. Afterwards rotate the password and review forwarding rules. Train staff on [reporting paths](/security/blog/clicked-a-phishing-link-at-work). When you fix shared mail passwords, [check mailbox forwarding rules](/security/blog/check-mailbox-forwarding-rules) the same week; attackers often combine both.

If the shared login is US-hosted SaaS, include [transfers to the United States](/security/blog/transferring-personal-data-to-the-united-states) in processor documentation. For suppliers in critical roles, [NIS2 for Swiss suppliers](/security/blog/nis2-for-swiss-suppliers) helps you place the risk, even when the reporting duty does not apply to most SMEs.

## Policy wording without a template article

Write internally: "No shared passwords except documented exceptions with an expiry date." Each exception needs an owner and next review date in the same folder as IT rules, so insurer questionnaires and customer audits get the same answer.

## Legacy app with only one user

If the application stays single-user for now, isolate access: jump host or remote desktop with personal Windows login, session logging if risk requires it. Record owner and rotation date in the inventory. Plan replacement with multi-user or API in procurement instead of extending the shared secret.

## SSO and shared passwords

Single sign-on through Microsoft or an IdP does not automatically replace a shared shop password outside the tenant. List logins that bypass SSO: social owner, till, franking machine, old web admin. That list is often shorter than expected and worth tackling first. Teams on SSO everywhere with audit logs, and no shared logins touching mail, finance, or customer data, can relax this week; everyone else starts with the inventory.

Keep shared logins in one table: system, owner, last rotation, MFA yes or no. The offboarding procedure states which shared secrets rotate and who tells the team. A new password never belongs in a Teams chat or on paper at the screen.
