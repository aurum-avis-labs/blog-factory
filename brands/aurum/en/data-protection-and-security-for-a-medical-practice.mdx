## Data protection and security for a medical practice

Data protection and security for a medical practice start with health data and professional secrecy, not with a generic SME checklist. Patient names on the reception screen, lab results in mail, dictation apps, and photos of wounds on a private phone are all in scope under the [FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de). You still need appropriate technical and organisational measures ([Art. 8](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)); training does not replace access control or processor contracts.

This is for physicians, practice managers, and administrators in group or solo practices in Switzerland with a handful to a few dozen staff. It is orientation, not legal or medical advice.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Lock screens on every workstation in treatment and reception areas</li>
  <li>Separate accounts; no shared login to the practice information system</li>
  <li>Processing agreements for hosting, backup, transcription, and billing partners</li>
  <li>Clear rule: no patient photos on private phones without a documented exception</li>
  <li>Incident path if mail with diagnoses goes to the wrong address</li>
  <li>Annual refresh on phishing because billing fraud targets practices too</li>
</ul>

## Cases that fit a practice, not every office

A locum doctor opens yesterday's patient list on the monitor while a visitor waits at reception; the list stays visible. A hospital partner sends discharge letters by mail without encryption because "we always did it this way". A physiotherapy admin exports the week's appointments to Excel for planning and leaves the file on a home laptop. A ransomware note appears on the server that holds imaging links; nobody knows when backups were last restored. Each case mixes Art. 8 security with professional duties and possible breach notification when high risk to patients is likely.

## Security overlaps

Phishing mail pretending to be an insurer or cantonal authority still lands in practice mailboxes. Shared passwords for the booking tool undo good server security. Review [when to sign processor agreements](/security/blog/data-processing-agreement-under-the-fadp) before the next cloud add-on.

## Waiting room and reception

Screens visible from the public area should not show full patient lists. Appointment software with sound alerts can call names loudly; consider discrete displays. Sign-in sheets on paper belong in a locked drawer the same day, not on a clipboard overnight. Couriers and sales reps should not photograph the whiteboard schedule.

## Professional secrecy and the FADP together

Medical confidentiality and data protection overlap but are not identical. A breach notification to the FDPIC may be required when loss of health data creates high risk to patients, even when professional rules already require internal reporting. Document both paths in a one-page playbook: who calls the insurer, who calls IT, who decides on external notification.

## IT partner and hosting

Your practice software vendor should appear on the processor list with a signed addendum. Ask where backups are restored in a test. If the partner offers "free" remote monitoring, clarify what they can see on screens. Before a patient portal goes live, run the screen in [when a DPIA is required](/security/blog/when-a-dpia-is-required-in-switzerland) and align clinical leads with admin.

## Training rhythm

New assistants need induction on mail, photos, and USB rules on day one. Annual refresh can combine with security topics: phishing pretending to be insurers, invoice fraud to suppliers of medical consumables, and lost devices. Many firms under 250 staff in [Deloitte's sample](https://www.allnews.ch/sites/default/files/ckfinder/userfiles/Deloitte%20Cyber%20Studie%202026_0.pdf) train regularly; use that rhythm for clinical quality and data handling without promising audit certificates.

## Who should not overbuild

A solo physician with one assistant and paper records may need only written rules and a single IT contact. Complexity grows with imaging volume, multi-site groups, and external billing services. Match effort to how many digital copies of the same record exist.

## A made-up example: lab results to the wrong patient

A made-up example: a group practice with 18 staff. The lab clerk attaches PDF results to a patient mail but picks the homonym from the address book. The recipient replies "that is not me". Reception stops further sends, IT reviews outbound mail rather than locking the account blindly, and the lead physician documents time, affected people, and whether local copies exist. Counsel clarifies FDPIC notification and patient information. The next day the team channel carries a short rule: "when in doubt on recipient, pause and check the number in the PIS", without naming the clerk in the subject line.

## Devices, USB, and home access

USB sticks from suppliers or conferences should not go into treatment-room PCs when policy forbids it. Home access with patient data runs through approved VPN or terminal services, not private Dropbox folders. Lost phone with mail app: remote wipe and password reset the same day, logged in the incident file.

## What to do first

Walk reception and treatment rooms once: screens, papers, and who can hear calls. Align with your IT partner on backup restore test and admin accounts. Document who reports misaddressed mail and when the lead physician decides next steps. Marketing or teaching photos need written consent, separate from clinical records.

## Billing and external partners

Billing bureaus and transcription firms often see more data than assistants in the room. Each partner needs a processor agreement, access limited to required fields, and clear deletion for working copies. If a partner offers "free" analytics, check whether patient data feed statistics.

## A seven-day start

Day one: check lock screen and full-disk encryption on every device with patient access. Day two: list cloud services that hold health data; request missing processor agreements. Day three: walk reception visibility and paper storage. Day four: short call with IT on backup restore test. Day five: document internal path for misaddressed mail. Day six: compare website photo consents with old gallery pages. Day seven: book combined training or run an internal 30-minute rules session. Visible progress matters for patient trust and insurer questions.
