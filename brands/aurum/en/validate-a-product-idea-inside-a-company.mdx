## Validate a new product idea inside a company

You validate a new product idea inside a company by naming the decision the board must make, choosing the riskiest assumption behind that decision, and running the smallest test that can falsify it with outsiders or paying behaviour, not only with internal applause. The output is a short evidence pack: what you tried, what happened, what you would do next, and what the data still cannot prove. A slide that says "customers want this" without a measure is not validation. It is an unproved assumption.

This is for innovation leads, product owners, and general managers in Swiss firms who must show a test before a full programme budget, not for a hobby project or a tender for extra developers. How stage-gate and lean loops differ in that conversation is a separate topic. The budget conversation itself is its own topic. Choosing who builds once the test needs software is a separate decision, covered in [getting an MVP built for you](/blog/getting-an-mvp-built-for-you).

## How to validate a new product idea inside a company

Internal ideas fail when the first deliverable is a roadmap. Validation starts earlier: one sentence on the customer job, one assumption that would kill the idea if wrong, and a stop rule written before anyone books a workshop room.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Write the board decision in one line</h3><p>Examples: fund a pilot, stop quietly, or change the segment. If the line is "build the platform", rewrite it until it names a go, pivot, or stop.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Pick the riskiest assumption</h3><p>Not "people need software". Prefer "operations managers will change how they approve orders" or "three suppliers will upload price lists without a procurement mandate".</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Choose a test shape that can fail</h3><p>Interviews alone rarely falsify willingness to act. Pair them with a concierge step, a paid pilot, a waitlist with friction, or a thin product strangers can use.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Run the test with people you do not manage</h3><p>Colleagues are useful for usability, not for demand. Include at least one channel where a polite "yes" costs time or money.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Publish the evidence pack</h3><p>Hypothesis, method, sample, primary metric, result, and limits. Link raw notes in an appendix. The board should see the falsifier, not only the highlight reel.</p></li>
</ol>

## What internal validation can and cannot show

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>Reasonable to infer</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Whether a defined segment will take a specific next step under real friction</li>
      <li>Which objection appears often enough to change the offer or the channel</li>
      <li>Whether IT, legal, or procurement blocks the idea before engineering starts</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Not proven by one internal test</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Repeatable revenue, margin, or a sales cycle you have not measured</li>
      <li>That the idea is protectable: ideas as such cannot be protected ([IGE on ideas](https://www.ige.ch/de/uebersicht-geistiges-eigentum/ein-leitfaden-fuer-innovative-und-kreative/idee/koennen-ideen-geschuetzt-werden))</li>
      <li>That a vendor-built prototype automatically belongs to the firm without a contract: employee work can sit with the employer under Swiss copyright rules, but external authors keep rights until assignment is written ([software copyright overview](https://www.rentschpartner.ch/ict-law/schutz-von-software))</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>A successful internal pilot can still be a dead product in the market. Stop when the only participants are employees, when the metric is attendance, or when you have no written falsifier and every outcome becomes "iterate".</p>
</aside>

## A made-up example: supplier uploads before a build request

A made-up example: an innovation lead in a Swiss industrial firm with about 180 staff hears that buyers retype supplier PDFs into the ERP. The board decision is whether to fund a six-month build. Before the request, the lead writes: "If fewer than four of ten invited suppliers upload a price list within two weeks when we handle onboarding by phone, we do not fund engineering." They run concierge onboarding for ten suppliers drawn from existing relationships, not from the innovation team’s friends list. Six upload; three refuse because their IT policy blocks external portals; one uploads the wrong file twice. The evidence pack shows the count, the refusal reasons, and a note that procurement has no rule yet for third-party upload tools. The recommendation is a pivot to email-based intake with a human check, and a smaller budget to test whether buyers will use corrected data. The board gets the falsifier result, not a mock-up tour.

## Who should not run a formal validation sprint

<ul class="blog-scenarios">
  <li><h3>If strategy already chose the product</h3><p>Validation becomes theatre. Either reopen the strategy question or run an honest post-launch review instead of a fake test.</p></li>
  <li><h3>If IT will not allow a pilot environment</h3><p>Fix access, data handling, and a named owner before you promise dates. A test that cannot be deployed is a slide deck.</p></li>
  <li><h3>If the only success measure is "engagement"</h3><p>Without a behavioural threshold tied to the decision, you will not know when to stop. Write the threshold while the budget is still zero.</p></li>
</ul>

Funding hints are optional context, not a validation method. [Innosuisse Initial Coaching](https://www.innosuisse.admin.ch/de/initial-coaching-fur-start-up) offers a voucher up to CHF 10'000 for eligible start-ups with an innovative, science-based idea; internal corporate teams usually need a different path, but the same discipline applies: evidence before scale.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is a survey enough to validate a product idea inside a company?</h3>
  <p>It shows stated interest, not behaviour. Use surveys to sharpen questions, then run a test where a "yes" costs time, data, or money.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How is this different from a classic stage-gate process?</h3>
  <p>Stage-gate spreads risk across phases with governance checkpoints. Lean validation tries to kill the riskiest assumption early with a small experiment. Many firms blend both; the argument on the board is which gate needs data, not which label wins.</p>
</div>
