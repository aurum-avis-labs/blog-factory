<p class="blog-lede">A clickable prototype is enough when the open question is whether people understand the flow, can complete a task on screen, or will back a direction in a room, not whether they will return to working software next week.</p>

## Clickable prototype, when enough

You do not need a deployed product to learn that a buyer misreads step three, or that two roles need different entry points. You do need one when the riskiest assumption is retention, data you store, payment, or whether strangers finish the job without you in the room. A clickable prototype is linked screens with realistic copy and fake data. Nothing persists. Nothing sends email. That is a feature while you are cheaply wrong about layout, and a ceiling once behaviour is the test. A proof of concept is a different artefact: it tests whether an API or a model can be connected at all, not whether anyone wants the product. For how an MVP differs from lighter artefacts, see [what is an MVP](/blog/what-is-an-mvp).

## What it can answer

Use a prototype when stakeholders must agree on scope before money and calendar are committed. Innovation leads often need something the board can tap through in ten minutes, not a slide that hides missing steps. Founders use the same artefact in early interviews when the goal is "show me where you would stop" rather than "use this for a week".

It also fits when usability is the hypothesis: label order, number of steps, whether mobile layout reads at arm's length. You watch where people hesitate. You rewrite copy the same afternoon. You are not yet asking whether the segment pays.

## A practical sequence (without pretending it is software)

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Write one scenario</h3><p>One persona, one job, start and end state. If you need three scenarios, you may already be scoping an MVP.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Draw only the screens on that path</h3><p>Five to nine linked frames usually suffice. Empty states and error states count; settings pages do not, unless settings are the product.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Use believable fake data</h3><p>Names, amounts, and statuses that match Swiss context where it matters. "CHF 1,200" reads differently from "$99".</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Run three sessions</h3><p>Think aloud, no defending the design. Note where they ask "what happens next" because the prototype is silent.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Decide the next artefact</h3><p>Another prototype round, a fake door on a landing page, or a narrow deployed build. Do not extend the prototype until the question is still about screens.</p></li>
</ol>

## A made-up example: board slot on a Thursday

A made-up example: an innovation lead at a family-owned logistics firm, not a client. The board gave twenty minutes in June to show a customer portal idea: upload a delivery note, see status, request a change. No budget for engineering yet. In ten days she linked seven screens in a design tool with fake shipment numbers and Swiss addresses. Two internal users and one friendly customer operations manager clicked through on a laptop. All three reached "request change" but two asked whether email would fire, which the prototype could not show. The lead noted that as a behaviour question, not a layout fix. She kept the prototype for alignment and scheduled a fake door landing page for strangers, still without a backend. She did not claim validation or product-market fit in the board paper. She stated what the taps proved (flow clarity) and what they did not (notification behaviour).

## Clickable prototype, when enough stops being enough

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>A prototype does not prove retention, pricing power, operational cost, or security posture. It does not show whether your stack survives real load. It does not protect the idea: ideas as such cannot be protected, only execution and brand can (<a href="https://www.ige.ch/de/uebersicht-geistiges-eigentum/ein-leitfaden-fuer-innovative-und-kreative/idee/koennen-ideen-geschuetzt-werden">IGE on ideas</a>). A wrapped mobile app still has to do more than replay the same screens: Apple’s guideline 4.2 rejects pure repackaged websites (<a href="https://developer.apple.com/app-store/review/guidelines/">App Store Review Guidelines</a>).</p>
</aside>

Stop extending the prototype when every new question starts with "after they sign up" or "when the data is real". That is the line where a deployed minimum product, analytics, and honest drop-off matter. Vibe-coded shells with a fake checkout are still demand tests, not behaviour tests, no matter how polished the clicks look.

## Common questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is a clickable prototype the same as an MVP?</h3>
  <p>No. An MVP is deployed software narrow enough that users can complete the core path and you can measure what they do. A prototype teaches layout and language, not habit.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How long should we spend on it?</h3>
  <p>Long enough to test one scenario with a handful of sessions, not long enough to polish every edge case. If you are past two weeks without new learning, the artefact is probably wrong.</p>
</div>
