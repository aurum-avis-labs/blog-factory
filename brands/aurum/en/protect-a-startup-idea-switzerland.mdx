<p class="blog-lede">You cannot protect a startup idea as such in Switzerland. You can protect how you express it, the brand you trade under, inventions that meet patent rules, and confidential detail you keep out of public reach through contracts and discipline.</p>

## Can I protect my startup idea in Switzerland?

The Swiss Institute of Intellectual Property is explicit: [ideas as such cannot be protected](https://www.ige.ch/de/uebersicht-geistiges-eigentum/ein-leitfaden-fuer-innovative-und-kreative/idee/koennen-ideen-geschuetzt-werden). Telling someone you will build "Uber for dog walkers" does not give you a monopoly on that sentence. What follows is which assets founders actually control, what a non-disclosure agreement changes, and when obsession with secrecy slows validation more than it helps.

This is for founders with an idea or pre-seed project, often without a technical co-founder, who worry that pitching, hiring, or shipping will let someone copy them. It is not legal advice. For how software engagements are structured locally, see [startup software development in Switzerland](/blog/startup-software-development-switzerland). For privacy when you start building, the companion pieces are [privacy requirements for a Swiss MVP](/blog/privacy-requirements-swiss-mvp) and [App Store privacy labels](/blog/app-store-privacy-labels).

## What you can protect instead of the idea

<ul class="blog-facts">
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Copyright</span><strong>Code and content</strong><p>Your source, your wording, your visual assets. Others may build a similar product; they may not copy your files.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Trademark</span><strong>Name and sign</strong><p>A registered mark can stop confusing use of your brand in the classes you filed. It does not lock the underlying concept.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Patent</span><strong>Novel inventions</strong><p>Only where statutory criteria are met. Most software-led startups lean on copyright and speed, not a patent portfolio on day one.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Contract</span><strong>NDA and assignment</strong><p>Confidentiality clauses and written transfer of rights to contractors. Paper does not replace clear ownership in the repo.</p></li>
</ul>

A trademark filing in Switzerland starts at CHF 450 for one class for ten years, with renewal at CHF 550, on the current [IGE trademark FAQ](https://www.ige.ch/de/etwas-schuetzen/marken/faq). Recheck fees before you budget. Registering a name is sensible when you are committed to the brand; it is not a substitute for learning whether anyone wants the product.

## NDAs: what they do and what they do not

A mutual or one-way non-disclosure agreement can make it a breach of contract to share defined confidential information. It does not stop another team from independently building a similar offer if they never received your materials. It does not protect an idea you already posted on LinkedIn, entered in a public hackathon, or described in a pitch deck sent without restrictions.

Use an NDA when you share detailed roadmaps, unreleased designs, or pricing models with advisors, agencies, or early hires. Skip treating it as a permission slip to avoid customer conversations. The usual failure mode is months of "stealth" while a competitor ships a mediocre version and learns in public.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>An NDA is a contract between named parties. It is not registration of your idea at the state. It does not prove originality, and it does not replace copyright on code or a trademark on the name customers see.</p>
</aside>

## Who owns the software you pay for

Swiss copyright law treats employee and contractor work differently. Software created by an employee in the performance of their duties generally belongs to the employer ([Art. 17 URG](https://www.rentschpartner.ch/ict-law/schutz-von-software), as summarised by practitioners). An external author keeps copyright until a written assignment passes it to you. A handshake or a line in an email is a weak place to discover ownership during due diligence.

Before a freelancer or studio ships production code, agree in writing who owns the repository, the designs, and the data exports. The contract chain from subcontractor to repository is worked through in [who owns the code when an agency builds it](/blog/who-owns-the-code). The product validation package Aurum runs ends with the customer owning code, designs, data, and documentation; that pattern should be normal in any paid build, not a surprise at handover.

## Who should worry about protection, and who should skip the spiral

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>When protection work pays off</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>You are about to share detailed specs with contractors or a co-founder candidate</li>
      <li>You have a distinctive brand name you will put on invoices and the App Store</li>
      <li>You rely on a process or dataset that is hard to recreate and not yet public</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Who should skip heavy legal theatre first</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Founders who have not spoken to ten potential buyers because the deck is "not ready"</li>
      <li>Ideas that differ only by slogan while the workflow is commodity software</li>
      <li>Teams that want a patent before a single paid pilot</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

<p class="blog-pull">Secrecy protects detail you have not yet tested; it does not prove the market wants what you will not describe.</p>

Competitors can copy features. They copy slower when you learn faster: interviews, a narrow MVP, clear analytics.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Does copyright stop someone from building the same type of app?</h3>
  <p>No. Copyright stops copying your expression (code, assets, long-form copy). Independent implementation of a similar idea is normal. Your defence is execution, data, distribution, and brand trust.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should I file a trademark before I validate demand?</h3>
  <p>Many founders file once the name is stable and they are willing to defend it. Filing before you know the segment can lock you into wording you later pivot away from. Balance cost against how public the brand already is.</p>
</div>
