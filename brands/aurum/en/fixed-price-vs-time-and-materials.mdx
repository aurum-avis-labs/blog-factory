## Fixed price vs time and materials

**Fixed price vs time and materials** is not a question of which label sounds safer. It is who carries the cost when the brief was incomplete, when the market answer arrives mid-build, or when integration turns out to be harder than the slide deck implied. A fixed-price contract pays a stated sum for a stated deliverable. Time and materials bills hours or days at agreed rates against work performed. Hybrid models appear often in Switzerland: a fixed discovery phase, then time and materials for build, or a fixed price per milestone with a written change process.

This is for founders and innovation leads comparing quotes from agencies, freelancers, and studios before they sign. It is not a template contract. When the numbers are large or equity is involved, involve counsel. How Swiss quotes differ by partner type sits in [MVP cost in Switzerland](/blog/mvp-cost-switzerland). Whether you pay cash, equity, or a mix is a separate conversation, in [paying a development partner in equity](/blog/paying-a-partner-in-equity).

## Fixed price vs time and materials: who owns the surprise

Under fixed price, the vendor prices unknowns into the quote or narrows scope until the unknowns disappear. That is why vague briefs produce either padded numbers or change-order friction later. You get predictability on the invoice line if the statement of work matches reality. You do not get unlimited pivots.

Under time and materials, surprise shows up as hours. Transparency on timesheets does not automatically mean control: without a product owner who can cut scope, spend drifts while everyone stays busy. The model suits learning loops; it punishes passive buyers.

| Topic | Fixed price | Time and materials |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Budget cap | Agreed total if scope holds | Open until you pause or cap hours |
| Scope change | Change order, often re-priced | Absorbed in the next invoice |
| Vendor risk on underestimation | Mostly vendor | Mostly you |
| Your risk on shifting priorities | Change orders or deferrals | Directly on the meter |
| Best early signal | Signed acceptance criteria | Weekly demo plus burn report |

Neither model proves the product will find customers. Both can ship working software that nobody needs.

## What to put in writing before money moves

Regardless of label, the same clauses decide whether the engagement ends cleanly.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Define the unit of delivery</h3><p>Milestone, release tag, or hours bucket. «MVP» without a feature list is not a unit.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Name the change path</h3><p>Who approves scope changes, in what form, and whether fixed price resets or T&amp;M continues.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Set reporting rhythm</h3><p>Weekly written status for T&amp;M; milestone demo and sign-off for fixed price.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Tie payment to evidence</h3><p>Repository access, environments you control, and documentation listed in the brief, not only an invoice PDF.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Separate validation from construction</h3><p>If the goal is to learn whether to build, price discovery and measurement explicitly. Building everything under a fixed cap without a learning plan is still a gamble.</p></li>
</ol>

Fixed-price quotes often assume you provide decisions within agreed days. Silence is treated as acceptance of the vendor’s interpretation. Time-and-materials quotes assume you will read the burn. Neither assumption is friendly if nobody owns product decisions on your side.

## A made-up example: two quotes for the same brief

A made-up example: a B2B founder in Zurich sends the same five-page brief to two vendors. Vendor A offers CHF 95'000 fixed for eight weeks, three milestones, two revision rounds per screen. Vendor B offers four senior days per week at an hourly rate, estimate twelve to sixteen weeks, no cap. The founder chooses A because the board asked for a single number. Week five, pilot users ask for a different onboarding path and an SSO integration that was «out of scope» on page two. Vendor A issues a change order. Vendor B would have absorbed the work in the next fortnight of hours. The founder pays the change order and keeps the cap mentally intact, but the calendar slips six weeks. Vendor B’s uncapped model would have cost more in francs if the founder had not stopped feature requests in week ten.

The lesson is not that A or B was dishonest. The brief treated learning as build risk. A fixed price hid it until the change order. Time and materials would have shown it on the third invoice.

## What neither model proves

A signed contract and a deployed app do not show repeatable acquisition, unit economics, or that you should keep investing. Fixed price can encourage cutting tests and analytics to protect margin. Time and materials can encourage building features because the team is booked.

If the question is «should this product exist?», commercial structure is secondary to whether measurement and distribution are in scope. Studios and some agencies bundle validation; many dev shops sell execution only. Read the statement of work for weeks after launch, not only for syntax.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is fixed price always cheaper?</h3>
  <p>No. Vendors add contingency for ambiguity. A tight brief can make fixed price efficient. A vague brief often makes time and materials cheaper early and more expensive later, or the reverse if change orders stack up.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Can we mix models?</h3>
  <p>Yes. Common pattern: fixed discovery workshop and specification, then time and materials for build, or fixed price per release with a shared backlog. Write where the switch happens.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Where does equity fit?</h3>
  <p>Equity is payment, not a pricing model. You still need scope, reporting, and handover. Cash, equity, or a blend belongs in the discovery conversation, not in a verbal side deal.</p>
</div>
