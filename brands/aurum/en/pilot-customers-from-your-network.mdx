## Pilot customers from your own network

You find **pilot customers from your own network** by naming who already lives the problem, asking for a short, specific trial instead of a favour, and writing down what you will measure before anyone logs in. The first list should be people who would use the product if you did not exist, plus one introducer who can open a door you cannot. Warm access is fast. It is also easy to misread as market proof.

This article sits after problem interviews and before you treat polite sign-ups as traction. For how pilots fit next to landing pages, concierge tests and paid traffic, see [how to validate a startup idea](/blog/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea). Here the focus is the network list, the ask, and the limits.

## Who counts as a pilot customer

A pilot customer tries the workflow you care about, on a schedule you set, and gives you evidence you could show a sceptical colleague. They are not a morale booster, a logo for a slide, or a friend who clicks every button once to be kind.

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>Who belongs on the list</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Someone who already pays in time or money to cope without your product</li>
      <li>A buyer or daily user who matches the segment you named in discovery</li>
      <li>A person willing to share a redacted export, a call notes file, or a recurring task</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Who should stay off the list</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Anyone who only wants to support you personally</li>
      <li>A senior contact who will never operate the tool</li>
      <li>Twelve peers who all share the same employer as you</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Start with twenty names, then cut half. If every remaining name shares your surname, your former employer, or your accelerator cohort, you still have a learning set, not a market sample.

## How to work the network without a vague "try my app"

Write the pilot on one page: duration, success signal, support you will give, and what happens to their data. Send that before you share a link.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>State the job, not the feature list</h3><p>"For three weeks, use this instead of the Friday spreadsheet for job handovers." One outcome, one comparison.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Ask for an intro, not a group chat</h3><p>"Who runs ordering for a firm like yours? May I use your name in the first line?"</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Book a start date and an end date</h3><p>Pilots that drift for months blend into friendship. End dates force honest feedback.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Capture behaviour, not opinions</h3><p>Did they return without a reminder? Where did they stall? What did they revert to?</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Close with a clear next step</h3><p>Paid continuation, a second team, or a written "not for us" counts. Silence does not.</p></li>
</ol>

Second-degree links often beat cold mail in Swiss B2B: alumni lists, trade associations, or a supplier who serves your target industry. You still owe the same scoped ask.

## What a network pilot can show

Network pilots surface workflow friction, missing fields, onboarding that assumes your knowledge, and the first story you can tell a stranger. They are weak at cold acquisition, price from unknown buyers, two-sided marketplace balance, and retention after social obligation fades. Early marketplace tests among people close to the team can show supply-side interest without proving that strangers will post jobs repeatedly.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>Even honest pilots from your network are biased toward yes and toward forgiveness. They can keep you from building the wrong product for imaginary users. They cannot replace a test where the visitor does not know your name.</p>
</aside>

## Who should skip network pilots for now

Skip leaning on friends when the product only makes sense for anonymous consumers, when regulatory buyers must see a vendor they found themselves, or when you already have repeat usage from people who met you online. In those cases, a landing page with a costly call to action or a small paid channel tells you more than a tenth warm login.

Also pause if you are using pilots to avoid writing down who the customer is. "SMEs in Switzerland" is not a network segment. Narrow until you can name a role and a recurring Tuesday task.

## A made-up example: inventory checks for dental practices

A made-up example: a founder sells a lightweight stock-count tool to small dental practices in the canton of Bern. She lists thirty contacts from university friends, family, and a WhatsApp group of practice managers she met at a trade fair.

She removes anyone who is not responsible for ordering consumables. Twelve names remain. She sends a two-page pilot brief: four weekly counts, fifteen minutes each, compare to the paper list they use today, she joins the first session on Teams, no integration with their practice software in this phase.

Six agree; four finish all four counts. Two stop after week one when the lead nurse is on holiday. Three of the four finishers say the paper list is still faster for emergency orders; all four ask for barcode support, which she had deprioritised. One practice offers a paid month if barcodes work on supplier catalogues. None of the six are relatives. The useful output is the stall at emergency orders and one paid extension, not "six pilots launched".

## What network pilots do not prove

They do not prove market size, paid channel payback, or marketplace liquidity without social pressure. They do not replace funnel metrics or a validation window with pivots. When the same workaround appears in several pilots and a stranger-facing test agrees, you have a stronger case for build or paid traffic.

## When the validation package fits

The [product validation package](/services/mvp-validation) fits when network pilots have shown a painful workflow and the open question is whether people you did not know last month will use deployed software and whether acquisition can be measured. Weeks one to four put a real app in front of users, not a slide. Weeks five to twelve add positioning, a landing page, paid traffic on Google and LinkedIn, and up to three pivot loops, ending in Go, Pivot or Stop with what the numbers still cannot prove.

If you are still assembling the first list of names, stay with scoped pilots and notes. Book the package when you need behaviour and channel data that warm intros cannot supply.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How many pilot customers from your network are enough?</h3>
  <p>Enough to see the same blocker twice without prompting, or to learn that your list is too homogeneous. There is no fixed count. If the tenth pilot repeats the first story, another friend login rarely adds information.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should you charge network pilots?</h3>
  <p>A small fee or a signed pilot statement filters politeness. Zero cost is fine for a very early prototype if you label the feedback as biased. Do not treat a free favour as willingness to pay retail price later.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>When should you leave the network?</h3>
  <p>When you need to test messaging, price, or signup from cold traffic, or when the product only works if strangers trust you without a mutual contact. Move to a landing page test or paid ads while keeping one or two network pilots for deep workflow notes.</p>
</div>
