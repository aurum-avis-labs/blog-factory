## When does automation pay off for a small business?

<p class="blog-lede">Automation pays off for a small business when a task comes back often enough, takes long enough and ties up enough people that the hours it eats in a year are worth more than building and running the automation.</p>

The rough formula is frequency × duration × people. Two things the formula misses can tip the decision: errors that cost money to fix, and a task that stops whenever one person is away. A bounded task that takes one to two combined hours a week is where the numbers start to work. A ten-minute job once a month almost never does.

This is for owners and office leads in a Swiss business with a handful to a few dozen people who want a threshold before they spend anything. The worked example uses guide prices from our offer. You can redo it with your own figures.

## Why "it annoys everyone" is not a threshold

Annoyance often points at the wrong task, because the loudest job is rarely the most expensive one. The month-end report that takes two painful hours feels like the obvious candidate. Three people who each spend ten minutes every morning copying web enquiries into the CRM add up to two and a half hours a week, about five times as much. Nobody complains, because each slice is small.

The other usual yardstick is usage. According to [adesso's GenAI Impact Report 2026 for Switzerland](https://www.adesso.ch/adesso-ch/whitepaper/genai-impact-report-2026-ch.pdf), 47 percent of Swiss companies measure GenAI success by process efficiency and 45 percent by usage rate. Those are adesso's figures, not ours. Usage is easy to count. It does not tell you whether a task got cheaper. A licence that everyone opens daily can leave the Monday report exactly where it was.

## The threshold: frequency × duration × people

The threshold is what the task costs by hand in a year, compared with what the automation costs. Count the three inputs rather than estimating them.

<ul class="blog-facts">
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Frequency</span><strong>Runs per month</strong><p>Counted from the last four weeks, not from memory.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Duration</span><strong>Minutes per run</strong><p>Including the questions afterwards and the corrections.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">People</span><strong>Everyone involved</strong><p>Whoever does part of it, or waits for the result.</p></li>
</ul>

Multiply them for hours per month, then per year. Multiply those by what an hour costs your business, wages plus social charges.

Set the result against two costs: the first year of the automation, and every year after. The build is paid once, running it every month.

As a rule of thumb, automation looks good when the task is weekly or daily, follows fixed rules, involves several people or causes costly corrections. It looks bad when the task is rare, different every time, or something a checklist could handle. What decides a borderline case is in the next section.

## Two costs the formula misses

Errors and dependence on one person often decide a borderline case, and neither shows up in minutes.

**Errors.** A figure in the wrong column, an invoice with last year's rate, a reminder that never went out. Each one costs the correction plus whatever it caused: a credit note, a customer on the phone, a payment that arrives late. Count how many there were last month and what the worst one cost.

**One-person dependency.** If only one person can do the task, it stops when she is on holiday or ill, and it leaves with her if she moves on. That is waiting time for everyone who needs the result, and a risk that no hour count shows. Sometimes the cheaper fix is a written instruction and a second person who has done it twice. If the task is frequent, follows fixed rules and still hangs on one person after that, it is a strong candidate.

## A worked example with guide prices

A made-up example: an engineering office with around 15 people. Every Monday the office lead builds the project-hours report from two exports, time tracking and the project list. That takes 75 minutes. Then three project leads spend about ten minutes each checking their lines and asking questions. That is 105 minutes a week.

Assume 45 working weeks a year and CHF 60 per hour including social charges. Both are assumptions for the example, so put in your own. That gives about 79 hours a year, or roughly CHF 4'700 of hand work.

The automation fits our Small package: one clearly bounded workflow with few systems, 2–3 days, guide price CHF 3'200–4'800. Hosting and operation add CHF 50–150 a month depending on the solution, so CHF 600–1'800 a year. There are no AI service costs here, because merging two exports needs rules, not a model.

| | Low end | High end |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Year one: build plus running | CHF 3'800 | CHF 6'600 |
| Each following year: running | CHF 600 | CHF 1'800 |
| Hand work avoided per year | CHF 4'700 | CHF 4'700 |

At the low end, the build is paid back within the first year. At the high end, during the second. Over two years the office avoids about CHF 9'450 of hand work for CHF 4'400–8'400 in cost, and the report no longer waits when the office lead is away. These are guide prices. The fixed price for a specific task is confirmed in writing after the Process Check.

Now change one number. If the same report ran monthly instead of weekly, it would cost about 21 hours, or CHF 1'260, a year by hand. Running costs alone would take between half of that and more than all of it, and the build would never be paid back on hours. Only errors or the holiday gap could still justify it. Frequency moves the answer more than any other input.

## When automation does not pay off

Automation does not pay off when the task is rare, different every time, unstable, or about to change anyway.

- **Rare.** Quarterly or yearly tasks, unless a single error is expensive. A checklist is cheaper.
- **Different every time.** If each run needs judgement, there is no rule to automate.
- **Unstable.** The columns change names, three people are sort of responsible, nobody can say when the task is finished. Automating it would cement the mess. First a named owner and a stable input.
- **About to change.** A new ERP next spring, a supplier moving to a portal. Wait, then automate the new process.
- **The hours have nowhere to go.** If nobody waits for the result and the person has slack, saved minutes are not saved money. In a small business what counts is whether the freed time goes into work someone pays for, or work that was being left undone.
