## Cyberattack reporting obligation Switzerland: most SMEs are outside it

The cyberattack reporting obligation in Switzerland since 1 April 2025 requires certain operators of critical infrastructure to notify the National Cyber Security Centre (BACS) within 24 hours of becoming aware of a significant incident. Energy, drinking water, transport, and parts of public administration are in scope. A typical Swiss SME with 20 to 250 staff in trade, services, or manufacturing is usually not a covered operator, even after ransomware or a serious breach. Confusion comes because voluntary BACS reports, insurance forms, and data-protection notifications use similar words. For customer data retention and deletion, see [retention periods and a deletion concept](/security/blog/retention-periods-and-a-deletion-concept).

In the second half of 2025, BACS received about 29'006 voluntary reports and 145 reports under the mandatory duty, according to BACS's [half-year report](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/hjb-25-2-de). Fraud made up about 52 per cent of reports; ransomware cases were counted separately, about 57 in the same period. That shows how small the mandatory pool is compared with everyday fraud and phishing reports.

## Who is covered and who is not

The federal rules target operators whose services society depends on at national level. If you run a regional logistics office, a law firm, or a parts supplier, you are unlikely to be a critical infrastructure operator («Betreiber kritischer Infrastrukturen») under this duty unless a sector law explicitly lists you. When in doubt, legal counsel and your industry association beat a blog article. This text is orientation, not legal advice. Official guidance is on the [BACS reporting duty page](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/informationen-zur-meldepflicht).

## What most companies should do instead

If you are not under the duty, you still contain incidents, inform your insurer, consider police reports for fraud, and run internal reporting as in [CEO fraud](/security/blog/ceo-fraud-switzerland). Voluntary information to BACS remains welcome for the national picture. Insurance forms often ask about a «cyber incident» without using the BACS definition. Keep the questions separate: statutory report yes or no, insurance claim, privacy assessment if personal data leaked.

  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>Do not tell staff that «every cyberattack must be reported to BACS in 24 hours». That misstates the law and hides the internal steps that actually protect the firm.</p>

## How this differs from FDPIC breach notification

A personal data breach may require notification to the FDPIC when likely high risk to individuals, «as soon as possible», via databreach.edoeb.admin.ch. That is data-protection law, not the BACS critical-infrastructure cyber duty. One event can trigger both assessments in a large operator; a typical SME might face only the privacy question, not a statutory BACS duty.

## Internal roles before the first serious incident

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Who decides whether an incident is «significant» (for covered operators)</li>
  <li>BACS contact and reporting path saved, not only at the IT provider</li>
  <li>Separately: who assesses FDPIC notification for data leaks</li>
  <li>Short board note: duty yes or no, not «we report everywhere»</li>
</ul>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>We had ransomware. Must we call BACS within 24 hours?</h3>
  <p>Only if you are a covered critical infrastructure operator. Most SMEs should focus on recovery, internal logging, insurer, and privacy assessment first. Voluntary BACS contact is optional.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is this the same as NIS2?</h3>
  <p>No. NIS2 targets certain suppliers and sectors in the EU chain. The Swiss BACS duty for critical infrastructure is a separate regime. Large firms may discuss both; a standard SME often only needs clarity: no CI reporting duty, but still sound internal reporting.</p>
</div>

## Voluntary reports still help the national picture

BACS collects voluntary information on fraud and intrusion attempts from businesses without a statutory duty. Those voluntary reports dominated the 2025 half-year statistics. They do not replace your internal response plan. Do not delay containment to fill a form.

For an SME without a duty, voluntary reporting can make sense when you see a fraud pattern that might hit peers in your industry. You do not need to report every failed phishing attempt. Store BACS contact details next to your insurance number, not only in IT documentation.

## What «significant» means for covered operators (briefly)

Operators under the duty must report significant disruptions. The exact test depends on sector law. This article does not go deep for energy suppliers. For a standard SME board the shorthand is enough: without a critical infrastructure role there is no 24-hour BACS duty because of office ransomware. You still run recovery, communication, and privacy steps properly.
