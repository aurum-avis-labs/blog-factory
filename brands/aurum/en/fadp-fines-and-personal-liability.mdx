## FADP fines and personal liability

FADP fines and personal liability in Switzerland focus on intentional breaches of data protection duties, not on every employee who mis-addresses an email. Swiss criminal law under the revised FADP provides for fines of up to CHF 250'000 against **natural persons** when someone acts **with intent**. Negligence, panic after an incident, or a first mistake handled openly sit in a different category from deliberate sale of customer lists or wilful ignoring of known risks.

This article is for owners and managers in a Swiss SME with roughly 20 to 250 staff who heard about personal fines after the revised FADP. It is orientation, not legal advice.

## What the FDPIC and the law say about penalties

The [FDPIC explains criminal provisions](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/strafbestimmungen) tied to [Art. 61 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de). Offences include intentional violations of professional secrecy duties, intentional failure to meet defined security obligations, and serious disregard of FDPIC orders. Prosecution lies with cantonal authorities. The FDPIC does not replace a court.

The figure CHF 250'000 is the statutory ceiling for natural persons acting with intent, not a default ticket for a lost USB stick reported and secured the same afternoon. Legal commentary continues on whether every detail in the Data Protection Ordinance creates criminal exposure if breached. Treat fines as real but stay calm in how you explain them to staff.

## Who in the company may be personally exposed

Criminal fines target a **natural person**, not the legal entity as such. Insurers and customers still ask: "Who carries the risk?"

Roles that often appear in those conversations:

- **Management and board:** budget, priorities, approval of cloud tools, and conscious decisions against known risks.
- **Part-time data protection lead or HR:** applicant and employee data, reporting paths, retention.
- **Finance:** payment data, invoices, CEO fraud processes (civil and criminal tracks can overlap; counsel clarifies).
- **IT lead or external MSP:** access control and backups, rarely sole "guilt" without instruction from management.

The "IT on the side" person often implements technology but rarely decides alone on intentional law-breaking. Personal liability discussions should not scapegoat the lowest-paid administrator for a missing group policy the board never funded.

## Intent matters

Swiss criminal law requires intent for these fines. That distinguishes a manager who knowingly keeps exporting health data to personal Gmail for years from a team lead who discovers an autocomplete error and escalates. Training, documented decisions, and quick containment support the narrative that the company takes [Art. 8 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de) seriously even when something goes wrong.

If management explicitly resolves in a meeting to ignore known FDPIC orders, the discussion shifts from negligence towards intent. Such minutes should not exist. Conversely, a written resolution on budget for encryption and training helps if someone later judges the firm's care. That is not a guarantee against prosecution, but evidence of organisational diligence.

## What an SME should not claim in conversations

On insurer questionnaires, customer audits, and internal mail we hear wording that promises more than the law delivers, or frightens staff into silence.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>"Every data protection mistake makes management criminally liable." Without intent, that is usually wrong.</li>
  <li>"The FDPIC fines us up to CHF 250'000." The ceiling applies to natural persons with intent, not as an automatic company penalty.</li>
  <li>"We are compliant after training." Training does not make anyone "compliant"; it is one measure under Art. 8.</li>
  <li>"GDPR fines always apply to us as a Swiss SME." EU administrative fines and Swiss criminal fines are different mechanisms.</li>
  <li>"We must report within 72 hours." That is GDPR language; in Switzerland you report "as soon as possible" when a high risk is likely.</li>
</ul>

Employment consequences for intentional misuse of data by staff are a separate field from FADP criminal fines; HR and counsel clarify termination and damages.

## What is not a criminal fine scenario

Reporting a breach to the FDPIC when required. Documenting an incident that stays below the notification threshold. Replacing a weak password policy after an audit. These are hygiene. Civil complaints from customers, reputational damage, and insurer exclusions are separate risks from criminal fines.

## GDPR is a different enforcement map

If the GDPR applies because you target EU residents, EU supervisory authorities can impose administrative fines on undertakings under EU rules. That is not the same mechanism as Swiss criminal fines against individuals. Swiss-only SMEs still care about GDPR when they have EU customers, but the headline CHF 250'000 figure belongs to Swiss criminal law for natural persons with intent.

## Reduce risk without fear campaigns

Agree who approves cloud tools. Keep processor agreements on file. Run data protection training so people report fast. Day-to-day duties are summarised in [Swiss FADP obligations for SMEs](/security/blog/swiss-fadp-obligations-for-smes). Sensitive categories deserve tighter access control because harm scales faster.

  <h3>Can the FDPIC fine me CHF 250'000 directly?</h3>
  <p>The FDPIC explains criminal provisions and may refer cases to cantonal prosecutors. The maximum fine of CHF 250'000 applies to natural persons with intent under Art. 61 FADP, not as a daily administrative fine on the company.</p>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Does the IT provider take the blame instead of management?</h3>
