## Measure whether an automation works

<p class="blog-lede">Four numbers before go-live and the same four after a few weeks: time per case, errors, lead time and share handled. Logins are not one of them.</p>

You judge whether an automation works by four measures you write down before it goes live, then count again with the same definitions. If time per case, errors, lead time and the share of cases the automation actually finishes did not move as planned, it has not landed yet, however often people open the tool.

The sheet you fill in before you spend anything is [how to calculate the benefit of an automation](/prozess-check/blog/calculate-automation-benefit). This page is the count afterwards. A run that fails overnight is operations, covered in [who runs the automation afterwards](/prozess-check/blog/who-runs-the-automation). What a rebuild would roughly cost is in [what automation costs in Switzerland](/prozess-check/blog/what-automation-costs-switzerland).

## Usage is not the result

Logins, prompts and tokens show the tool was touched. They do not show the work got shorter or cleaner.

<div class="blog-stat">
  <strong>45%</strong>
  <p>adesso, GenAI Impact Report Switzerland 2026: share of Swiss companies that measure GenAI success by usage rate. In the same survey, 47% name process efficiency. Their executive survey, not ours. The shares add up past 100%, so firms could name more than one measure.</p>
</div>

That report from adesso Schweiz is a February 2026 survey by HEUTE UND MORGEN of 100 Swiss executives (a separate chapter covers 750 end customers). adesso's useful line: usage measures show whether employees use GenAI, not whether it works. After usage rate (45%) and usage frequency (40%) come cost (32%), customer satisfaction (28%), revenue growth (27%) and fewer complaints (18%). Five percent do not measure at all. Treat those as adesso's findings, not targets for your firm. For recurring office work, a sheet with four numbers is often enough, not a BI project. If your decision only tracks logins, you still will not know after six weeks whether the work got shorter.

## What to write down before go-live

Count a real stretch, ideally the last four weeks, with the same definitions you will use later. Memory of "it takes ages" will not compare.

<ul>
  <li>Time per case: minutes from starting the case until it is done, including corrections and questions. Time three cases, not one.</li>
  <li>Errors: how many cases last month had to be redone, and what "redone" means.</li>
  <li>Lead time: calendar time from request to result leaving, not typing minutes.</li>
  <li>Share handled: of all cases in the period, how many left on the new path without a person rebuilding them.</li>
  <li>Who still uses the old path: names or roles. Adoption is the condition for the four numbers, not the result itself.</li>
</ul>

Write the numbers where the date stays visible: a spreadsheet row, a notebook line, an email to yourself. If you only start counting after go-live, mark the earlier figure as reconstructed from timestamps and treat it as weaker. Note who counted and which cases were excluded, or the team will argue definitions instead of results two weeks later.

## A made-up example

A made-up example: small wholesaler confirming orders by copying lines into a mail. Four weeks before go-live: about 40 confirmations a week, twelve minutes each including the check, six corrections in a month, mail leaving on average two days after the order.

Six weeks later: still about 40 a week. Checking a draft takes four minutes, two corrections in the last month, same-day sending on the new path. In a sample week, 32 of 40 left without anyone rebuilding the mail. Eight were still pasted into the old template by two people who did not trust the draft. Without the share handled you would only see shorter minutes and miss the stubborn cases.

| | Before | After six weeks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmations a week | about 40 | about 40 |
| Minutes of staff time per confirmation | 12 | 4 on the new path |
| Corrections in a month | 6 | 2 |
| Days from order to confirmation | 2 | same day, on the new path |
| Share that left without a rebuild | all manual | 32 of 40 |

The login report would have looked healthy in week one and week six. It would not have shown the eight cases that never took the new path. Those eight are the next conversation, not a reason to declare the automation finished. Minutes saved apply only to the 32. Price them with the benefit sheet if you need francs; do not invent satisfaction you did not collect. All table figures are invented for the arithmetic; your counts replace them.

## When a number stays flat

Time per case barely fell: often "check" became a full redo. That is a design problem, not a reason to celebrate the licence. Look at three cases that still take long and note which step stayed manual.

Errors unchanged: read the source, do not stack features. A wrong product code from the master list survives any new prompt. [Who runs the automation](/prozess-check/blog/who-runs-the-automation) is about failed runs; this count is about cases that completed and were still wrong.

Lead time unchanged: the wait came from approval, a batch or a Friday queue, rarely from typing. Automating typing leaves that wait in place.

Share handled stays low: you rarely have a measurement problem. People are staying on the old path. Ask what the outliers do not trust before you scale the same pattern to a second task. Write the answers next to the eight cases in the example, not only in a meeting.

## What to leave out

No dashboard for five cases a month. The checklist above is the whole system. Do not convert a feeling into a percentage. Do not treat a vendor's "hours saved" estimate as your after figure. Do not average the new path with the old and call it the result. Report the share, then minutes on each path separately. A one-off project has no weekly baseline. Judge it once against the points you wrote before the build, then stop. This page is for work that repeats.

## Common questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How long should you wait before counting again?</h3>
  <p>At least one full work cycle, often four to six weeks, so exceptions and habit show up. Earlier you measure adoption only, not effect.</p>
</div>
