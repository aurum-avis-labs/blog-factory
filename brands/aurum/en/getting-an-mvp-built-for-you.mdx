## Getting an MVP built for you

Getting an MVP built for you is a partner decision, not a trend. Most founders will not ship a full stack alone while they are also selling, raising, and hiring. Outsourcing fails when the brief is a document and the next meeting is the demo, three months later. It holds when both sides can see the same decision the product is supposed to answer, and when you can take the code with you.

This guide is about partner selection: how to tell whether someone can deliver what you need to learn from the market. ## 1. Write the decision before you collect quotes

Before prices, write three lines:

- Which decision the MVP should enable. Pricing, a product-market signal, a build an investor can try, or an internal stop.
- Which result you would accept in the first four to eight weeks after launch.
- What is out of scope for v1, in a list someone can argue with.

Worked example. A founder invites 15 target users. The decision is: do at least eight of them finish the core path in week six without a founder sitting next to them? If yes, fund the next slice. If not, stop or change the offer. A partner who only hears "build the platform" will add screens. A partner who hears the eight-out-of-fifteen line will cut screens until that path is observable.

Without those three lines, every bidder optimises for a larger scope. Your risk is building the wrong thing. Their risk, if you let them, is looking busy.

## 2. Discovery before a fixed price

Strong partners want a discovery phase, or paid scoping, before they sign a fixed bid. A blind signature at CHF 40,000 often buys later overruns, or a box so narrow that the learning goal no longer fits. The number is an illustration of a common quote shape, not a price list.

Ask in the first call: when user feedback arrives, how does scope change, who approves it, and what happens to the price? You want a written change path. "We will be flexible" is not a path.

Discovery should leave you with a ranked backlog, the risks they refuse to hide (payments, a nasty integration, a second language), and a picture of the first release a stranger can click. If discovery is free and unlimited, it is usually a sales meeting. Pay for the weeks that force the cut.

## 3. IP, the repository, and who can deploy

Clarify this in the contract and in the tools:

- The Git repository sits in your organisation, or transfers to you on exit with a date.
- You have a licence to the code that was written for you, including scripts that build and deploy it.
- Cloud accounts are in your name, or you have an export that is more than a zip of a database.
- You know who may deploy to production, and who can read logs the week after launch.

If this stays fuzzy, you are renting the product. The week you disagree, you find out whether you can hire someone else to continue it. Ask for a short "Tuesday test": if the contract ended next Tuesday, which logins would you still have by Friday?

## 4. Milestones a user can touch

Good milestones are demonstrable. Authentication and the core journey. A minimal admin view so you are not editing the database by hand. Payments if the decision requires money to change hands. Logging so a failure is visible.

Weak milestones sound like progress and show nothing: "backend done", "sprint 3 complete", "API finished". Tie each milestone to something a user, or you playing a user, can do in a browser. Put the date next to it, and put the out-of-scope list next to that, so the date is about the path you wrote down, not about every idea from the last interview.

## 5. Put users in the calendar

If a partner builds exactly the document you wrote and never puts a user in front of it, you can pay for a polished product aimed at the wrong hypothesis. The minimum is a repeating review with real or representative users, notes of what you learned, and a backlog that changes because of those notes.

## 6. Red flags

- Fixed price, scope not understood, no way to change either.
- No repository access and no metrics, explained as "security". Security is access control, not secrecy from the owner.
- Ticket-taking with nobody accountable for the architecture.
- "We handle everything" and no measure for whether the MVP worked.

One of these can be a negotiation. Two of them are a reason to keep looking.

## 7. When you need product judgement as well as a build

Some founders need a technical lead who will argue with the scope. Some need a team that will implement a scope they already trust. If you need product judgement as well as the build, read [when to get a real tech partner](/blog/when-to-get-a-real-tech-partner), then revisit [venture studio vs agency](/blog/venture-studio-vs-agency). The label on the website matters less than whether they will learn with you or only close tickets.
