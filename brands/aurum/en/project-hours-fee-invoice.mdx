## From project hours to the fee invoice

You get from project hours to a fee invoice in five steps: book time on a project and a phase, check who is missing and what does not fit, raise a budget warning where you asked for one, draft the invoice from those hours, and have one person approve it. The copying between the time record and the invoice is the part a workflow can take. The fee you will stand behind stays with a person.

This is for architecture and engineering offices, and for agencies or consultancies that bill the same way: time against a project, a phase, or a monthly retainer. It is not a trades daywork sheet. A daywork report records a crew on site, with material, for one customer's invoice. That route is in [from daywork report to invoice](/blog/daywork-report-to-invoice). Which of the surrounding jobs in a design office are worth looking at, and where the SIA orders sit, is in [AI for architecture and engineering offices](/blog/ai-for-architects-engineers).

## Step 1: The same codes on the timesheet and on the invoice

An hour can become a line only if the bin it sits in is the bin the client is billed for. Decide the list once: client, project, phase or work package, and, if you use them, a task such as meeting, drawing or site. Agencies can use the same list with "retainer" or "campaign" in place of a design phase. Write the codes down. If the time tool says "phase 3" and the contract says "Bauprojekt", the invoice draft needs the client's name, stored next to the code. Do not ask each person to invent a label on Friday.

Rates live on the project, not in the narrative the person types next to the hours. Senior, junior, travel, a reduced rate for a particular phase: look them up. A workflow that guesses a rate from a job title in a sentence will bill the wrong figure.

## Step 2: Hours booked in the week they were worked

The capture is boring on purpose. Each person enters time in one tool, on the day or at a fixed point in the week, against the codes from step 1. A second spreadsheet "for the real numbers" is how the invoice drifts away from what people remember.

If the contract pays travel or expenses, those are fields, not a note at the bottom. Receipts sit with the entry. What you do not pay, you do not collect. A blank day for someone who was on leave is different from a blank day for someone who was in meetings all week and booked nothing. The second is the case you want to see.

## Step 3: Checks before anyone drafts an invoice

On the morning you bill, the review list is the exceptions, not every line.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Someone is on the project team and has no hours in the period</li>
  <li>Hours sit on a phase you have already closed, or on "internal" for a client meeting</li>
  <li>The same person has overlapping hours on two projects</li>
  <li>A phase has hours and no rate, or a rate and no hours you expected</li>
  <li>The period's hours, at the stored rates, cross a budget line you marked</li>
  <li>A description is empty where the client has asked to see one</li>
</ul>

A budget warning is not a refusal. Some contracts say you stop and ask the client before you pass a figure. Others say you bill and discuss. Write that rule next to the project. The workflow applies the rule you stored. It does not invent a stop because a round number was crossed. Hours on a closed phase go back to the person who booked them, not silently onto the next phase.

## Step 4: One draft per project or per phase

Group the approved hours the way the contract bills: one invoice per project per month, or one invoice when a phase closes, or a retainer line plus hours above it. The draft uses the rates on the job and lists the phase names the client expects. Attach the hour list if the contract asks for it. The covering letter is a template. How to fill a letter from fields you already hold is in [creating documents from templates](/blog/generate-documents-from-templates).

The draft is created in the software where you already invoice, by import or by an interface that software actually offers. We do not assume a product until we see the file. A second invoice typed from the draft on paper is the failure you are trying to remove.

Mark the hours as billed when the invoice is approved, not when the draft appears. A draft that is abandoned should release the hours, or next month you will miss them.

## Step 5: One person approves

One person, usually the project lead, reads the draft: the total, the phase, anything flagged, the letter. They approve, change a line, or hold the invoice. They do not retype it. A budget that has been crossed is a conversation, either with the client or inside the office, before or after the invoice, according to the rule from step 3. The workflow can put that sentence on the review list. It should not email the client that the project is over budget.

## When the time tool is enough

The time tool is enough when everyone books in it, the codes match the contract, missing hours are already obvious, and the invoice is produced from that record without a second typing. Many offices are close: the tool is there, and the invoice is still a spreadsheet the project lead builds on Sunday. That spreadsheet is the gap. Automation belongs between the tool you already trust and the invoice, not in a third timer.

## A made-up example: a phase billed on the first Monday

A made-up example: an engineering office of twelve people runs about twenty open projects. Time is booked in a tool the office already pays for. On the first working day of the month a project lead copies hours into a spreadsheet, applies rates from memory, and writes a short letter. Two projects are retainers. One phase was closed last month, and three hours were still booked on it.

In the changed route, the review list on that morning shows four items: a person staffed on a project with no hours, three hours on the closed phase, a phase whose amount at stored rates sits above the figure the contract told the office to flag, and a retainer project with hours that belong inside the retainer rather than on top of it. The project lead resolves those four, confirms the rest, and the drafts sit in the invoicing software, one per project, letters included. The rates did not change. What goes is the spreadsheet. How many hours that saves depends on how many invoices you write and how long one takes today. An agency with the same pattern, campaigns instead of phases, uses the same list.

## When to leave it

Leave it if you bill a handful of lump sums a year and nobody records time. Agree a single invoice date and a template. Leave it too if the phase codes change every month. Fix the list, use it for one billing cycle, and only then connect it to the invoice. A workflow cannot bill a structure that the team does not share.
