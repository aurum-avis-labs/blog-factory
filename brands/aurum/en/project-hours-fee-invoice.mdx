## From project hours to the fee invoice

<p class="blog-lede">Five steps take you from booked project hours to a fee invoice: shared codes, clean booking, exceptions before the draft, draft from the time tool, approval by one person.</p>

You get from project hours to a fee invoice by booking time on project and phase, checking who is missing and what does not fit, raising budget warnings where you asked for them, drafting the invoice from those hours and having one person approve it. Copying between the time record and the invoice is what a workflow can take. The fee you will stand behind stays with a person.

This is for architecture and engineering offices, and for agencies or consultancies that bill the same way: time against a project, a phase or a monthly retainer. It is **not** a trades daywork sheet. A daywork report records a crew on site, often with material, for one customer's invoice. That route is in [from daywork report to invoice](/prozess-check/blog/daywork-report-to-invoice). Which surrounding jobs in a design office are worth a look, and where the SIA orders sit, is in [AI for architecture and engineering offices](/prozess-check/blog/ai-for-architects-engineers).

## Step 1: The same codes on the timesheet and on the invoice

An hour becomes a line only if the bin it sits in is the bin the client pays for. Decide once: client, project, phase or work package, optionally a task such as meeting, drawing or site. Agencies use "retainer" or "campaign" instead of a design phase. Write the codes down. If the time tool says "phase 3" and the contract says "Bauprojekt", store the client's name next to the code on the draft. Do not ask each person to invent a label on Friday.

Rates live on the project, not in the narrative beside the hours. Senior, junior, travel, a reduced rate for one phase: look them up. A workflow that guesses a rate from a job title in a sentence will bill the wrong figure. If the rate changes with the calendar year, record the date on the project so the draft pulls the right figure for the period.

## Step 2: Hours booked in the week they were worked

Capture is boring on purpose. Each person enters time in one tool, on the day or at a fixed weekly point, against the codes from step 1. A second spreadsheet "for the real numbers" is how the invoice drifts from what people remember.

If the contract pays travel or expenses, those are fields with receipts, not a footnote. What you do not bill, you do not collect. A blank day on leave differs from a blank day full of meetings with no booking. You want to see the second case.

## Step 3: Checks before anyone drafts

On billing day the review list is the exceptions, not every line.

<ul>
  <li>Someone on the project team has no hours in the period</li>
  <li>Hours on a closed phase, or on "internal" for a client meeting</li>
  <li>Overlapping hours for the same person on two projects</li>
  <li>A phase with hours and no rate, or a rate and no hours you expected</li>
  <li>Period hours, at stored rates, cross a budget line you marked</li>
  <li>Missing description where the client asked to see one</li>
</ul>

A budget warning is not a refusal. Some contracts require a conversation before a figure; others allow billing and discussion afterwards. Write the rule on the project. The workflow applies the rule you stored. Hours on a closed phase go back to the person who booked them, not silently onto the next phase.

## Step 4: One draft per project or phase

Group approved hours the way the contract bills: one invoice per project per month, one when a phase closes, or a retainer line plus hours above it. The draft uses rates and phase names the client expects. Attach the half hour list if the contract asks for it. The covering letter is a template. Filling a letter from fields you already hold is in [creating documents from templates](/prozess-check/blog/generate-documents-from-templates).

The draft is created in the software where you already invoice, by import or an interface that software actually offers. We do not assume a product until we see the file. A second invoice typed from a paper draft is the failure you are trying to remove.

Mark hours as billed when the invoice is approved, not when the draft appears. An abandoned draft must release the hours, or next month you will miss them. On retainers, separate hours that belong inside the retainer from hours above it. The draft should not quietly fill the retainer with hours the contract already covers.

## Step 5: One person approves

Usually the project lead reads draft, total, phase, flags and letter. Approve, change a line, or hold. No retyping. A crossed budget is a conversation with the client or inside the office, per the rule from step 3. The workflow can put that sentence on the review list. It should not email the client that the project is over budget.

## When the time tool is enough

Enough when everyone books in it, codes match the contract, missing hours are obvious and the invoice is produced without a second typing. Many offices are close: the tool is there, the invoice still a Sunday spreadsheet from the project lead. **That spreadsheet** is the gap. Automation belongs between the tool you trust and the invoice, not in a third timer. A Process Check fits when that spreadsheet returns every month and covers more than a few invoices.

## A made-up example: first Monday of the month

A made-up example: engineering office, twelve people, about twenty open projects. Time in a tool the office already pays for. On the first working day a project lead copies hours into a spreadsheet, applies rates from memory and writes the letter. Two retainers, one closed phase with three hours still on it.

The review list shows four items: staffed person with no hours, three hours on the closed phase, phase above the contract flag, retainer with hours that belong inside the retainer. Resolve the four, confirm the rest, drafts in invoicing software with letters attached. Rates did not change; only the path from hours to lines did. The spreadsheet goes. Time saved depends on how many invoices you write and how long one takes today. An agency with campaigns instead of phases uses the same list with different labels in step 1.

## When to leave it

A handful of lump sums a year with no time recording: agree one invoice date and a template. If phase codes change every month, fix the list for one billing cycle before you connect it to the invoice. A workflow cannot bill a structure the team does not share.
