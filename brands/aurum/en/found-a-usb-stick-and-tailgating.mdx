## Found a USB stick and tailgating at work

If you find a USB stick in the car park, lobby, or at reception, the first rule is not to plug it into a company PC to «see who it belongs to». Tailgating is passing through a secured door behind someone else without badging yourself. Both are social engineering with physical access. The question «found a USB stick, what now» and clear tailgating rules belong in one guide because attackers often combine them: access first, then a «forgotten» device inside the office.

Swiss offices with 20 to 250 staff often trust colleagues at the door. Training should give reception a polite script and give everyone permission to challenge unknown followers without sounding rude.

## Found a USB stick: what to do

Set the device aside unchanged. Note location and time; photograph the spot if you can do so safely. Hand it to IT or a person named by management in an envelope marked «found, not scanned». Report the find through the same channel as suspicious mail, as described in [internal incident reporting](/security/blog/internal-incident-reporting).

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>No plug-in on production PCs, no «spare laptop» out of curiosity</li>
  <li>Envelope, place, time; IT or management owns next steps</li>
  <li>Report internally even when the stick has no label</li>
</ul>

## Why plugging it in is risky

USB sticks can trigger malware as soon as the operating system mounts the device. Auto-run is often disabled on modern systems, but disguised files, fake folder names, and devices that pose as keyboards remain a risk. Attackers drop sticks because curiosity and helpfulness work. The [Swiss SME cyber security portal](https://www.kmu.admin.ch/de/cybersicherheit) stresses staff awareness; physical bait is part of the same topic as phishing mail.

## Tailgating in everyday office life

Tailgating exploits politeness: someone carries boxes, quotes a name from LinkedIn, or says «IT sent me». In business centres several tenants share lifts and doors. When re-entering from smoking areas or after lunch, colleagues often hold the door. Policy: everyone badges individually, including people you know. Remote days reduce door traffic but increase coffee-area chats where strangers walk in with staff.

More scenes (courier with USB, fake technician) are covered in [social engineering at the office](/security/blog/social-engineering-at-the-office). Mail phishing stays separate in [how to spot phishing emails](/security/blog/how-to-spot-phishing-emails).

## USB by post and at the loading bay

Attackers post sticks to «the managing director» or leave them at ramps and warehouses. Reception and logistics should treat unexpected media like found sticks: IT only, no test on machines with customer data. A genuine IT upgrade stick arrives through your change process, not by post without notice.

## What IT does with found media

IT scans on isolated machines never joined to the domain. Outcome and disposal are documented. Staff should not «just take a look», including on an old personal laptop in the office. If the stick is labelled like your firm, still do not plug it in: attackers imitate company branding.

## What this is not

Not an IT upgrade stick you were told about in advance. Not firefighters during a announced drill. Not colleagues you know on the same floor who badge themselves. Not a red-team test without written notice to management and reception.

## Visitor rules that fit on one slide

One badge per person. Escort to the destination; log even «quick» deliveries. Facility publishes guest Wi-Fi centrally, not ad hoc printouts at reception. Report suspicious people without confrontation: time, description, photo of ID if safe.

## Who should know the rules

Reception, facilities, logistics, and anyone with server-room keys need the same short briefing. Your IT partner should know where isolated scan machines sit and who is reachable at weekends. Management should confirm in writing that reports are welcome even when the stick turns out harmless. Without that clarity, «just plug it in quickly» stays tempting.

## If someone already plugged a stick in

Alert IT immediately, have the PC taken off the network, and do not «clean up» alone. Passwords and tokens may be affected; IT owns the steps. Document internally like other incidents. External reporting to the NCSC is optional; speed internally matters.

## Tailgating in shared buildings

Building management and neighbouring tenants should know whom you contact after repeat incidents. Lifts and car parks are common places where visitors «ride along» without badging. Your rule stays: no access door without a badge, even when someone wears a uniform. Uniform alone is not a substitute for an appointment or ticket number.

## What IT does not need from you

IT does not need you to hunt for the «owner» by opening files on the stick. They need place, time, and who handed it over. Do not open files to «gather clues»; that delays proper analysis and adds risk.

## One line for training and onboarding

One sentence often suffices: «Report found USB and tailgating immediately, never plug in, do not hold the door.» [Security awareness training](/security/security-awareness) practises wording for reception and general staff so nobody «just wanted to help».

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>May I send the stick to IT by internal mail?</h3>
  <p>Yes, if your policy allows it. Mark it as unknown media and do not attach it to a PC first.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should I report tailgating if nothing was stolen?</h3>
  <p>Yes, internally. Repeat attempts help IT and facilities spot patterns before someone plants hardware or asks for credentials.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Does this apply when working from home?</h3>
  <p>At the company site, yes. Home office raises other risks; on days on site, badge rules apply when you re-enter the building.</p>
</div>
