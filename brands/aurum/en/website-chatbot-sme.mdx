## Website chatbot for a small business: yes or no

For most small firms, the better first answer is no: a clear FAQ page and a contact form will do more than a chatbot. A chatbot is the better answer when the same questions arrive in volume, the replies are stable and written down, and a person takes over the moment the question leaves that list.

This page is for owners and office leads of Swiss SMEs who are being offered a chat widget. It compares two ways of handling the questions that arrive on the website. It is not a ranking of products, and it is not about the telephone. A voice assistant that picks up the phone is a separate decision.

## What a good FAQ and a form already do

A good FAQ answers the questions your team repeats every week, in the words customers actually use. Opening hours, where to park, how to reach you, what you do not offer, how a quote starts, how long a typical reply takes. Each answer is a sentence a person approved. Changing it is an edit, not a training exercise.

A form, or a plain email address next to it, does the rest. The visitor writes the case. Your team reads it in the shared inbox and replies. The path is slow for the tenth copy of "are you open on Saturday", and it is the right path for "can you do this by Friday, and what would it cost". Nobody on the site has to guess.

If the FAQ is thin, a chatbot will not repair it. The bot has nothing stable to say, so it will either hedge or invent. Write the page first. Then count, for two weeks, which questions still land with a person. That count is the decision, not a vendor demo.

## What a chatbot adds, and what it does not

A chatbot on the site is a window. Someone types a question and gets a reply without waiting for office hours. That is useful when the answer is already on the FAQ and the visitor will not scroll to it, or when you want to catch the question outside your hours and put a name, an email and the topic into the inbox for the morning.

It does not know your stock, your diary or your prices unless you have connected those systems and a person has defined what the bot may say. It does not "know the business" because it sounds fluent. Fluency is not a source.

There are two shapes. A bot that answers only from pages you wrote is a search box with a conversation. A bot that books and writes into other systems is a different design, covered in [AI agent or workflow automation](/prozess-check/blog/ai-agent-or-workflow-automation): if you can name the steps before the message arrives, a fixed path is usually enough.

| | FAQ and form | Chatbot |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Same question every week | Visible answer, no conversation needed | Useful if people still fail to find the page |
| Price, stock, a date | The form collects the case; a person replies | Only if the bot may quote from a list you control, and a person still confirms the odd cases |
| When it is unsure | The visitor writes the question in full | It must say so and hand over, not fill the gap |
| What you maintain | The page | The page, plus logs, the handover, and a check when the model changes |
| Personal data | What the form asks for, and the privacy notice | The chat log as well, often at a third-party widget |

## Wrong promises are the real risk

The damage is not a clumsy sentence about parking. It is a price, a delivery date, a "yes, we can do that", or a medical, legal or financial answer the firm did not stand behind. Customers treat the window as the firm. If the text says the item is in stock, they may turn up to collect it. If it quotes a figure, they may plan around that figure.

The bot does not state prices, stock, appointments or what you can deliver. Those answers stay with a person. It may point to the FAQ, collect the question and say when someone will reply. Keep the allowed answers on a page you control. If the question is not on that page, the reply is a handover, not a guess. Turning the model's imagination off is a setting and a source list, not a hope. Review a sample of real chats every week before you trust the window with more.

If you sell in a way where an automated sentence could bind the firm, ask your lawyer before the bot is allowed to say yes.

## Data protection sits on the widget

A chat that asks for a name, an email or the content of a case collects personal data. Under [Art. 19 of the Data Protection Act](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/en), you inform the person, at least, who you are, why you collect the data, and who receives them. If the log is disclosed abroad, Art. 19 para. 4 also asks you to name the state and the safeguard. The privacy notice on the site has to match the widget, not the brochure from last year.

The [FDPIC](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/en/ai-and-data-protection) states that the Act applies to AI and that people may know whether they write to a machine and whether inputs train the model. Say that in the window. A free consumer chat behind the widget is the wrong place for customer text; see [ChatGPT and customer data](/prozess-check/blog/chatgpt-customer-data-swiss-dsg).

A third-party widget is a processor. You need a contract, you need to know which subprocessors see the transcript, and you need the location question answered on the vendor's own page. "Data stays in Europe" is not a data centre in Switzerland. How to read that claim is in [does AI have to run in Switzerland](/prozess-check/blog/ai-data-location-switzerland).

Answering opening hours is not an automated individual decision under Art. 21 FADP. Do not dress a FAQ window up as that, and do not let the bot refuse a customer, set a price or decide a claim on its own. Those are decisions for a person.

## Hand the case to a person

The handover is the product. The bot recognises that the question is not on the list, says so, asks for a way to reply, and places the thread where your team already works. For many firms that place is the shared inbox. How to sort that inbox, with rules first and a draft instead of an automatic send, is in [sort emails in a shared inbox](/prozess-check/blog/shared-inbox-sort-emails).

Do not give the bot a second inbox that nobody opens. If the morning pile is already too large, a widget adds a pile. Fix the pile, or the bot will apologise politely into the void.

A person sends anything that leaves the firm. The bot may prepare a draft from the FAQ. It does not press send on a price or a date. The same person, or a colleague, should be able to see the chat the customer saw, so the reply does not start from zero.

## Who keeps it true

A chatbot without an owner becomes a stale FAQ that talks. Someone updates opening hours, prices and the list of what you do not do. Someone reads the questions the bot could not answer and either adds them to the page or leaves them with the team. Someone checks, after a model or a widget update, that the limits still hold.

If that person does not exist, do not launch. The FAQ page is then the whole system, and it is easier to notice when it is wrong because customers email you the error.

Count three things once a month, not a vanity "conversations" total: how many chats were answered from the list, how many were handed over, and how many replies a person later had to correct. The third number is the one that decides whether the window stays.

## A decision you can make this week

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>List the ten questions the team answered most often last month.</li>
  <li>Put the stable ones on a page, in the customer's words, with a named owner.</li>
  <li>If those ten still bury the inbox, try a bot that may only quote that page.</li>
  <li>Write the sentence that says this is a machine, and where the log goes.</li>
  <li>Park prices, stock and appointments with a person, and test the handover on a quiet afternoon.</li>
</ul>

Skip the widget if the questions are few and different, if a wrong yes would be expensive, or if the site's job is to get a serious enquiry to a person. A form with a reply-time sentence will beat a fluent guess.
