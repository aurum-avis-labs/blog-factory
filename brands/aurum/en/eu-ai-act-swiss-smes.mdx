## The EU AI Act and Swiss SMEs in practice

For a Swiss SME that only has customers in Switzerland and does not run a system that decides about people, the EU AI Act is rarely the first lever. The work this month is the Swiss Data Protection Act (nDSG / FADP), the contract with your cloud vendor, and a rule about what staff may paste into ChatGPT. That daily piece is in [ChatGPT and customer data](/blog/chatgpt-customer-data-swiss-dsg). This page is the practical split, for owners and office leads. It is not legal advice and it does not read the Act for you.

The binding text and the timeline sit with the [European Commission](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai). If you supply into the EU, sell there, have an establishment there, or run a system treated as high-risk there, you need your own counsel. A blog post does not replace that conversation.

## Who this is for

Managing directors, ops and office leads in a firm of roughly 5 to 80 people, little or no in-house legal or IT. Someone on the board, or a customer, has asked what you "do about the AI Act". You want to know whether that question changes Monday's work.

This page is a poor fit if you employ a compliance team, ship a product that scores people, or hire at a volume where software filters candidates. Those are counsel questions from the first meeting. They are also a poor fit for a one-hour workflow check.

## Why waiting on a full reading of the Act fails the week

The usual reaction is one of two. A long policy nobody opens, or a decision to ignore the topic because the firm is Swiss and the server "is probably in Europe". Staff still paste a customer list into a private chat, because the policy does not name the tool they actually have on their phone. The [FDPIC](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/en/ai-and-data-protection) is plain that the Data Protection Act is technology-neutral: whoever processes personal data with an AI tool remains responsible. A foreign vendor does not move that responsibility off the firm. That is the FDPIC's guidance, and it is not a ruling on your case.

So the Act can be on a slide in Brussels while the live risk sits in a Free account. An allow-list and a data rule change that this month. A commentary on risk tiers does not.

## When the Act still lands on your table

Three situations, in ordinary language. You have customers in the EU, or you offer the product there. You have an establishment in the EU. Or the system decides about people: credit, hiring, biometric identification are the examples that already force a lawyer into the room.

Then you stop using this page as the plan. You take the Commission's text to counsel, you say which product and which market, and you ask what applies to that product. We do not rank your system as high-risk or out of scope from a blog.

For the Monday report from Excel, and for the invoice that arrives as a PDF, the list stays the shorter one below. Those workflows still have to respect the Data Protection Act if names, addresses or mandate files are in the file. They do not become an AI Act memorandum because a model helped draft a sentence.

## What you can do without parsing the Act

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Write the tools that are allowed. Free ChatGPT on a private phone is either on that list, with conditions, or it is off</li>
  <li>Customer data, payroll, and mandate files stay out of Free and Plus accounts</li>
  <li>Name one owner who may say a mail or a figure can leave the firm</li>
  <li>A person checks personal data and any number that will be reused, before it goes out</li>
  <li>If you sell into the EU or the system decides about people, book counsel, and take the product description with you</li>
</ul>

That is a team rule. It is what the [AI in Business workshop](/services/ai-in-business) is for: shared language, an allow-list, the same guardrails for the people who actually paste. It is a training and rules day. It does not build the Monday file.

## A joinery in St. Gallen, and a supplier who sells into Baden-Württemberg

Picture two firms, neither of them a client story, so you can see the split.

A joinery in St. Gallen sells kitchens to households in eastern Switzerland. The office uses mail, a small accounting tool, and a handful of ChatGPT prompts for supplier replies. There is no scoring of people, no hiring software, no EU shop. The Act is the wrong first project. The useful week is the allow-list, a company account if they need one, and the traffic light in the ChatGPT article: public, internal, personal. The owner can do that without a memo on EU risk classes.

A parts supplier in the same town ships repeat orders to workshops in Baden-Württemberg and keeps an online catalogue those buyers use. The same supplier is also being sold "an AI that screens CVs". Those are two different questions, and both leave this page. Cross-border customers and a hiring tool are counsel, with the product and the market written down. The kitchen joinery's prompt habit is not the template for that meeting.

If either firm also has one repetitive office workflow, invoices from portals or a weekly sheet, that is a separate job from the regulation question. Building that single workflow is the [Process Check](/prozess-check). One sentence, so the two purchases stay apart: the workshop sets the rules the team shares, the Check takes one recurring process. You do not need both because a headline mentioned the AI Act.

## Does the Act replace the Swiss Data Protection Act?

No. This is not legal advice. For personal data you process, the Swiss Data Protection Act still applies, including when the tool is a chatbot. The EU AI Act can still matter if you are in the EU market or you run a system that decides about people. If that is you, ask counsel. If you only serve Switzerland and the tools only draft text, start with the allow-list.
