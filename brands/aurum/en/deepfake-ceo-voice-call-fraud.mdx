## Deepfake CEO voice call fraud: treat the voice like an email you cannot trust

**Deepfake CEO voice call fraud** is synthetic audio that sounds like your chief executive on a phone call or voice note. Finance, an assistant, or IT is pushed to release payments, read out MFA codes, or bypass approval rules. It builds on [CEO fraud in Switzerland](/security/blog/ceo-fraud-switzerland), which often starts in email. Here the pressure arrives as a familiar voice, sometimes with background noise or a short clip on a messaging app. It is not AI-written phishing in the inbox, and not a standard fake IT support call.

Whether your firm must report a cyber incident to BACS within 24 hours depends on critical infrastructure rules. Most SMEs are not covered operators. Your first job is still to stop payment and verify identity out of band.

## How a cloned executive voice reaches finance

Attackers collect public clips from podcasts, webinars, LinkedIn video, or press appearances. They generate audio that matches pace, accent, and typical phrasing. Calls go to accounts payable, an executive assistant, or the helpdesk, often with a "bad line" and a request to act now. Some combine a short voice note on WhatsApp, Teams, or Signal with a follow-up email. The script mirrors email CEO fraud: secrecy, urgency, and "do not call back on the usual number, I am in meetings".

<ul class="blog-flow">
  <li><strong>Harvest</strong><p>Public voice and video of executives online.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Clone</strong><p>AI audio that matches pace and accent.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Pressure</strong><p>Urgent payment or code on a live line.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Loss</strong><p>Transfer or account takeover if nobody verifies.</p></li>
</ul>

## Who gets called first

In many firms the call does not go to the chief executive directly. It goes to an assistant, accounts payable, or the helpdesk. The voice sounds like whoever can release payments or open systems. Those roles need the same stop rules as finance, not only the executive team.

<ul class="blog-scenarios">
  <li><h3>Executive assistant</h3><p>Voice note about a "confidential transfer today". Callback through the HR number, no waiver of the approval process.</p></li>
  <li><h3>Accounts payable</h3><p>Call with a changed payee and pressure to act "before close". Hang up, start internal approval, do not confirm on the line.</p></li>
</ul>

## Stop rules that work on voice as well as email

Some calls ask for an MFA code "for the meeting" or "so I can log in". That is often account takeover, not only a transfer. No codes on the phone, even when the voice sounds impatient.

Apply the same scepticism you use for payment instructions that arrive only by email. If the voice insists on secrecy from finance or controlling, that is a stop signal.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Hang up and call back on the known number, never on the caller ID</li>
  <li>Do not read codes out loud on the phone</li>
  <li>Do not waive approval because "the boss is on the line"</li>
  <li>Never change a beneficiary during an unverified call</li>
  <li>Report internally even when no money moved</li>
</ul>

A made-up example: an assistant receives a WhatsApp voice note that sounds like the owner asking for a same-day CHF 40'000 transfer to a "new consultant". She replies that she will confirm through the usual mobile number stored in HR. The real owner has not sent the note. Finance holds the payment.

Link payment controls to the [four-eyes principle for payments](/security/blog/four-eyes-principle-for-payments) so a second person must approve even when "the boss" called.

## Voice notes on Teams, WhatsApp, and Signal

Short voice notes count as calls. A familiar voice alone does not justify a payment release. State that payment instructions run only through your documented approval process, not through private chat channels. IT cannot reliably tell on a mobile phone whether audio is synthetic. Process and callback stay central.

## What deepfake voice fraud is not

It is not a defective headset on a real executive call. It is not standard vishing from fake Microsoft support. It is not the same article as invoice fraud with a changed IBAN, where the scam starts in writing. It is not proof that video calls are unusable. Verification rules apply to every channel. Antivirus on laptops does not detect a synthetic voice on a mobile phone.

Live deepfake video in real time is still rare in many SME cases. Short clips in chat apps are often enough to create trust. Policy should require callback verification for any payment instruction, regardless of medium.

## What to do first if a call felt wrong

If no payment left, document the number, time, request, and channel. Report internally and warn finance and assistants the same day. Offenders try several people. If one person refuses, still report the pattern, not only "nothing happened".

If money moved, call the bank immediately, involve the police, and preserve recordings or chat exports. Voluntary reports to BACS remain possible. The statutory 24-hour duty applies only to covered critical infrastructure operators.

Document attempts even without a loss. Insurers and later audits ask about repeated tries. Whether you also need a data protection adviser is a separate question.
