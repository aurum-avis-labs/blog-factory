## Competitor analysis for an MVP

Competitor analysis for an MVP is a short map of how your target buyers already solve the job, what they pay or tolerate today, and which trade-offs you plan to test. It is not a forty-page market report, a patent search, or proof that strangers will pay you. You do it so you do not rebuild an incumbent feature list or miss that the real rival is a spreadsheet and a phone call.

This is for founders and innovation leads who have a problem hypothesis and are deciding what to put in a first build. If you still need the wider validation menu, start with [how to validate a startup idea](/blog/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea). Interviews and problem checks come before you treat a competitor grid as evidence.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Write the job in one sentence</h3><p>Who acts, which outcome they want, and how often the pain shows up. Without that, every product in the category looks like a direct rival.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>List real alternatives</h3><p>Named tools, agencies, in-house scripts, paper, and doing nothing. Include the workaround people defend because it is familiar.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Try what you can in public</h3><p>Sign up, read pricing pages, skim docs and changelogs. Note what they optimise for, not every checkbox on a comparison site.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Mark one testable wedge</h3><p>One behaviour or segment you will measure in your MVP, not a roadmap of gaps. If the wedge needs ten features to show up, it is not a wedge yet.</p></li>
</ol>

## What belongs on the map

**Direct rivals** sell a similar outcome to the same buyer. **Indirect rivals** solve the job differently: a consultant, a marketplace, or a general tool stretched into a workaround. For B2B in Switzerland, the indirect row often matters more than the flashiest SaaS logo, because procurement and trust sit with a person, not a landing page.

Capture a few fields per alternative, in plain language:

- How the buyer gets started today (self-serve, demo, referral, tender)
- What seems to be monetised (seat, transaction, project, hardware)
- Where friction shows up in public reviews or your interviews (onboarding, language, integrations, support hours)
- What they clearly will not do (no mobile app, no TWINT, no on-prem option)

You are looking for **constraints**, not a scorecard that always favours your idea. If every row looks weak and your row looks perfect, you probably graded yourself too kindly.

## What competitor analysis does not prove

A filled grid does not show market size, regulatory clearance, or willingness to pay. It does not show that you can acquire users cheaper than an incumbent with a decade of brand and support. It also does not protect the idea: under Swiss practice, ideas as such cannot be protected; execution and distinct assets can be, which is a different conversation with your adviser.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>Competitor analysis informs the hypothesis you will test in build and acquisition. It does not replace interviews, a concierge test, or measured conversion on a real offer. Treat it as input to scope, not as a Go decision.</p>
</aside>

## A made-up example: scheduling visits for trades firms

A made-up example: two people aim at small trades firms that book customer visits. Their job sentence is: "office staff schedule same-day visits without double-booking crews." Their list includes a generic calendar, an industry-specific field-service app with a demo-only price, WhatsApp threads, and a whiteboard in the depot.

They sign up for the field-service trial and hit a long form before the first route appears. Public pricing is "contact sales." WhatsApp works until someone forgets which thread holds which address. The whiteboard fails when a driver calls in sick.

They write one wedge to test: "self-serve booking in under five minutes for firms with at most six crews, German UI, no CRM import on day one." They do not promise to beat the enterprise app on routing optimisation. They note that the real competitor for week one is WhatsApp, not the logo on the comparison blog.

That map fits on two pages. It tells them what to leave out of the MVP and which interview question to ask next: "What broke the last time you tried software for this?"

## How this connects to an MVP scope

Use the map to cut scope, not to inflate it. If every competitor ships ten integrations, your MVP might ship one integration and measure whether anyone completes onboarding without a sales call. If incumbents win on compliance narratives you cannot match in twelve weeks, your test might target a narrower segment or a manual back office for the first cohort.

When the wedge is written, pair the map with problem validation and interview notes so you are not arguing from screenshots alone. The next question is whether anyone changes behaviour when your offer is real, not whether your grid has more green cells.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How many competitors should you list?</h3>
  <p>Enough to cover direct tools and the workaround people actually use. Three logos and no spreadsheet row is usually incomplete. Twenty rows without a job sentence is noise.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should you copy the market leader's feature list?</h3>
  <p>No. Copy the job and measure one difference you can ship and observe. Parity features are expensive proof that you are late, not that you are validated.</p>
</div>

## When the product validation package fits

When the wedge is narrow but you still need a deployed product, landing page, paid acquisition on Google and LinkedIn, and honest drop-off data over several weeks, the [product validation package](/services/mvp-validation) matches that sequence. Weeks one to four put a real app in production, not a mockup. Later weeks measure cost per lead and conversion, with room to pivot a few times before a Go, Pivot, or Stop recommendation.

It fits teams that already did light competitor mapping and customer conversations, and now need measured behaviour rather than another desk pass. It is a poor fit if you only wanted a ranking slide or if you are not willing to stop when the data stay weak.
