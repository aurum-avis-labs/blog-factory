## When a customer portal is worth it

A customer portal is worth it when the same trade customers keep asking for a status or a document you already hold, the answer is the same six or so fields every time, and those fields are right often enough that you would show them without a person checking first. It is weaker when the questions change, the volume is small, or the record would embarrass you if the customer saw it unedited. A login does not create trust on its own. It shows whatever is in the record.

Email and a phone call remain the right tool for a small office that still answers one-off questions. A portal is a narrow window onto one record. It does not replace a conversation about exceptions, price or delay.

## Who should skip a portal for now

<ul class="blog-scenarios">
  <li><h3>Fewer than a dozen status questions a week</h3><p>Login support can cost more than the phone. Count one week before you accept a quote.</p></li>
  <li><h3>Every answer still needs a person</h3><p>When coordination, a discount or «we need to check internally» comes before every figure, a portal publishes the same uncertainty.</p></li>
  <li><h3>The data sits in Access or Excel without one row per customer</h3><p>Without a source that separates each customer from the others, you only build a prettier link onto chaos. Settle [whether to replace that database](/prozess-check/blog/replace-access-database) before you draw a login screen.</p></li>
</ul>

If your trade software already offers a customer login, a second portal for the same status is rarely worthwhile. Use the account you have and invest in clean fields.

## What stays in email and on the phone

A portal suits repeating facts: order number, promised date, delivery note, whether something is in transit. Email keeps anything that needs context or changes quickly.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Price changes, goodwill and «we will make an exception»</li>
  <li>Apologies when something went wrong, and the plan for today</li>
  <li>Questions you cannot answer yet without asking internally</li>
  <li>New enquiries that are not about an existing job</li>
</ul>

Anyone who expects the portal to work like chat will still write to you. Plan for a person in the office for that, not only password-reset mail.

## What customers should see

Each customer sees their own rows: the open job, the date you are willing to promise, the documents you already send. They do not see another customer's rows, your internal notes, or a price you have not agreed. One shared password on a webpage is not an account. You cannot revoke it for one firm without locking everyone out, and you cannot tell who looked.

If the rows live in a system that already has an interface, the missing piece is the pipe, priced as in [what it costs to have an API built](/prozess-check/blog/build-an-api-integration-cost). A portal that a colleague still fills in by hand is a second place to type.

Someone has to notice when the pipe fails, and when a customer cannot get in. That ongoing work is described in [who runs the automation afterwards](/prozess-check/blog/who-runs-the-automation). If we host the small app, hosting is CHF 50–150 a month, plus whatever the platform or the model actually uses, and you can cancel it.

## What a portal must not become

A customer portal is not a replacement for your job system, not a project board for everyone involved, and not a marketing site with news. It should not grow into a full case file where every internal note lands.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>Show only what you would still say if the customer rang. A portal that displays a draft, an internal note or another customer's document does more harm than no login at all.</p>
</aside>

If the portal becomes a second place where staff «quickly» type something without updating the main system, you have two truths. Customers ring because the screen does not match, and you have doubled the upkeep.

## The support you are taking on

A portal adds a helpdesk, even a small one. People forget passwords. They write to say the date on the screen is not the date they were told. They try from a phone on a site. If nobody in the office owns those messages, the portal becomes a complaint about you, written by your own customers.

Count that before you count the calls you hope to avoid. If fifteen calls a week become ten calls plus five «I cannot log in» mails, you have moved the work. It pays when the avoided calls are the repetitive ones and the new mails are rare because the data matches what you would have said on the phone.

Also decide what happens when the status is wrong. The customer should not be the first person to discover a slip. A wrong date on a screen feels more final than a wrong date in a conversation. If your process still corrects the record the morning the job leaves, do not display that record the night before.

## The right of access is a different job

Under the Swiss Data Protection Act, any person may ask whether you process personal data about them. The federal data protection authority describes that right of access, including that the person has to exercise it themselves, on its [page on the right of access](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/auskunftsrecht). A portal does not fulfil that request, and the law does not require you to build one. Answering a request is a procedure of its own, covered in [how to answer a data subject access request](/prozess-check/blog/data-subject-access-requests). Keep the two apart. A login that shows an order status is a convenience. An access request is a reply you still have to write.

## A made-up example

A made-up example: a metal workshop with 12 people and about 40 trade customers. The office takes roughly 15 calls a week that are only «where is my order». The answer is six fields in the job system: order number, part, promised date, whether it has shipped, the carrier, the document. The owner pulls ten recent jobs and compares the screen with what the office would have said. Eight match. Two have a date the coordinator changed by phone and did not type back.

They should not open a portal this month. They should close that gap, for a few weeks, until a fresh sample matches. Then a portal that shows those six fields, one account per customer, is a sensible small build, fed from the job system rather than typed again. If the two mismatches were the normal case, the portal would publish the confusion. Email stays until the record is the same story the phone tells.

The firm, the counts and the eight-out-of-ten are invented. Your sample decides.

## Who looks after it

Write down who creates an account, who closes it when the customer leaves, and who answers «the date is wrong» before you accept a price. Those three tasks are the operation. The screen is the smaller part.

We have not built a ready-made customer login on top of bexio, Abacus or a trade package. If one of those already offers its own portal, use that and skip a second one. A second login for the same status is how offices end up maintaining two answers.

  <p>Sometimes for one document. For repeating status questions you lose separate accounts, an audit trail and the ability to close access when the relationship ends.</p>
</div>
