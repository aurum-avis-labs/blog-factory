## Social engineering examples in the office beyond e-mail

Social engineering at the office manipulates people to bypass process: a "technician" at the door, a "new auditor" asking for Wi-Fi, a delivery driver tailgating into the server closet, or a supplier call that pressures finance. E-mail phishing is one slice; physical and phone tricks hit reception, warehouse, and assistants who want to be helpful. Swiss SMEs often have badge access but weak visitor rules. Attackers exploit politeness and busy Mondays.

Phishing traits sit in [how to spot phishing emails](/security/blog/how-to-spot-phishing-emails) and [spotting AI-generated phishing](/security/blog/spotting-ai-generated-phishing). The typical "Microsoft support" call is narrower in [fake Microsoft support call at work](/security/blog/fake-microsoft-support-call-at-work). Live phone fraud: [vishing calls at work](/security/blog/vishing-calls-at-work). ## What this is not

Not a red-team exercise you commissioned without notice. Not a sales visit you scheduled in the calendar. Not a building evacuation drill announced internally. Physical access can pair with digital follow-up; report odd visits to IT and management the same day.

## Building and tenant mix

In shared office buildings, tailgating affects multiple firms. Speak up in the lift if someone follows without a badge. Tell building management when the same unknown person appears twice. Do not leave server doors open for "quick cable work".

## Maintenance windows and fake technicians

Real technicians arrive in windows IT announced. Fake ones appear on busy days with clipboards. Check ticket numbers in the PSA tool, not on the visitor’s phone. Start remote support only after internal confirmation, never because of a uniform alone.

## Warehouse, loading bay, and cleaning

Drivers may ask to use your network "for the label printer". Route them through reception and a guest VLAN if policy allows, or refuse. USB drops happen here too, not only at the front desk. Cleaning crews and plant maintenance hold keys; social engineers pose as replacements. Verify with facilities before opening the server room. Sign contractors into the same log as visitors.

## Culture without blame

Security culture starts when leadership thanks reception for stopping a leadership guest who forgot a badge. Managers who stress embarrassment lower report rates. Thank people for every report, even when nothing happened.

## Reporting path like phishing

Physical attempts belong in the same internal channel as suspicious mail. IT needs time, description, and a photo of the badge when safe. External BACS reports are optional; internal early warnings help colleagues at reception.

## "CEO waiting" and synthetic voice

Pressure "leadership is downstairs" or "Wi-Fi for the audit only" combines social engineering with hurry. Confirm via Teams or desk phone with the named person, not the visitor’s mobile. Phone voices can be synthetic; that overlaps [vishing](/security/blog/vishing-calls-at-work) but matters without a call too.

## Insurance and customer questions

After an incident, insurers or customers sometimes ask what physical controls exist. A one-page visitor rule answers without inventing certificates.

## Fifteen-minute table-top

Once a year: fake fire inspector, fake auditor, courier USB. No actors needed; discussion alone fixes visitor-policy gaps. Outcome: one updated reception instruction page.

## A made-up example

A made-up example: a fiduciary office with 28 people in a shared building. Tuesday 08:15 someone wants "from the fire panel" into the comms room. Reception calls facilities and IT; no ticket, no access. Wednesday "supplier" calls finance about an IBAN update; accounts hang up and dial the number on the last invoice. Both cases go to the same internal reporting path as phishing.

## A three-sentence reception script

"Hello, I will connect you with the person who confirms the appointment." "Without confirmation I cannot grant access; you may wait in the lobby." "If you feel pressure, do not call me alone later; we report internally." A card at the desk beats a thick handbook.

## Tailgating and badge culture

Train staff that "holding the door" is a reportable moment, not rudeness. People who prop doors for colleagues should know strangers can follow. Building leases may add visitor duties; involve facilities.

## Write the IT partner into the rule

The external partner should know which rooms they may enter and how visitors are logged. Without a written rule, "technicians" reach the first available admin. Review the service provider list with facilities and IT twice a year.
