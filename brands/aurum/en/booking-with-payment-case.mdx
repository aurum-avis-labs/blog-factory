## Automate appointment booking with payment: how ours runs

You can automate appointment booking with payment so that a slot only counts once it is paid. Our own Process Check booking works that way. The page reads free times from Microsoft Bookings through Microsoft Graph and sends the visitor to Stripe Checkout. Only after Stripe confirms the payment does a webhook create the appointment in Bookings, with a Microsoft Teams link. Bookings then sends the confirmation email. Nobody in the office has to touch it.

This is for owners and office leads who sell paid appointments, such as a first consultation or a paid assessment, and who already work in Microsoft 365. It describes a flow we built for ourselves and use every day: you go through it when you book the Process Check. It is not an argument that every firm should build one. For many, a ready-made tool is enough, and a section below says when.

## Can Microsoft Bookings take payment itself?

No. Microsoft's own [Bookings FAQ](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365/bookings/bookings-faq) answers the question "Can I link a payment option to my Bookings?" with "No, there's no payment support in Bookings." The answer carries no country exception, so it applies in Switzerland as well.

That gap is the reason for the whole flow. Bookings has a public booking page that customers can use directly, and it books the appointment straight away. For a paid appointment, that link would let anyone skip the payment. So we deliberately leave it unused. Our website has its own date and time picker, and Bookings only receives the appointment once the money is in.

## The flow, step by step

Five steps, three systems: our website with a small API behind it, Stripe, and Microsoft Bookings.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Read the free times</h3><p>The booking page asks our API for free 60-minute slots. The API reads availability from Bookings through Microsoft Graph, so Bookings stays the one calendar that knows what is free.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Choose a time and add details</h3><p>The visitor picks a date and time and enters an email address, optionally a name and a short note on which task we should look at.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Pay on Stripe's page</h3><p>The visitor is sent to Stripe Checkout, a payment page hosted by Stripe. Card details are entered there, not on our site.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>The webhook creates the appointment</h3><p>Once the payment has gone through, Stripe sends a signed message to our API. The API checks the signature and creates the Bookings appointment as an online meeting, which gives it a Teams link. The note from step 2 goes into the appointment.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Bookings sends the confirmation</h3><p>The confirmation email with the Teams link comes from Bookings, the same way as for any other Bookings appointment.</p></li>
</ol>

Both Graph pieces are documented by Microsoft: reading [staff availability](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/bookingbusiness-getstaffavailability?view=graph-rest-1.0) and [creating an appointment](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/graph/api/bookingbusiness-post-appointments?view=graph-rest-1.0), where an appointment marked as online comes back with a Teams join link.

## Why the webhook creates the appointment, not the thank-you page

The appointment is created in the webhook because the thank-you page is not reliable. Stripe says so in its [fulfilment guide](https://docs.stripe.com/checkout/fulfillment): you cannot rely on the page after checkout, because a customer can pay and then lose their connection before that page loads. The webhook goes from Stripe to the server directly.

Two more points from Stripe's [webhook documentation](https://docs.stripe.com/webhooks) shaped the flow. In live mode, Stripe retries a failed delivery for up to three days. And the same event can arrive more than once. So creating the appointment has to be safe to run twice for the same payment, and someone has to see when deliveries fail. A paid slot with no appointment is the one failure this flow must never hide.

Checking the signature matters for a different reason. Without it, anyone who found the address could send a fake "paid" message and get an appointment for free.

## What we deliberately left manual

Not every part of a booking is worth automating. We drew the line here:

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>Runs automatically</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Showing free times from Bookings</li>
      <li>Payment, which reserves the slot</li>
      <li>The Bookings appointment with its Teams link</li>
      <li>The confirmation email</li>
      <li>The note on the task, inside the appointment</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Stays manual</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Rescheduling, free up to 12 hours before, by email or phone</li>
      <li>The money-back decision after the appointment</li>
      <li>The refund itself, through Stripe back to the card</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Rescheduling is rare enough to handle by email. The refund depends on a judgement: did the hour turn up something worth automating? A person makes that call, not a rule in code.

One detail we only noticed in use: the Stripe page follows the language of our booking page, but the Bookings confirmation email uses the language set in the Bookings business settings. If you serve customers in two languages, check both.

## When a ready-made booking tool is enough

For many firms a booking tool with payment built in is the better choice, and nobody needs to build anything. That is the case when you have a few appointment types with a standard flow, the tool confirms a booking only after payment, it handles CHF and the way your customers want to pay, and you do not need the appointment in your Microsoft 365 calendar.

Check those points on the vendor's own pages before you commit. Currency is the usual trap for a Swiss firm. Calendly, for example, lists AUD, CAD, EUR, GBP and USD for [payments through Stripe](https://calendly.com/help/calendly-stripe), and CHF is not on that list at the time of writing. Payment methods are the second point. Stripe offers [TWINT](https://docs.stripe.com/payments/twint) in Checkout for customers in Switzerland, but whether a booking tool passes that on is something to check, not assume.

Skip a custom flow, too, if you sell only a handful of paid appointments a month. An invoice after the appointment may be simpler still. And if your customers are businesses that expect an invoice, card payment up front will put some of them off, however well it is built.

## When a custom flow makes sense

A custom flow earns its keep when the booking has to fit systems you already run. In our case the company runs on Microsoft 365, the calendar is Bookings, the meeting is Teams, and payment had to come before confirmation, which Bookings cannot do on its own.

It fits when you already use Bookings and want to keep one calendar, when payment must come before the slot is fixed, and when the booking should carry information further, such as a note into the appointment. It also fits when the booking page should sit on your own site, in your own languages.

Plan the running costs as well. Stripe charges per payment, with no set-up or monthly fee on its [Swiss pricing page](https://stripe.com/en-ch/pricing). Hosting and running an automation we operate costs CHF 50–150 a month, depending on the solution. Someone has to watch for failed webhook deliveries, and the credentials the flow uses to reach Bookings have to be renewed before they expire. Which package size a flow like this would be for you depends on how many systems it touches. That is what the Process Check settles.
