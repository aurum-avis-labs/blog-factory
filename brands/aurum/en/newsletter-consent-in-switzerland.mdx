## Newsletter consent in Switzerland: UWG and the FADP together

Newsletter consent in Switzerland sits on two laws. The Unfair Competition Act ([Art. 3 para. 1 lit. o UWG](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2003/143/de)) governs promotional e-mail and similar channels: mass advertising without permission or without a narrow existing customer relationship is unfair competition. The FADP governs how you store addresses, protect them, and delete them when someone opts out ([Art. 19 and Art. 8 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)). Marketing and office staff in an SME of about 20 to 250 people need both in mind. This is not a copy of German GDPR newsletter templates.

What belongs in the [privacy notice](/security/blog/privacy-notice-requirement-switzerland) is its own topic. How to send personal data by e-mail without guessing each time is in [sending personal data by e-mail](/security/blog/sending-personal-data-by-email).

## B2C, B2B and "we already know them"

For consumers, the safe route is clear consent before the first promotional send. "They once asked a question" does not justify monthly product news unrelated to that question.

For B2B addresses taken from a contract or an ongoing supply relationship, you may often inform about similar services if opt-out is easy and you document the relationship. Once the mail reads like cold outreach, that basis is gone and you are back to consent as for B2C.

## What the sign-up form should say

The text should state plainly that you will send advertising, not only "news" or "updates". Name the rough rhythm (monthly, quarterly) and link to the privacy notice. A pre-ticked marketing box is risky. Better: active confirmation, its own field, no fine print buried in general terms.

If someone types an address from a paper form at a fair, you need the same evidence as online: photo or scan of the form, date, and who collected it. "A colleague noted it back then" rarely survives a complaint.

## Double opt-in and evidence in practice

Double opt-in is not its own line in the UWG. It is good practice: a confirmation e-mail before the first newsletter goes out. It helps in disputes because you can show timestamp, IP if proportionate, and the wording on the form.

Keep evidence where marketing and privacy can find it: an export from the tool, a screenshot of the form plus log, or a short internal memo with date and owner. A CRM tick without text rarely survives a complaint to the FDPIC or a UWG dispute.

## Lists you should not import

Purchased address lists, fair contacts without documented advertising consent, and profiles scraped from the web are the most common mistake in small teams that want to "just test once". Former employees sit on a separate list. Without fresh marketing consent they stay off it.

If an agency delivers "qualified leads", ask in writing where consent is stored and how you would produce it on complaint. If the answer is vague, do not import.

## Storage, security and unsubscribe

The FADP requires appropriate technical and organisational measures ([Art. 8 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)) and transparent information about processing ([Art. 19 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)). Anyone who signs up should understand what the address is for and how to withdraw. For newsletters that means: only fields you need, access for few roles, a secure tool with passwords and roles. Delete opt-outs and hard bounces from active lists promptly. Someone marked suppressed in the tool must not reappear through a careless CSV import.

If the newsletter provider hosts abroad, say so in your [privacy notice](/security/blog/privacy-notice-requirement-switzerland). The notice should state plainly what the address is used for and how to object.

Every send needs a working unsubscribe link. Test it quarterly with an internal test account. Opt-out must work without login and without a justification.

## When someone says they never signed up

Keep consent logs and headers from the first confirmation e-mail. If someone replies angrily, answer factually, check the tool entry and remove the address from all active lists at once. Disputing proof without producing it escalates the case. If the facts are unclear, get advice before you send again.

## EU contacts and other languages

Recipients in the EU can trigger GDPR duties in addition to Swiss rules, even when your company is in Switzerland. This article centres on Switzerland. EU lists or multilingual campaigns without review are a separate project.

## A made-up example

A made-up example: a 40-person services firm in Aargau wants to put "all fair contacts" into the newsletter. Marketing stops the import. Instead the admin writes half a page of rules: double opt-in only via the website form, one owner, yearly check of the suppression list, no send to addresses from the old Excel "prospects 2019". Reception gets ten minutes in the team meeting on what to do when someone asks "can I use this e-mail".

## Who owns this in the company

Marketing wants to send, IT runs the tool, HR still has former staff on an old list. Name one person who holds consent and opt-outs together, even if they do not write every newsletter. Without a name, complaints land on the managing director at 6 p.m. without a log.

## Training and next step

New staff on reception need a clear "no" to list imports without proof on day one. A live data protection session with your forms and CRM fields goes deeper than a PDF in a folder, in one to two hours. See [data protection awareness training](/security/datenschutz).
