## Choosing an automation partner: six checks before you sign

You are about to hire someone to automate a recurring office task and you have no IT department to judge the offer. A short checklist before you sign pays off: price basis, who builds and who runs it, data flow, tests and documentation, exit, references. Then ask whether a small specialist is the right kind of partner at all. For some firms the existing IT provider or an internal owner fits better.

We are one such small provider, so read the checks as a list to hold every offer against, ours included. What an automation costs is in [what automation costs in Switzerland](/prozess-check/blog/what-automation-costs-switzerland). Running it afterwards is in [who runs the automation](/prozess-check/blog/who-runs-the-automation).

## Fixed price or hourly rate?

For one clearly bounded workflow, a fixed price against a written scope is easier for an SME to judge, because the risk of an overrun sits with the provider. Hourly billing fits work whose scope cannot be known in advance.

| | Fixed price | Hourly rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who carries an overrun | The provider | You |
| Fits | One bounded workflow you can describe | Open exploration, unclear systems |
| What to ask | What the written scope contains and what counts as a change | An estimate, a cap and a weekly statement of hours |
| Watch for | A vague scope that only moves the argument to the end | An estimate that keeps growing with no cap |

With us the price is fixed for the scope agreed in writing. Tests are always included and nothing is billed by the hour. The guide price for one bounded workflow with few systems is CHF 3'200–4'800. What the written scope should state (where the workflow starts, where it ends, which systems it touches and what stays manual) is in [after the Process Check](/prozess-check/blog/after-the-process-check).

## Who builds it, and who runs it?

Ask for names. At the first meeting you should hear who writes the code, whether anyone works as a subcontractor and who runs the automation after go-live.

With a small provider you know exactly who does the work. You carry a key-person risk. With a larger provider you get a team that can cover for each other, and often several layers between you and the person who builds. What matters is that you know which pattern you are buying.

If personal data is involved, the nDSG adds a rule: a processor may pass the processing on to a third party only with your prior approval ([Art. 9 para. 3 DSG](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)). So ask who else touches your data.

With us, Robert Schlittler develops the automations himself and runs them afterwards, for CHF 50–150 a month depending on the solution, plus AI service costs by usage. You can cancel the running at any time. Ask us, as you would anyone, who steps in when the builder is away. The rest of the running questions are in [who runs the automation](/prozess-check/blog/who-runs-the-automation).

## Where is your data processed?

Ask which systems your data passes through, in which country, and which AI service reads it. A document in an automation usually passes through the automation itself, the platform it runs on and often an external AI service. You should be able to get all three named.

For personal data, the nDSG applies. You may hand the processing to a provider if it processes the data only as you would be allowed to, and you must satisfy yourself that the provider can keep the data secure ([Art. 9 DSG](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)). Disclosure abroad has its own conditions ([Art. 16 DSG](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)). These are practical questions for the first meeting, not legal advice. Which contract you need is for your data protection adviser or lawyer.

Riccardo Conti, our co-founder, is responsible for security and data protection. You do not have to hand over customer data to judge a provider: describing the workflow, or using a redacted or made-up example, is enough for the Process Check.

## Tests, documentation and exit

Ask what "done" means, in writing. Tests should run on examples your contact person knows well. The documentation should be enough for someone else to take over.

In our packages tests are always included. Documentation and handover to your team are part of the Large package. The five questions a good documentation answers, and the exit questions (whose accounts it runs on, what you receive if you cancel), are in [who runs the automation](/prozess-check/blog/who-runs-the-automation). Ask them before you sign. A partner who cannot answer them in writing has told you something.

## References you can verify

Ask for something you can look at, not a slide of logos. A verifiable reference is a system you can see working, a case described in enough detail to check, or a person you can call. Ask which of your systems the partner has connected before, and accept "not yet" as an honest answer.

Here is what we can show. The booking for our own Process Check reads free slots from Microsoft Bookings through Microsoft Graph, sends the visitor to Stripe Checkout, and a Stripe webhook then creates the appointment with its Teams link. It is described in [automating appointment booking with payment](/prozess-check/blog/booking-with-payment-case). KitchenCrew is a product we built for Swiss restaurants: teams type a shopping list in a chat and receive order drafts per supplier in their inbox. A pilot is running with Swiss restaurants, and the [portfolio page](/portfolio/kitchencrew) has the case.

Beyond that, we have built with Microsoft 365 (Graph, Outlook, Bookings, Teams), Google Workspace (Sheets, Apps Script, Drive) and Stripe. We have not yet built integrations with bexio, Abacus or industry-specific software. An automation can work around such programs. What your program offers in the way of an interface is clarified in the Process Check.

## Red flags

Six signs that an offer is not ready to sign.

<ul class="blog-scenarios">
  <li><h3>A tool comes first</h3><p>The pitch names a platform before anyone has asked what your task is, who does it and how often.</p></li>
  <li><h3>ROI percentages without a method</h3><p>"80 percent less effort" needs a baseline: which task, counted how, over what period.</p></li>
  <li><h3>Strategy slides without a workflow</h3><p>A roadmap with no single task described from start to end, with systems and someone who checks the result.</p></li>
</ul>

Other warning signs: a first project that covers five processes; no written scope or no named person on either side; integrations promised when nobody has checked that your software has an interface at all.

## Questions for the first meeting

Take these to every provider, and write down the answers.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Is the price fixed against a written scope, and what counts as a change?</li>
  <li>Who writes it, does anyone work as a subcontractor, and who steps in when the builder is away?</li>
  <li>Who runs it after go-live, what does that cost per month, and can I cancel at any time?</li>
  <li>Where is my data processed, and which AI service reads it?</li>
  <li>Which tests are included, on whose examples, and who signs off?</li>
  <li>What documentation and handover do I get, and what do I receive if I leave?</li>
  <li>Which of my systems have you connected before, and what can I look at or whom can I call?</li>
  <li>What would you advise me not to automate?</li>
</ul>

The last question is often the most useful. A partner who finds something not to automate is working on your problem, not on the order.

A small specialist fits when you have one bounded workflow, no IT team, and you want it built for a fixed price and looked after. Then the checklist above applies to us as it does to anyone else.
