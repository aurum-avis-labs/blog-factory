## Choosing an automation partner: what SMEs should check

Check six things before you sign with an automation partner: whether the price is fixed against a written scope, who builds the automation and who runs it, where your data is processed, which tests and documentation are included, what you receive if you leave, and which references you can verify. Then ask whether a small specialist is the right kind of partner at all. For some firms a larger IT provider or an internal person is the better choice, and a section below says which.

This is for owners, managing directors and office leads in a Swiss SME who are about to hire someone to automate a recurring task and have no IT department to judge the offer. We are one such provider, so read the checks as a list to hold every offer against, ours included. What an automation costs is in [what automation costs in Switzerland](/blog/what-automation-costs-switzerland). Running it afterwards is in [who runs the automation](/blog/who-runs-the-automation).

## Fixed price or hourly rate?

For one clearly bounded workflow, a fixed price against a written scope is easier for an SME to judge, because the risk of an overrun sits with the provider. Hourly billing fits work whose scope cannot be known in advance.

| | Fixed price | Hourly rate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who carries an overrun | The provider | You |
| Fits | One bounded workflow you can describe | Open exploration, unclear systems |
| What to ask | What the written scope contains and what counts as a change | An estimate, a cap and a weekly statement of hours |
| Watch for | A vague scope that only moves the argument to the end | An estimate that keeps growing with no cap |

With us the price is fixed for the scope agreed in writing. Tests are always included and nothing is billed by the hour. The guide price for one bounded workflow with few systems is CHF 3'200–4'800. What the written scope should state (where the workflow starts, where it ends, which systems it touches and what stays manual) is in [after the Process Check](/blog/after-the-process-check).

## Who builds it, and who runs it?

Ask for names. At the first meeting you should hear who writes the code, whether anyone works as a subcontractor and who runs the automation after go-live.

With a small provider you know exactly who does the work, and you carry a key-person risk. With a larger provider you get a team that can cover for each other, and often several layers between you and the person who builds. Neither is wrong. What matters is that you know which one you are buying.

If personal data is involved, the nDSG adds a rule: a processor may pass the processing on to a third party only with your prior approval ([Art. 9 para. 3 DSG](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)). So ask who else touches your data.

With us, Robert Schlittler develops the automations himself and runs them afterwards, for CHF 50–150 a month depending on the solution, plus AI service costs by usage. You can cancel the running at any time. We are a small provider, so ask us, as you would anyone, who steps in when the builder is away. The rest of the running questions are in [who runs the automation](/blog/who-runs-the-automation).

## Where is your data processed?

Ask which systems your data passes through, in which country, and which AI service reads it. A document in an automation usually passes through the automation itself, the platform it runs on and often an external AI service. You should be able to get all three named.

For personal data, the nDSG applies. You may hand the processing to a provider if it processes the data only as you would be allowed to, and you must satisfy yourself that the provider can keep the data secure ([Art. 9 DSG](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)). Disclosure abroad has its own conditions ([Art. 16 DSG](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de)). These are practical questions for the first meeting, not legal advice. Which contract you need is for your data protection adviser or lawyer.

Riccardo Conti, our co-founder, is responsible for security and data protection. And you do not have to hand over customer data to judge a provider: describing the workflow, or using a redacted or made-up example, is enough for the Process Check.

## Tests, documentation and exit

Ask what "done" means, in writing. Tests should run on examples your contact person knows well, and the documentation should be enough for someone else to take over.

In our packages tests are always included. Documentation and handover to your team are part of the Large package. The five questions a good documentation answers, and the exit questions (whose accounts it runs on, what you receive if you cancel), are in [who runs the automation](/blog/who-runs-the-automation). Ask them before you sign. A partner who cannot answer them in writing has told you something.

## References you can verify

Ask for something you can look at, not a slide of logos. A verifiable reference is a system you can see working, a case described in enough detail to check, or a person you can call. Ask which of your systems the partner has connected before, and accept "not yet" as an honest answer.

## Red flags

Six signs that an offer is not ready to sign.

- **A tool comes first.** The pitch names a platform before anyone has asked what your task is, who does it and how often.
- **ROI percentages without a method.** "80 percent less effort" means something only with a baseline: which task, counted how, over what period.
- **Strategy slides without a workflow.** A roadmap that never describes one task from start to end, with the systems it touches and who checks the result.
- **Everything at once.** A first project that covers five processes. One process with an owner is the way in.
- **No written scope.** Or no named person on either side.
- **Integrations promised unseen.** Nobody has checked that your software has an interface at all.

## Questions for the first meeting

Take these to every provider, and write down the answers.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Is the price fixed against a written scope, and what counts as a change?</li>
  <li>Who writes it, does anyone work as a subcontractor, and who steps in when the builder is away?</li>
  <li>Who runs it after go-live, what does that cost per month, and can I cancel at any time?</li>
  <li>Where is my data processed, and which AI service reads it?</li>
  <li>Which tests are included, on whose examples, and who signs off?</li>
  <li>What documentation and handover do I get, and what do I receive if I leave?</li>
  <li>Which of my systems have you connected before, and what can I look at or whom can I call?</li>
  <li>What would you advise me not to automate?</li>
</ul>

The last question is the most useful. A partner who finds something not to automate is working on your problem, not on the order.

## When a larger IT provider or an internal person is the better choice

Sometimes another kind of partner fits better, and then you should not hire a small specialist, us included.

- **You already have an IT provider** that runs your Microsoft 365 tenant, workstations and support, and the automation would live inside that tenant. Start with them. They know your set-up and already have the access.
- **You need guaranteed response times** or round-the-clock support, or the automation touches core systems such as your ERP or your identity management. A larger provider has people to cover for each other.
- **You expect many small automations** over the years, and someone in the firm has the time and the interest. An internal owner then costs less than several monthly fees.

A small specialist fits when you have one bounded workflow, no IT team, and you want it built for a fixed price and looked after.
