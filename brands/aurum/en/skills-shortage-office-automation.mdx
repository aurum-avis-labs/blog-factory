## Office skills shortage: automate instead of hiring?

Automate the repetitive part of the work and hire for the rest: the two answers do not compete. Automation takes over steps that repeat and follow rules, such as copying data between systems or assembling a weekly report. It does not replace a person, and it does not produce the specialist you cannot find. There is also a caveat about the premise. The latest Swiss figures show a surplus of commercial and administrative staff overall, not a shortage. So the reason to automate a task is the task itself, not the labour market.

This is for owners and office leads in a Swiss SME who hear "skills shortage" everywhere and wonder whether automation is the answer to a vacancy. It draws on three studies. Every number below is the authors' own, not ours.

## What the figures say about office roles

Nationally, commercial and administrative roles currently show more supply than demand. The [Skills Shortage Index 2025](https://www.adeccogroup.com/en-ch/future-of-work/swiss-skills-shortage/swiss-skills-shortage-2025) from Adecco Group Switzerland and the University of Zurich, published in November 2025, sits about 22 percent below the previous year. Only 4 of 32 occupational groups still show a clear imbalance: health professions, site management, technical specialists and electronics. For commercial, administrative and ICT roles the study reports the sharpest drop in vacancies and rising unemployment, which it describes as an oversupply of skilled workers.

The [Adecco Job Index for Q2 2026](https://www.adeccogroup.com/-/media/project/adecco-group/switzerland/job-index/job-index-q2-2026/job_index_q2_2026_medienmitteilung.pdf), published in July 2026, adds a twist. Vacancies for skilled commercial, administrative and trade staff fell 13 percent year on year. Vacancies for office roles rose 9 percent, mostly for general office and secretarial staff. Adecco adds that the effect of AI differs strongly by job profile.

The SME view sounds different. In the [AXA SME labour market study 2025](https://www.axa.ch/de/ueber-axa/medien/medienmitteilungen/aktuelle-medienmitteilungen/2025/20250723-kmu-arbeitsmarktstudie-2025.html), run by Sotomo among 300 SMEs in March 2025, 44 percent mostly or always had trouble filling vacancies and another 40 percent partly. What is hard to find is experienced, highly specialised staff (83 percent) and managers (68 percent). Professionals without work experience were mostly rated easy to find.

Both pictures fit together. Hiring is hard for specialists and managers. For routine office work, the market looks easier on average. Averages say nothing about your region or your job advert, so a failed search of your own counts. But they are a reason not to buy automation because of a headline.

## Automation relieves work, it does not replace people

Automation takes a repeated step off a person's desk, not the role. Two examples from our Process Check offer: service technicians fill in their reports on the phone and an invoice draft appears that the office only checks. A weekly ERP evaluation is produced overnight and sits in the inbox on Monday morning. In both cases a person still checks the draft and reads the evaluation. What disappears is the retyping and the assembling.

Freed hours are not a vacancy. A clearly hypothetical example: in an office of four, a total of six hours a week goes on copying orders from emails into the ERP. Automating that does not replace a colleague, because six hours is less than a sixth of a full-time post. What you get is six hours for work that is currently left undone. Whether those hours are worth the cost is the arithmetic in [when does automation pay off for a small business](/blog/when-automation-pays-off-small-business). How to work it through for one task is in [calculating the benefit of an automation](/blog/calculate-automation-benefit).

If the vacancy is a full role, automation rarely closes it. It can make the role smaller, and sometimes that is the difference between hiring and not hiring.

## Which office tasks to hand over

Hand over tasks that repeat, follow rules and already exist in digital form. Keep with people everything that needs judgement.

## What does not work: automating a task nobody owns

Automation does not fix a task without an owner. It repeats today's mess faster. If three people are "sort of responsible", the columns change name every month and nobody can say what a correct result looks like, there is nothing to automate yet.

The [adesso GenAI Impact Report 2026 for Switzerland](https://www.adesso.ch/adesso-ch/whitepaper/genai-impact-report-2026-ch.pdf), a survey of 100 executives, names poor data quality and missing processes and governance as the biggest hurdles, 32 percent each. That is adesso's finding, not ours. It matches office life: the obstacle is rarely the tool.

Two more traps. One is buying automation to close a vacancy that is really judgement work: the automation does not do that work, and the position stays open. The other is waiting for the tool to organise the work for you. Before anything is built, one named person owns the task, says what a correct result looks like, checks the output and answers when the input changes.

## When hiring is the better answer

Hire when the work needs judgement, contact with people or specialist knowledge, or when the hours you would free do not add up to anything. Automation does not produce a specialist or a manager, and according to AXA those are exactly the roles SMEs find hardest to fill. Hire or train in that case, and use automation to make the role smaller.

The same goes for tasks that are rare or different every time. There is little to automate, and a checklist is cheaper.

## What to do first

List the routine tasks of the person who is overloaded, or of the role you want to fill. For each one, write down how often it comes up, how long it takes, who is involved and who owns it. Cross out everything that needs judgement. What is left are your candidates. Count one of them for a week.
