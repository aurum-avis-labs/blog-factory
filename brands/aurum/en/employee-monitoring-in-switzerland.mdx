## Employee monitoring in Switzerland

Employee monitoring in Switzerland is allowed when it serves a clear purpose and is not mainly there to control behaviour. [Art. 26 of the Labour Ordinance (ArGV 3)](https://www.seco.admin.ch/dam/seco/de/dokumente/Arbeit/Arbeitsbedingungen/Arbeitsgesetz%20und%20Verordnungen/Wegleitungen/Wegleitungen%203/ArGV3_art26.pdf.download.pdf/ArGV3_art26_de.pdf) forbids surveillance systems intended to monitor how staff behave. The FDPIC (EDÖB) expects prior information, proportionality, and a job-related purpose for employee personal data ([Art. 328b of the Code of Obligations](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/1996/236/de#art_328_b), [guidance on technical monitoring at work](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/technische-mittel-zur-uberwachung-am-arbeitsplatz)). CCTV in the office is a separate topic (CCTV at work in Switzerland). This text is for owners and HR in companies of roughly 20 to 250 people who wonder whether keystroke loggers, screen capture, or «productivity scores» are acceptable.

After a hiring round, retention of application data is covered in [how long to keep job applications](/security/blog/how-long-to-keep-job-applications-switzerland).

## Is monitoring mandatory?

No. The FADP does not prescribe a specific monitoring product. It requires appropriate measures for data security. Logs on company devices may be part of that; always-on screen video for performance league tables usually is not. Insurers and customers sometimes ask for «awareness» or «access control». That is not the same as an «employee monitoring» module with hourly screenshots.

## What counts as problematic monitoring

Continuous screen recording, keystroke logging on every app, or ranking individuals by mouse activity often look like behaviour control rather than IT security. Tools marketed as «employee productivity» deserve a hard look: which fields are collected, who sees named reports, and whether less intrusive options exist (project time tracking with consent, access logs for security incidents only).

WhatsApp chats used for business are a data protection issue too (WhatsApp for business and data protection), not a monitoring substitute.

## What employers still may do

Security monitoring on company devices (malware detection, login anomalies, DLP rules on confidential files) is common if staff are informed in an acceptable-use policy and measures are proportionate. Phishing simulations are a different debate: announce them and do not use click scores in individual performance reviews, as set out in [clicked a phishing link at work](/security/blog/clicked-a-phishing-link-at-work).

## BYOD and private phones

When staff use private devices, the job-related purpose test is stricter. Company mail on a private phone may justify security updates and a clear separation, not reading private chats. State what applies on company equipment and what you do not monitor on personal devices. Without a written rule, BYOD becomes a dispute quickly.

## Who can defer a formal policy

A five-person office with no remote monitoring and no plans to buy surveillance software can note «no behaviour monitoring» in the staff handbook and revisit when they grow.

A 120-person firm rolling out global «productivity analytics» needs a written assessment, staff involvement where Swiss law requires it, and privacy documentation before rollout. Switzerland does not have a German-style works council in most SMEs; still talk to HR and to the people who will open the dashboards.

## What to document before you switch a tool on

Write down the purpose (security incident, loss prevention, or performance), the data fields collected, retention period, and which roles may open named dashboards. If the vendor stores data outside Switzerland, note that in your processing records under [Art. 12 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de). Staff should receive the summary in plain language before logging starts, not only in a 40-page IT policy.

## A made-up example: a productivity module after the pitch

A made-up example: a services firm with 45 staff and outsourced IT. A line manager wants «productivity insights» after a vendor pitch because home office «feels too loose». HR lists existing logs with IT: VPN, Microsoft 365 sign-ins, contract time tracking already in place. The new module would store screenshots every ten minutes and «activity scores» per person.

Management stops the purchase. Instead: update the acceptable-use policy (what applies on company laptops), tighten project time tracking, and plan a data protection session that explains why browsing history is not the fix. Endpoint protection stays, without screen video.

## Employee access requests

Under the FADP, staff may ask what personal data you hold about them, including logs if they exist. If you cannot explain what a monitoring product stores, you are not ready to deploy it. Keep exports from HR and IT tools separate so a subject access request does not accidentally disclose another employee’s records.

  <h3>Does home office change the rules?</h3>
  <p>Company laptops on a home network may still run security software you disclosed. Cameras or always-on recording in a private room without a clear rule is higher risk. State what applies on company equipment and what you do not monitor on personal devices.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is keylogging allowed for IT support?</h3>
  <p>Support with the person’s consent and a clear end date is different from permanent logging of all staff. Without consent and a narrow purpose, it is risky. Ask counsel if unsure.</p>
</div>

## First steps for management

List existing tools: time tracking, VPN logs, MDM, collaboration. Mark whether each tool serves security or performance control. Remove duplicates before you buy a new «productivity» module. Ask IT for a configuration export, not only the marketing brochure.

## Data protection training

A live data protection session covers what staff may use for customer contact, what the company may log on devices, and where monitoring crosses the line, in one to two hours. Details are on [data protection awareness training](/security/datenschutz).

Surveillance meant to control behaviour is tightly limited at work in Switzerland. Inform people beforehand, stay with a job-related purpose, and do not score individuals for performance reviews. This is orientation under ArGV 3, OR 328b and the FADP, not a legal opinion.
