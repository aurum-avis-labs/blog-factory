## Turn a service into a digital product

You turn a service into a digital product when you package one repeatable outcome so a customer can trigger it, track it, or receive it without your team renegotiating the scope each time. That is not the same as digitising paperwork: electronic invoices, a shared drive, and a booking link in an email signature leave the know-how in people's heads. A product holds the rules, the hand-offs, and the status in software someone outside your firm can use with modest onboarding.

This is for owners and innovation leads in Swiss SMEs that sell expertise (consulting, technical service, care, logistics support, training delivery) and wonder whether an app, a client portal, or a small SaaS line could scale what already works in meetings and phone calls. If the question is internal only, read [validate a product idea inside a company](/blog/validate-a-product-idea-inside-a-company). If the board expects stage gates before budget moves, [stage gate vs lean startup](/blog/stage-gate-vs-lean-startup) explains how a measured pilot fits a gate without pretending a slide deck is evidence. When you are ready to hire build capacity, the partner checklist is in [getting an MVP built for you](/blog/getting-an-mvp-built-for-you).

## Productise one job, not the whole firm

Start from a job your best customers already pay for, expressed as an outcome:

- **Trigger**: what the customer does to start (upload files, answer five questions, pick a slot).
- **Rules**: what your team decides every time (eligibility, pricing band, which colleague owns the case).
- **Deliverable**: what lands in their inbox, dashboard, or account when you are done.
- **Status**: where they see progress without calling you.

If you cannot name those four without a two-hour workshop, the first product is too wide. Narrow until one corridor of your service could run as a pilot for ten customers.

<aside class="blog-callout">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Note</span>
  <p>A portal that only hosts PDFs you already email is document storage. A product changes who does the repetitive thinking: the software applies your checklist, assigns work, or produces the artefact your fee used to buy as labour hours.</p>
</aside>

## How to turn your service into a digital product

Treat the first release as a learning instrument, not a replacement for every revenue line.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Map the paid path on paper</h3><p>Follow three recent jobs from first contact to invoice. Circle steps that repeat verbatim and steps that need senior judgement. Only the repeats belong in v1.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Choose the buyer and the price shape</h3><p>Same firm as today, or a adjacent segment? Subscription, per job, or credits? If you cannot describe who pays without naming a single hero client, pause before code.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Define the smallest self-serve surface</h3><p>Often a web app with login, one workflow, and notifications. Native apps and marketplaces come later unless mobility is the job itself.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Run a manual or concierge slice first when the risk is value</h3><p>If you are unsure anyone will pay for the outcome without your face in the room, deliver the same result by hand for a month and log drop-off. Software follows proof, not the other way around.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Ship with measurement from day one</h3><p>Record who starts, who finishes, where they stall, and whether they return without a reminder call. Opinions from your sales team are not a funnel.</p></li>
  <li data-step="6"><h3>Write what v1 deliberately excludes</h3><p>Payments, multi-language, integrations, and compliance workflows you have not tested with a lawyer belong on an out-of-scope list you show to the board.</p></li>
</ol>

## What a first digital product proves, and what it does not

<ul class="blog-facts">
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Proves</span><strong>Repeat demand</strong><p>Strangers or existing clients complete the workflow without you rebuilding the offer each time.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Proves</span><strong>Rule clarity</strong><p>Your exceptions surface early; you know which decisions must stay human.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Does not prove</span><strong>Full margin</strong><p>Support load, hosting, and revision requests may eat the spread you had on site visits.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Does not prove</span><strong>Market size</strong><p>Ten loyal service clients using a portal is not TAM. It is a pilot cohort.</p></li>
</ul>

Ideas as such are not protectable in Switzerland; execution, contracts, and brand are ([IGE on ideas](https://www.ige.ch/de/uebersicht-geistiges-eigentum/ein-leitfaden-fuer-innovative-und-kreative/idee/koennen-ideen-geschuetzt-werden)). If employees build the prototype on company time, employer ownership of the software is the default under Swiss practice ([software ownership](https://www.rentschpartner.ch/ict-law/schutz-von-software)); external authors need a written assignment before you treat the repo as yours.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>Turning a service into a digital product tests whether a packaged outcome travels without you in the room. It does not prove regulated compliance, partner integrations, or that you can acquire customers cheaper than your field team. Those need their own scoped tests.</p>
</aside>

## A made-up example: compliance checklists for installers

A made-up example: a training and audit firm in eastern Switzerland serves installation companies with annual safety documentation. Today an expert visits sites, fills paper checklists, and emails PDF summaries. Leadership wants «digitalisation». They resist building a generic document vault.

They pick one outcome: a customer uploads photos and serial numbers; within 48 hours they receive a signed checklist PDF with open items highlighted. For six weeks they run a concierge pilot: a Typeform intake and a human reviewer, priced per site at CHF 180. Eight firms from the existing client list join; five complete the upload without a reminder call. Two ask only for storage, not the review, which tells them the value is the judgement, not the folder.

Version one of the product is a logged-in upload, a status page, and the same PDF template generated automatically for the easy 70 percent of cases. Experts still handle disputes manually. The firm does not claim SaaS multiples. They claim that five of eight completed without chasing, and that automation should start at validation rules, not at a mobile app store launch. The board gets a Go, Pivot, or Stop note with that cohort named explicitly.

## Who should productise now, and who should wait

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>Who this is for</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>SMEs with a repeatable deliverable clients already rebuy on a schedule</li>
      <li>Teams that can name the workflow steps juniors perform today</li>
      <li>Firms willing to narrow the first release to one buyer type and one price shape</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Who should wait or stay manual</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Businesses where every job is bespoke and senior partners sell on relationships alone</li>
      <li>Owners who want an app mainly because competitors mention «AI» in brochures</li>
      <li>Organisations that have not stabilised the service quality on site; software will encode chaos</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>
