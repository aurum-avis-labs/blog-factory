## How to document a process before you automate it

To document a process before you automate it, sit down for about 30 minutes with the person who does it today, go through eight details, and record one real run on screen. The eight details are trigger, steps, systems, decisions, exceptions, result, owner and frequency. Give the exceptions the most attention, because that is where an automation breaks in daily use.

This is for owners, office managers and team leads who have a recurring task that lives in one person's head. You do not need a flowchart, a method or any software. One page of text and one recording are enough.

Which process to take first is covered in [which process should you automate first](/blog/which-process-to-automate-first). This article is the next step: writing down the one you chose so that someone else can follow it. The page is useful even if you never automate anything. It works as the cover arrangement you never had, and it shows where two people do the same task differently.

## The eight details that are enough

Eight details describe a process well enough that a person who has never done it could take it over on Monday. Write in your own words, the way people talk in the office.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Trigger: what starts it? An email, a date, a form, a phone call</li>
  <li>Steps: what happens next, in which order? One sentence per step</li>
  <li>Systems: where does each step happen? Inbox, Excel, portal, accounting, paper</li>
  <li>Decisions: where does someone decide, and by which rule?</li>
  <li>Exceptions: what deviates, and what happens then?</li>
  <li>Result: what exists at the end, where, and who needs it?</li>
  <li>Owner: who does it, who covers, who checks?</li>
  <li>Frequency: how often a month does it come up, and how long does one run take?</li>
</ul>

For systems, count anything where something is looked up or stored, including the Excel file on someone's own laptop and the folder of templates. For frequency, look at the last three months instead of guessing. Together with the duration and the question of who needs the result, that is the sum that tells you whether automating pays off.

## How to spend the half hour

Two people work better than one. The person who does the task talks, and a second person asks questions and takes notes. Someone who has done a task for years skips steps that have become second nature. A person asking questions notices them.

Five minutes: pick one run from last week, with its files, meaning the last email and the last export. What counts is how it went, not how it should go.

Ten minutes: go through the eight details, trigger and result first, then the steps in between.

Ten minutes: record one real run. Five minutes: read the page aloud and mark every spot where someone says "it depends".

Stop after 30 minutes, even if gaps remain. A marked gap is a result. It shows what only one person knows about the task today.

## Why exceptions matter more than the steps

Exceptions show what an automation has to handle and where it should stop and ask a person. The whole team knows the steps of the normal case. The exceptions are often known only to the person who does the task, and they do not mention them unprompted because to them they are routine. A rule that only knows the normal case is wrong at the first case that deviates.

Three questions bring them out:

- "When did this last not go the usual way, and what did you do?"
- "What do you do when a detail is missing or wrong?"
- Ask the person who receives the result as well: "What do you regularly send back?"

Then take the last ten runs and look for the places where someone queried, corrected or added something by hand. Work from the files, not from memory.

Write each exception in three parts: if this happens, that follows, and this person decides. If the exception can be put as a rule, an implementation can take it over. If someone decides by feel, that step stays with a person, and the automation puts the case in front of them. When a growing list of exceptions argues against automating at all, that is covered in [when AI makes the process worse](/blog/when-ai-makes-the-process-worse).

## The recording: one real run on screen

Record a real run, with real files, at normal speed. Described from memory, small actions go missing: which window opens next, where a value is copied from, which file is open on the side. The recording shows them. It does not have to look good or run longer than ten minutes. One run is enough.

You do not need to install anything. On Windows, the [Snipping Tool](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/use-snipping-tool-to-capture-screenshots) records video: the Windows logo key, Shift and R open the recording overlay. Microsoft has [deprecated](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/steps-recorder-deprecation) the older Steps Recorder and points to the Snipping Tool, among others.

On a Mac, Shift, Command and 5 open the [screenshot toolbar](https://support.apple.com/en-us/102618), which records the screen and lets you choose a microphone. Talk while you work if your tool records sound. Otherwise jot down what stands out.

The recording will show real names, amounts and addresses. Show it only to people who see the process anyway, and do not upload it to a free AI tool. If personal data is involved, ask your data-protection adviser who may keep and view the recording. For the Process Check you do not need customer data: a blacked-out or made-up example is enough, or you simply describe the process.

## An example: expense claims in a company of 25

A hypothetical example: in a company of 25 people, the office manager processes the expense claims every month. After the half hour, her sheet looks like this.

| Detail | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | By the 5th of the month, staff email photos of their receipts |
| Steps | Save receipts to a folder, enter them in the Excel list, match against the card statement, hand the list to the managing director for approval, pay out with salary |
| Systems | Inbox, Excel, the card provider's portal, accounting software, a cost-centre list on the office manager's laptop |
| Decisions | Category per receipt. Approval by the managing director from CHF 200 |
| Exceptions | Receipt missing: signed self-made receipt. Foreign currency: rate of the statement date. Client meals: note the guests' names. For two people who travel a lot, something is regularly missing: the office manager asks first |
| Result | Approved list in the accounting software, payout with salary |
| Owner | Office manager. Cover: nobody |
| Frequency | Monthly, about three hours |

In conversation, the office manager named four systems. On the recording, she opens the cost-centre list on her laptop before every receipt, and it was in nobody's list. That she always asks two people first only came up on the second pass through the exceptions. Both are on the sheet now: a fifth system and an exception with a rule.

The sheet shows what runs by rules: entering, matching, categorising. It shows exceptions that have a clear rule, and a decision that stays with the managing director. And it shows that nobody covers. Whether automating pays off is then a sum of frequency, duration and benefit.

## When the description is done, and when to skip it

After the 30 minutes, read the page to someone who does not know the process. If they can follow it, the description is done. If they ask about three places, fill in those three and nothing more.

Skip the description if the task comes up twice a year or looks different every time. There is nothing to pin down. If two people tell the same process differently, the description has not failed. That is the finding: someone first has to decide how the process runs, and own it.

Do not document the whole company or every variant. One process, one sheet, one recording.
