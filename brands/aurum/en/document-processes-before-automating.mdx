## Document a process before you automate it

<p class="blog-lede">In about 30 minutes you can pin down one recurring process: eight details on one page and a short screen recording of a real run.</p>

Documenting a process before you automate it does not mean mapping the whole company. It means writing down the one task you plan to improve next so that someone else can follow it. The eight details are trigger, steps, systems, decisions, exceptions, result, owner and frequency. Give the exceptions the most attention, because that is where an automation breaks in daily use.

This is for owners, office managers and team leads who have a recurring task that lives in one person's head. You do not need a flowchart, a method or any software. One page of text and one recording are enough.

Which process to take first is covered in [which process should you automate first](/prozess-check/blog/which-process-to-automate-first). This page is the step after that: holding the chosen process still. It is useful even if you never automate anything. It works as the cover arrangement you never had, and it shows where two people do the same task differently.

The half hour pays off for a recurring task that one person does today: cover for absence, a rough "does it pay off?" sum, preparation for a Check. Skip it when the task comes up twice a year or looks different every time, or when two people tell the same process in conflicting ways and nobody owns the standard.

## The eight details that are enough

Eight details describe a process well enough that a person who has never done it could take it over on Monday. Write in your own words, the way people talk in the office.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Trigger: what starts it? An email, a date, a form, a phone call</li>
  <li>Steps: what happens next, in which order? One sentence per step</li>
  <li>Systems: where does each step happen? Inbox, Excel, portal, accounting, paper</li>
  <li>Decisions: where does someone decide, and by which rule?</li>
  <li>Exceptions: what deviates, and what happens then?</li>
  <li>Result: what exists at the end, where, and who needs it?</li>
  <li>Owner: who does it, who covers, who checks?</li>
  <li>Frequency: how often a month does it come up, and how long does one run take?</li>
</ul>

For systems, count anywhere something is looked up or stored, including personal Excel files and template folders. For frequency, use the last three months, not a guess. With duration and who needs the result, you have a rough pay-off sum. The rule is one process, one sheet, one recording. Variants for other teams do not belong on the same page.

## How to spend the half hour

Two people work better than one. The person who does the task talks, and a second person asks questions and takes notes. Someone who has done a task for years skips steps that have become second nature. A person asking questions notices them.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Pick a run</h3><p>Five minutes: one real run from last week, with the email and export. What counts is how it went, not how it should go.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Work through the eight details</h3><p>Ten minutes: trigger and result first, then the steps in between.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Record on screen</h3><p>Ten minutes: one real run at normal speed.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Mark the gaps</h3><p>Five minutes: read the page aloud. Mark every spot where someone says "it depends", then stop.</p></li>
</ol>

Stop after 30 minutes, even if gaps remain. A marked gap is a result. It shows what only one person knows about the task today.

## Why exceptions matter more than the steps

Exceptions show what an automation has to handle and where it should stop and ask a person. The whole team often knows the steps of the normal case. The exceptions are frequently known only to the person who does the task, and they do not mention them unprompted because to them they are routine. A rule that only knows the normal case is wrong at the first case that deviates.

Three questions bring them out:

- "When did this last not go the usual way, and what did you do?"
- "What do you do when a detail is missing or wrong?"
- Ask the person who receives the result as well: "What do you regularly send back?"

Scan the last ten runs in the files for queries, corrections or manual adds. Write each exception as: if this, then that, this person decides. Rule-like exceptions can go to an implementation; judgment stays with a person. When exceptions pile up against automating, see [when AI makes the process worse](/prozess-check/blog/when-ai-makes-the-process-worse).

## The recording: one real run on screen

Record a real run, with real files, at normal speed. Described from memory, small actions go missing: which window opens next, where a value is copied from, which file is open on the side. The recording shows them. It does not have to look good or run longer than ten minutes. One run is enough.

On Windows, the [Snipping Tool](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/apps/use-snipping-tool-to-capture-screenshots) records video (Windows logo key, Shift and R). On a Mac, Shift, Command and 5 open the [screenshot toolbar](https://support.apple.com/en-gb/102618) with optional microphone.

The recording will show real names, amounts and addresses. Show it only to people who see the process anyway, and do not upload it to a free AI tool. If personal data is involved, ask your data-protection adviser who may keep and view the recording. For the Process Check you do not need customer data: a blacked-out or made-up example is enough, or you simply describe the process.

## An example: expense claims in a company of 25

A hypothetical example: in a company of 25 people, the office manager processes the expense claims every month. After the half hour, her sheet looks like this.

| Detail | Entry |
| --- | --- |
| Trigger | By the 5th of the month, staff email photos of their receipts |
| Steps | Save receipts to a folder, enter them in the Excel list, match against the card statement, hand the list to the managing director for approval, pay out with salary |
| Systems | Inbox, Excel, the card provider's portal, accounting software, a cost-centre list on the office manager's laptop |
| Decisions | Category per receipt. Approval by the managing director from CHF 200 |
| Exceptions | Receipt missing: signed self-made receipt. Foreign currency: rate of the statement date. Client meals: note the guests' names. For two people who travel a lot, something is regularly missing: the office manager asks first |
| Result | Approved list in the accounting software, payout with salary |
| Owner | Office manager. Cover: nobody |
| Frequency | Monthly, about three hours |

On the recording she opens a cost-centre list before every receipt, though conversation named only four systems. A repeat pass on exceptions surfaced that she always chases two travellers first. The sheet now holds a fifth system and a ruled exception, plus cover nobody provides.

## When the description is done

After the 30 minutes, read the page to someone who does not know the process. If they can follow it, the description is done. If they ask about three places, fill in those three and nothing more.

If two people tell the same process differently, the description has not failed. That is the finding: someone first has to decide how the process runs, and own it.
