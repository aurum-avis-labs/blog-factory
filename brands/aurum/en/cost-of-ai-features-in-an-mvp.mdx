## Cost of AI features in an MVP

The cost of AI features in an MVP is mostly variable spend on inference and storage, plus the engineering time to gate, log, and cap those calls. A one-off licence fee is rarely the whole story. You pay per token or per request on the vendor you choose, you may pay for embeddings and a vector index, and you still pay for the human loop when the model is wrong. This page is for founders and innovation leads in Switzerland who want an honest budget before they wire ChatGPT or another API into a validation build, not a price list that goes stale next month.

Model list prices move. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, and Azure publish current rates on their own pricing pages; read the page in the week you ship and bake a buffer for retries, longer prompts, and tests you run yourself. This article does not quote those numbers. If you need a franc figure for the board, build a spreadsheet from your own prompt length, expected sessions, and the vendor page that day.

## What actually shows up on the bill

Think in three buckets: each call to the model, the data you send with it, and the work around the call.

Inference is usually priced on input and output tokens. A short classification on one sentence is cheap. A feature that pastes a ten-page PDF into the context on every click is not. Long chat threads, automatic summarisation of whole inboxes, and "ask anything about this upload" flows multiply tokens fast.

Embeddings and search add a second meter. You pay to turn text into vectors, you pay to store them, and you pay again when you query a hosted index. For an MVP that only needs search across fifty uploaded documents, a small managed service is often enough. For thousands of tenants with private corpora, the architecture choice matters more than the model brand.

Guardrails are the third cost people forget. Rate limits, content filters, logging, and a human review queue are not line items on the API invoice, but they decide whether a viral post becomes a five-figure week. Budget alerts on the cloud account are part of the feature, not an ops afterthought.

## A checklist before you turn the feature on

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Write the shortest prompt that still works; measure tokens on ten real inputs, not on lorem ipsum.</li>
  <li>Log request id, user id, model name, and token counts on every call in staging and production.</li>
  <li>Set a monthly budget alert and a per-user or per-day cap in application code.</li>
  <li>Cache identical questions for a few minutes if your product allows it.</li>
  <li>Keep a non-AI path for the core job so the app still works when the API is slow or down.</li>
  <li>Decide what you will do when the model hallucinates; manual review beats silent wrong answers.</li>
</ul>

## A made-up example: weekly menu drafts

A made-up example: a founder in Lucerne tests whether small event caterers will accept AI-drafted supplier orders from a short menu list. Ten caterers get access for two weeks. Each draft calls the API twice: once to turn three bullet ingredients into line items, once to polish the email text. Early tests used long system prompts copied from a blog; after logging, they cut the system block by half and moved static instructions into code. Average tokens per draft fell, but one caterer pasted a full wedding brief into the free-text field and burned a day's budget in an afternoon. They added a character limit, a warning, and a "send anyway" path that still worked without the model. The pilot proved that chefs would click "send draft", not that inference at five hundred active users would stay under CHF 500 per month. That second question needs caching, model choice, and load assumptions, not another week of prompts alone.

## What the cost of AI features in an MVP does not prove

A two-week pilot with twelve friendly users shows whether anyone uses the AI path and whether the output is good enough to continue. It does not prove unit economics at scale, compliance with your sector's rules, or that a wrapped mobile app is the right surface for the feature.

## Smaller models, shorter context, fewer calls

The usual way to overspend in an MVP is not picking the wrong brand; it is calling the largest model on every button press. Start with the smallest model that passes your own acceptance tests on twenty real inputs. Trim context: send the paragraph that matters, not the whole database row history. Batch work where latency allows. For many Swiss B2B MVPs, the first version should be "suggest a draft" with a human send button, not fully automated outbound messages.

When data location matters in customer conversations, remember that region choice on the database (for example Supabase in Zurich) is separate from where the model provider processes text. Your adviser and your customer contract care about both; this article only flags that they are two line items.
