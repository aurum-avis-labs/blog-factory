<p class="blog-lede">A concierge MVP and a wizard of oz MVP both test whether the outcome you promise is worth paying for before you automate it: you deliver the result manually, or behind a simple front that looks like software, while you watch what people actually do.</p>

## What is a concierge and wizard of oz MVP?

A **concierge MVP** means you do the work yourself (or with a small team) for a short list of customers: spreadsheets, email, exports, phone calls, whatever matches the job. The customer knows they are in a pilot or a service, and you learn whether the finished result matters enough to repeat.

A **wizard of oz MVP** uses the same manual engine but often hides it behind a product-shaped surface: a form, a chat box, a bare dashboard. The visitor believes they are using an early product; you fulfil the request by hand and study friction, language, and drop-off. The label comes from the story where the wizard is a person behind a curtain. In practice the two terms overlap; teams argue about naming more than they change the work.

This is for founders with an idea or pre-seed traction who do not yet have a technical co-founder and need evidence beyond friendly encouragement. For landing pages, interviews, and the full menu, see [how to validate a startup idea](/blog/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea). Here the focus stays on manual delivery, honest limits, and when to stop pretending the spreadsheet is the product.

## How to run a concierge or wizard of oz MVP

Keep the cohort small, the promise narrow, and the log honest. You are buying learning, not revenue at scale.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Write the outcome in one sentence</h3><p>Who receives what, by when, and what would count as failure. If you cannot describe the deliverable without naming your future tech stack, simplify.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Choose five to fifteen people, not the internet</h3><p>Pick a segment you can reach without pretending you already have a platform. Mix strangers with warm intros only if you label which is which in your notes.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Decide how visible the manual work is</h3><p>Concierge pilots often say "we do this for you this month". Wizard-of-oz tests keep the curtain unless trust requires transparency. Deception without a follow-up plan burns the next launch.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Charge or commit something real</h3><p>Free favours teach politeness. A modest fee, a signed pilot letter, or a clear time commitment from the buyer separates curiosity from need.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Log every step you repeat twice</h3><p>Those repetitions become your automation backlog. Steps you hate are often the ones customers barely notice.</p></li>
  <li data-step="6"><h3>Set an end date and a stop rule</h3><p>Example shape: stop manual delivery if fewer than three of ten pilots renew or expand, or if fulfilment takes more than twice the time you assumed.</p></li>
</ol>

## What a manual MVP proves, and what it does not

<ul class="blog-facts">
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Proves</span><strong>Outcome value</strong><p>Someone received the result and came back, paid, or referred a peer under fair terms.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Proves</span><strong>Workflow detail</strong><p>You see exceptions, data formats, and approval steps surveys never surface.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Does not prove</span><strong>Scale</strong><p>Your hands do not predict cloud cost, support load, or onboarding once many users arrive.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Does not prove</span><strong>Self-serve product</strong><p>People who like a white-glove pilot may refuse a self-service app with the same logic.</p></li>
</ul>

A strong concierge run is weaker than repeat usage in deployed software and stronger than a [fake door test](/blog/fake-door-test) that only records a click. It sits well after a [smoke test landing page](/blog/smoke-test-landing-page) when strangers already accept the promise and your open question is whether the delivered outcome changes behaviour.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>A concierge or wizard of oz MVP is evidence about the job and the offer, not proof of product-market fit. If the risk is marketplace liquidity, compliance in a regulated workflow, or infra cost at volume, you still need a later test with real software and honest measurement.</p>
</aside>

## A made-up example: weekly supplier summary for a small manufacturer

A made-up example: a founder in Aargau targets operations leads in firms with 40–90 staff. The hypothesis is that buyers lose half a day each week reconciling supplier delivery notes with invoices. They offer a four-week pilot: every Monday morning the lead receives a one-page PDF listing mismatches from the previous week. There is no app. The founder collects exports by secure upload and builds the PDF in a spreadsheet template. Five firms agree; three pay CHF 200 for the month, two join free through a trade contact.

Week two shows that one buyer only cares about three suppliers, not the full list. Week three kills a fancy chart nobody opens. Week four, two paid pilots ask to continue; one free pilot drops out when the internal buyer changes. The founder does not claim software margins. They claim that a narrow reconciliation report is worth renewing for two of five, and that automation should start at ingestion rules, not at a mobile dashboard. The next step is either a thin wizard-of-oz upload page with the same human backend, or a scoped build conversation, not a pitch deck about AI.

## Who should try manual delivery, and who should skip

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>Who this is for</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Founders whose hypothesis is about the value of the outcome, not only the headline on a page</li>
      <li>B2B ideas where trust and exceptions dominate, and a polite survey would lie</li>
      <li>Teams that finished a cheap demand test and need to see whether fulfilment breaks the promise</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Who should skip or shorten it</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Ideas where the risk is "will anyone click?"; start with a measured page instead</li>
      <li>Products that only work at scale (marketplaces, real-time matching, network effects)</li>
      <li>Founders who cannot sustain manual work for the agreed window; burnout invalidates the data</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Remember that ideas as such are not protectable in Switzerland; execution and brand are ([IGE on ideas](https://www.ige.ch/de/uebersicht-geistiges-eigentum/ein-leitfaden-fuer-innovative-und-kreative/idee/koennen-ideen-geschuetzt-werden)). Manual delivery does not hide your concept. It buys time to learn before you fund code.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is wizard of oz the same as concierge?</h3>
  <p>Often in conversation, yes. Concierge usually stresses visible service; wizard of oz stresses a product-like front with manual fulfilment. Pick the label that keeps you honest with customers, then run the same disciplined pilot.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>When do you stop manual delivery?</h3>
  <p>When you have a written rule hit (renewals, referrals, or time spent per customer), or when the same manual steps repeat cleanly enough that automation is the next experiment, not a default twelve-month roadmap.</p>
</div>
