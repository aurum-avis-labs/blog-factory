## AI in restaurants and hotels: the office work

AI in a restaurant or a small hotel earns its place in the office, beside the till and the booking book, not on the pass during service. The point-of-sale system already takes the guest bill. Reservation software already holds the tables that guests booked online. A hotel system already pushes rates and availability to the booking sites. What still fills the afternoon is the work that never enters those systems: the supplier order written after service, the group enquiry in the inbox, the reservation left on a slip by the phone, the supplier invoice, and the hours the team wrote on paper. Those are the candidates. The menu, the price and the promise to a guest stay with you.

This is for owners and the person who does the office in a Swiss restaurant, café or small hotel, often one person after lunch.

## What the till, the book and the hotel system already do

Start with the systems you already pay for. A lot of the evening is already covered, if the team actually uses them.

[Lightspeed](https://www.lightspeedhq.com/pos/restaurant/inventory/) describes restaurant inventory that deducts stock as menu items are sold, shows what is on hand, warns when stock is low and can send recurring orders to suppliers. [resmio](https://www.resmio.com/reservierungen/) states that guests can see table availability and reserve online, including on your website and on Google, and that those bookings land in the reservation software. Its [table plan](https://www.resmio.com/planung/tischplan/) shows which tables are free, reserved or occupied. [Mews](https://www.mews.com/en/products/mews-channel-manager) states that its channel manager, inside the hotel system, connects to more than 400 channels, including Booking.com, Expedia and Airbnb for Hotels, and syncs rates and availability in real time so one room is not sold twice.

If your till already counts stock, your book already takes online tables, and your hotel system already updates the channels, there is nothing to rebuild on that stretch. A second program that retypes what those systems already store only adds a place for the two versions to disagree. What your own program can connect to is a question for the check.

The gap sits beside those systems. It is the message, the paper and the phone call that nobody has entered yet.

## Five office jobs that keep returning

Five jobs show up in almost every small hospitality business. They follow rules, they start from something already written down, and they land on the same person.

### Supplier orders

A kitchen buys from several suppliers, each on a different path: a web shop, an email, a phone call, a form. The list starts on the wall, on a note at the pass or in a message after service. Someone splits it, turns "two crates" into the supplier's unit and types it again, once per supplier. Which article you need stays a kitchen decision. How one product turns a single list into a draft per supplier is in the [KitchenCrew case](/blog/kitchencrew-supplier-ordering-case). This page does not retell that case.

### Group and event enquiries

A company asks for dinner for 24, or a family asks for rooms and a meal. Someone checks whether the room or the corner is free, looks up last year's package and writes an offer with menu, price per person, deposit and a reply date, then chases the ones that went quiet. Whether you want that group on a Saturday, and at what price, stays with you. Copying the old offer is not judgement.

### Reservations that never reach the book

Online bookings are already in the system if you use a tool such as resmio. The leak is everything else. A regular calls and a server writes a name on the pad. A guest writes to the address on the website asking to move a booking, and the mail stays in a personal inbox. By the evening the book and the pad disagree. A workflow can match the mail or the phone note to a date and a party size, and put a draft into the book for a person to confirm. It should not confirm a table the book already shows as full.

### Supplier invoices and deposits

The guest bill is the till's job. The office job is the butcher's invoice as a PDF, the drinks invoice that does not match the delivery note, and the deposit that reached the bank and was never marked on the booking. Matching a clear invoice to an open order is sorting. Disputing a quantity, or deciding whether a deposit is forfeited after a late cancellation, is not.

### Hours for payroll

Service writes start and end on paper or in a chat, including a split shift and a replacement who came in for one evening. Once a month someone retypes that into the payroll file. Whether a shift counts as overtime stays with the person who runs payroll. A workflow can collect the sheets into one list per person and flag a day with no entry. It does not decide the wage.

## What a small hotel adds, and a restaurant does not

A restaurant's book is tables and covers. A small hotel adds arrivals, room moves and messages from the booking sites. If a channel manager such as the one Mews describes already syncs availability, you do not need a second tool to stop a double booking on those channels. You still need a person, or a careful workflow, for the request that is not a standard room: twelve rooms plus dinner, an extra bed, a late arrival written only in the guest's mail. Housekeeping hears about it when someone copies that mail onto the list.

## Why a chat account does not clear the afternoon

A chat assistant helps with a sentence: a polite answer to a group, a translation for a guest. It does not watch the inbox, check the book, file the supplier invoice or turn Friday's paper hours into a list. Those are workflows between the mailbox, the booking tool and the folder where you keep offers. Guest names, telephone numbers and invoices do not belong in a free chat account. Where customer data may go is covered in [ChatGPT and customer data under the Swiss DPA](/blog/chatgpt-customer-data-swiss-dsg).

## What stays with the host

Judgement stays with a person: the menu and the price, whether you accept a group on the busiest Saturday, a promise about an allergy, a complaint at the table. A workflow prepares, sorts and reminds. It does not speak for the house.

Some of the five jobs are not worth automating even though they repeat. A restaurant that orders from one supplier, in that supplier's own shop, once a week. A hotel with a handful of group enquiries a year. The four questions in [which process to automate first](/blog/which-process-to-automate-first) still apply: it repeats, it hurts, it has a clear end, and the data already exists.

## Where to start

Pick the one job that costs the most hours in a normal fortnight. Count the supplier orders, the group mails, the stray reservations, the invoices and the hour sheets. If one number is small, leave it. If one stands out, that is the case.

If that job lands on the same person every week, the [Process Check](/prozess-check) is a calm way to look at it.
