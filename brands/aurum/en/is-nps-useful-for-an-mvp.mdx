<p class="blog-lede">NPS is useful for an MVP when you already have people who used the product on their own terms and you want a simple score to track over time. It is not proof of product-market fit, retention, or willingness to pay.</p>

## Is NPS useful for an MVP?

Net Promoter Score (NPS) asks one question: how likely are you to recommend this product to a friend or colleague, on a scale from 0 to 10? People who answer 9 or 10 count as promoters, 7 or 8 as passives, and 0 to 6 as detractors. The score is the share of promoters minus the share of detractors, often reported between -100 and +100. For a pre-seed founder, that format is attractive because it is one number you can put in a board update without building a custom survey.

The limit is what the number represents. NPS captures stated intent to recommend, usually at one moment, often from whoever bothered to reply. It does not show whether someone came back next week, referred anyone without a prompt, or would pay after a trial. Those behaviour patterns matter more when you are deciding whether to keep building. How disappointment, retention, and referrals fit together is spelled out in [how to find product-market fit](/blog/product-market-fit-how-to-find-it). This page stays on NPS itself.

You are the reader if you have a first build, a small user base, and a question like «should we run an NPS survey before the next sprint?» You are not the reader if you only have a landing page waitlist, if every respondent is a friend who will say 10, or if you need a legal or finance sign-off from three enterprise accounts and NPS would sample the wrong people.

## When NPS helps

NPS helps when three conditions hold at once.

First, respondents used the product for a real job, not a guided demo. A score after you walked someone through every screen is politeness, not signal.

Second, you have enough answers that one enthusiastic founder does not move the needle. There is no magic headcount. Treat any score built on fewer than a few dozen independent users as directional only, and say so in the room.

Third, you will run the same question again on a schedule, with the same wording and the same definition of «user». A single spike after a launch week is marketing noise. A flat or falling score while retention improves is a hint that your survey population does not match the people who actually stick.

Pair NPS with events you already log. If you have not wired analytics, start with [analytics setup for an MVP](/blog/analytics-setup-for-an-mvp) and a short [event tracking plan](/blog/event-tracking-plan-for-an-mvp) so you can compare «would recommend» with «came back without a reminder».

## What NPS does not prove

A high NPS does not prove product-market fit. Detractors can be vocal while a narrow segment retains. Promoters can love a free beta and disappear when you ask for money. The Sean Ellis «how disappointed would you be if this went away?» test targets a different question and has its own sample-size limits; we keep that discussion in the PMF article linked above rather than repeating it here.

NPS also does not prove security, data handling, or that your MVP is ready for App Store review. It does not replace interviews where you watch someone struggle with onboarding. It does not tell you which feature to cut when the board wants a smaller scope.

## NPS and other early signals

Use NPS as one line on a small dashboard, not the dashboard.

Retention and activation events answer «did they come back?» Referrals and inbound messages answer «did they tell someone without a coupon?» Qualitative follow-up on the optional «why?» field answers «what would have to change?» NPS sits beside those, useful when leadership wants a single trend line.

If promoters cite the same missing capability in the open text, that is backlog input. If detractors cite trust, compliance, or performance, fix or scope before you scale spend. If passives dominate, you may be likable but not essential; that often points to positioning or segment choice, not another cosmetic release.

## A made-up example: survey after a quiet launch

A made-up example: a two-person team ships a B2B workflow tool to sixty people on a waitlist. Thirty open an account, twelve complete the main action once, six return in week two without an email nudge. They send NPS to all thirty account holders. Fourteen respond. NPS is +36. Open text praises speed but asks for an export their roadmap already lists.

They note the score, then report retention for the twelve who activated and the six who returned. They interview three detractors who said the product is «fine» but not worth switching from spreadsheets. The founder does not claim PMF in the update; they say recommendation intent looks positive among respondents while repeat usage is still thin. The next sprint prioritises export and a tighter onboarding path to the first action, not a referral programme.

## Common mistakes

Surveying everyone who ever signed up, including people who never logged in, drags NPS toward passives and teaches you little. Changing the question wording between months breaks the trend line. Offering a gift card for a 10 invites scores that collapse when the reward stops.

Running NPS instead of talking to churned users is the expensive mistake. The score is cheap to collect; the conversation is where you learn whether the problem was acute enough to switch tools.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is NPS the same as the Sean Ellis test?</h3>
  <p>No. NPS asks about recommending to others. The Ellis test asks how disappointed someone would be if the product disappeared. Both are surveys; neither replaces retention. See the PMF article for when the Ellis heuristic helps.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should an MVP survey run inside the product or by email?</h3>
  <p>Match the channel to who you want to hear from. In-app catches active users; email can reach people who tried once and left. Be consistent so you compare like with like next quarter.</p>
</div>
