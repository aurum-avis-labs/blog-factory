## Automate supplier orders in a restaurant: what KitchenCrew does

You can automate supplier orders in a restaurant without taking the decision away from the kitchen. KitchenCrew, a product we built, works like this: the team types the shopping list into a chat once, and one order draft per supplier arrives in their inbox, ready to check and send. It is built for Swiss gastro workflows, with a catalogue upload and email integration. A pilot is running with Swiss restaurants.

This page is for restaurant owners and kitchen leads, and for anyone in an SME who orders from several suppliers by hand. It covers the problem, the design choices behind KitchenCrew and where they stop. It contains no figures on time saved and no restaurant names. The pilot is still running, and we do not publish numbers from it here.

## Why ordering in gastro is so much manual work

Ordering is manual work because one list goes to many suppliers, each on its own channel. A kitchen often buys from a wholesaler, a butcher, a vegetable supplier, a bakery and a drinks supplier. One takes orders through a webshop, one by email, one by phone or text message, one on its own form. Each has its own item names, units and order deadlines.

The list itself usually starts somewhere else: on a whiteboard, on a note by the pass, in a message from the sous-chef at the end of service. Then someone, often late in the evening, splits it by supplier, looks up the right item names, turns "two crates" into the supplier's unit and types it all out again, once per supplier.

Where it goes wrong is easy to guess. An item lands with the wrong supplier. A unit is misread, kilos instead of pieces. Something on the whiteboard never makes it into any order, and the kitchen notices the next morning. None of this is a lack of care. It is copying by hand, under time pressure, at the end of a long day.

## How KitchenCrew handles the list

KitchenCrew keeps the list in one place and takes over the splitting.

  <li><strong>List</strong><p>The team types what is needed into a chat.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Catalogue</strong><p>The uploaded supplier catalogues say which supplier carries which item.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Drafts</strong><p>One order draft per supplier arrives in the team's inbox.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Check and send</strong><p>A person reads the drafts before anything goes out.</p></li>
</ol>

The kitchen still decides what to order. What it no longer does is sort the list by supplier and retype it several times.

## Three design choices, and why we made them

Each choice follows from how restaurants already work, not from what the technology could do.

### Drafts instead of automatic sending

The drafts are drafts on purpose. An order commits money and goods. The kitchen knows things no list shows: a private function on Saturday, a closed day, a supplier who was short last week. A person who reads a finished draft keeps that judgement, and reading is quicker than typing. The responsibility for the order also stays where it was, with the team.

### Email, because it needs nothing new from suppliers

The drafts arrive by email because that channel asks nothing of the supplier. Nobody on the supplier side has to install software, open an interface or change how they take orders. The small supplier, the butcher or the farm, works exactly as before. The restaurant changes its side of the process, not theirs.

### The catalogue as the source

The catalogue decides which supplier gets which item, not a guess. Who carries the olive oil, what it is called there, whether it is sold by the litre or by the case: that comes from the uploaded catalogue. Without it, the chat would have to guess, and a guessed order is worse than a late one. The flip side is maintenance. When a supplier changes its range, the new catalogue has to go in.

## Where the limits are

KitchenCrew prepares orders. It does not replace what happens after them.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>A draft is not a confirmed order. Whether the supplier can deliver, and when, is only known from the supplier's own confirmation.</p>
</aside>

A supplier who only takes orders by phone still needs the phone call. The draft is then a clean list to read from. An outdated catalogue produces outdated drafts. And someone has to check: a team that wants orders to go out unread is asking for a different product, and a riskier one.

It is also not the right tool for every kitchen. If you order almost everything from one wholesaler through its webshop, that webshop is probably the simpler tool. If the list is the same every week, a standing order with the supplier beats any software. If nobody in the team will read the drafts, no ordering tool fixes that.

## What transfers to other SMEs with ordering work

The pattern transfers wherever one list turns into several orders. It has three parts: one input in one place, a reference list that decides who gets what, and one draft per recipient in the channel they already use, which a person checks before it goes out.

Two illustrations, both hypothetical. A trades business with a dozen people orders material for each site from several wholesalers. The foreman's list for next week becomes one draft per wholesaler, checked in the office before sending. A practice with several treatment rooms orders consumables from a handful of suppliers. The list collected from the rooms becomes one draft per supplier, checked by the practice manager.

The same idea, a draft that a person only checks, sits behind one of the examples on our Process Check page: service technicians fill in their reports on the phone, and an invoice draft appears that the office just checks.

What does not transfer by itself is the reference list. Restaurants have supplier catalogues that can be uploaded. In a trades business the reference might be a wholesaler's price list, an export from your own software, or a sheet someone keeps. Which of these your program can provide, as an export or through an interface, is something to clarify before anyone builds.

## Before you automate your own ordering

Three questions show whether ordering is a candidate in your business. You can answer them today, without any tool.

- How many suppliers get an order in a normal week, and on which channel each? Two suppliers on one webshop is a different situation from six suppliers on four channels.
- Is there a reference list per supplier, such as a catalogue, a price list or an export, and is it reasonably current?
- Who checks an order before it goes out today, and would that person check a draft instead?

If you order from several suppliers on different channels, have a current reference list and a person who checks, ordering is worth a closer look. If one of the three is missing, sort that out first. Software will not supply it.
