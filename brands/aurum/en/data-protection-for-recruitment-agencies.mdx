## Data protection for recruitment agencies

Data protection for recruitment agencies means candidate data is your stock in trade: CVs, salary expectations, interview notes, reference calls, and "not for this client" flags must stay purpose-bound. Clients sometimes name departing employees; candidates trust you not to spray PDFs across inboxes. The [FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de) applies even when everyone works on placement fees.

For agency owners and team leads in Switzerland with roughly 20 to 250 staff. Not guidance for HR departments storing their own applicants. Orientation, not legal advice.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Consent or legal basis documented before sharing a CV with a client</li>
  <li>Redacted exports; no full history mail when only a summary is needed</li>
  <li>Talent pool retention with deletion dates, not a "forever database"</li>
  <li>[Processor agreements](/security/blog/data-processing-agreement-under-the-fadp) for ATS, mail, and assessment tools</li>
  <li>Written rule: no candidate data in public AI tools</li>
  <li>Incident plan when a CV goes to the wrong hiring manager</li>
</ul>

## Typical agency mishaps

A consultant forwards five CVs in one mail without checking that the fifth name was "confidential search". A recruiter exports the whole database before leaving for a competitor. A client asks for WhatsApp voice notes of "personality impressions"; they spread in a group chat. Phishing to payroll or CRM steals ATS credentials. A junior copies notes into a free translation site with the candidate's address visible. These cases are agency-specific, not generic SME hygiene.

## What it is not

Not how long an employer keeps applications on its own server. Not a sales playbook. Not a free pass to automated scoring; new scoring tools need a [DPIA screen](/security/blog/when-a-dpia-is-required-in-switzerland).

## Confidential search and client briefs

Retained searches name employers and internal politics. Keep secure notes separate from mass mail. NDAs do not stop Outlook autocomplete. Before the first CV to a new client, agree in writing who is controller and who is processor, especially when the client stores everything in its ATS afterwards.

## References and back channels

A reference call reveals candidate data to a third party. Document consent scope. No voicemail with salary details on the referee's mailbox.

## ATS, exports, and freelancers

Nightly full exports to a director's laptop duplicate risk. In the cloud, test who can export all candidates in one click. Freelancers with full access need contracts and offboarding like employees; remove access when the project ends, not when the invoice is paid.

Interview notes in Teams or Slack are personal data like the ATS. Plan archiving and deletion there too. When clients ask you to "keep everything in our tool", clarify whether you may still hold copies in mail and who answers access requests.

## When clients send you their data

Some clients send org charts or names of departing leaders. Treat these like candidate data: limit purpose, do not merge into the general talent pool, agree deletion when the mandate ends. A client who wants to "keep everything" is a contract talk, not an ATS click.

## Information for candidates

Candidates need plain information: who you are, why you use the data, which client types receive CVs, how long you keep records, and how to request deletion. It does not need to sound legalistic, but it must match practice. A PDF on the website alone is weak if daily work happens in Outlook.

## A made-up example: Friday afternoon forward

A made-up example: on Friday at 16:00 a recruiter selects the wrong client name in Outlook and sends three CVs to a competing firm. The manager calls the client, documents the breach, checks with counsel whether high risk requires FDPIC notification, and tightens address book favourites on Monday. Training is scheduled; nobody claims the ATS alone fixes culture.

## Deletion and access requests

Candidates ask for deletion or access. Map copies in mail, ATS, backups, and client inboxes. Document what you deleted and what you cannot control. Keep "silver medalists" only with clear information and a time limit, not by habit.

## Distinction from employer HR

Companies keeping their own applicants use different retention and roles. Do not send them this checklist; they need employer-focused guides, not placement focus.

## Trade shows, badges, and marketing

Scanning badges at fairs creates another data flow. Before buying a lead scanner, note what consent you have from visitors and where exports go. Candidates who came for one role do not belong in the newsletter by default.

## Security after mail incidents

On suspected compromise, remove access and rotate shared CRM passwords the same day. Client contracts should state whether you act as controller or processor; without clarity, access requests suffer. Internal training on client FADP questions means explaining what you do, not blanket promises.

## Medical practice versus agency

Practices often share health data by mail; agencies share career data and salary expectations. More on practices is in [data protection and security for a medical practice](/security/blog/data-protection-and-security-for-a-medical-practice). Technical risks overlap (wrong recipient, phishing); legal nuance does not. Do not mix the checklists.

Paper applicant files in small agencies: same retention as digital. Before peak season, confirm processor terms for ATS and mail. Run a thirty-minute wrong-CV tabletop once per year. Keep a search list for access requests: ATS, mail, backups, client inboxes. When clients audit you on training aligned with FADP and GDPR, describe your measures factually; avoid blanket promises. This checklist is not a substitute for your lawyer on contracts, but it covers the most common agency slips.

Reference checks belong in the candidate privacy notice: who you call, what you record, and how long you keep it. Without that line, a call to a former manager feels like a surprise, even when consent exists.
