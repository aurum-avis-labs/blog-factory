The **Swiss Federal Act on Data Protection** (FADP, in German DSG, SR 235.1) protects the personal data of natural persons. The fully revised Act has applied since **1 September 2023**; you will also see it called the "nFADP" or "revFADP". For a small or mid-sized company the headline is simple: **size alone exempts you from nothing**. Transparency, data security, data subject rights and reporting serious breaches apply to every business that processes personal data. What a smaller firm can often skip is the formal record of processing activities, and only under conditions set out in the Data Protection Ordinance (DPO/DSV).

This guide is written for owners, HR and whoever runs IT in a Swiss SME. It is orientation, **not legal advice**; professional secrecy, health data and EU customers can add obligations. Statute: [Fedlex FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/en). Current as of **October 2026**.

## Scope in one paragraph

Under [Arts. 1–2 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/en) the Act protects the personality and fundamental rights of natural persons and applies to private parties and federal bodies. **Legal entities' data is no longer covered.** Personal data means any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person (Art. 5(a)) — a client contact's email address counts just as much as payroll or applicant files. *Sensitive* personal data includes health, religious or political views, genetic and biometric data, and data on criminal proceedings (Art. 5(c)). The Act also reaches processing initiated abroad that has an effect in Switzerland (Art. 3).

## What changed on 1 September 2023

The Federal Office of Justice confirms the revised law entered into force on 1 September 2023 ([FOJ](https://www.bj.admin.ch/de/neues-datenschutzrecht)). The federal SME portal lists the shifts businesses feel most ([kmu.admin.ch](https://www.kmu.admin.ch/kmu/en/home/fakten-und-trends/digitalisierung/datenschutz/neues-datenschutzgesetz-revdsg.html)): privacy by design and by default (Art. 7), a broader duty to inform at every collection (Art. 19), a record of processing activities with an SME exemption, impact assessments for high-risk processing (Art. 22), breach notification to the FDPIC (Art. 24), and profiling as a defined term.

## Duties at a glance

| Duty | Legal basis | Typical SME | In practice |
|------|-------------|-------------|-------------|
| Processing principles | Art. 6 FADP | **Always** | Purpose limitation, proportionality, accuracy, deletion |
| Data security | Art. 8 FADP; Arts. 1–3 DPO | **Always** | Access control, backups, encryption scaled to risk |
| Privacy by design/default | Art. 7 FADP | **Always** | Collect only what you need by default |
| Duty to inform / privacy notice | Art. 19 FADP; Art. 13 DPO | **Always** on collection | Who, why, recipients, countries abroad |
| Right of access | Art. 25 FADP; Arts. 16–19 DPO | **Always** | Free of charge, within 30 days; max. CHF 300 contribution if disproportionate effort |
| Processors | Art. 9 FADP | Cloud, payroll, newsletter tools | Contract plus assurance on security |
| Record of processing | Art. 12 FADP; **Art. 24 DPO** | Often **exempt** under 250 staff | Not if sensitive data at large scale or high-risk profiling |
| Impact assessment (DPIA) | Art. 22 FADP | **High risk only** | Document; consult FDPIC or your adviser on residual high risk |
| Breach notification | Art. 24 FADP | **Likely high risk** | As soon as possible — no fixed 72 hours |
| Data protection adviser | Art. 10 FADP | **Optional** | Not a mandatory DPO |
| Criminal fines | Arts. 60–64 FADP | **Intent only** | Up to CHF 250,000, against individuals |

## Privacy notice: the minimum content

Art. 19 requires you to inform people appropriately when you collect their data, including data obtained from third parties. At minimum: your identity and contact details, the purpose, recipients or categories of recipients and — unlike the GDPR's baseline wording — the **country** of any disclosure abroad plus the safeguard relied on. The FDPIC stresses that the format is up to you, but the information must be concise, transparent, understandable and easy to find ([FDPIC](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/informationspflicht)). In practice: update the website notice, check the job-application flow and newsletter sign-up, and name your cloud providers' hosting countries.

## Access requests: 30 days, usually free

Anyone can ask whether and what data you hold about them (Art. 25 FADP). In an SME that is typically a former employee in a dispute, a rejected applicant or an unhappy customer.

- **Deadline:** 30 days from receipt. If you cannot make it, tell the person within that period when they will get an answer (Art. 18 DPO).
- **Cost:** free by default. Only where the effort is disproportionate may you ask for a contribution of at most CHF 300, and you must say so up front (Art. 19 DPO).
- **Content:** at least your identity, the data itself, purpose, retention period, source and recipients.
- **Verify identity:** take reasonable steps to identify the requester (Art. 16 DPO) — do not email payslips to an unknown Gmail address.

## The record of processing and the SME exemption

Art. 12 FADP requires controllers and processors to keep a record. **Art. 24 DPO** carves out undertakings with **fewer than 250 employees on 1 January** of a given year — unless they process **sensitive data on a large scale** or carry out **high-risk profiling**. Neither the Act nor the Ordinance puts a number on "large scale"; a fiduciary firm with ordinary client files differs from a medical practice. The exemption covers the record only. Many corporate customers still ask for an overview in supplier questionnaires, so a lean spreadsheet (system, purpose, data categories, retention) is often worth keeping anyway.

## Impact assessments (DPIA)

Under Art. 22 a DPIA is required where processing may pose a high risk, for example large-scale processing of sensitive data or systematic monitoring of large public areas. If a high residual risk remains, you consult the FDPIC beforehand — or, as a private controller, your data protection adviser instead (Art. 23). Keep the DPIA for at least two years after processing ends (Art. 14 DPO). A standard SaaS CRM without profiling seldom needs one; AI-based candidate scoring or public-facing CCTV may.

## Data breaches: when to notify the FDPIC

A breach of data security means personal data is lost, deleted, destroyed, altered or disclosed without authorisation (Art. 5(h)). Under **Art. 24 FADP** you notify the FDPIC **as soon as possible** where the breach is **likely to result in a high risk**. There is **no statutory 72-hour clock** — a GDPR habit that even some Swiss guides repeat. Use the FDPIC's [DataBreach portal](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/databreach) and its [guidance](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/leitfaden-data-breach) (April 2025). Document every incident internally and keep the record for at least two years (Art. 15 DPO). Walkthrough: [Report a data breach to the FDPIC](/security/blog/report-a-data-breach-to-the-fdpic).

## Fines and personal liability

Swiss sanctions target **individuals acting with intent**, not company turnover. Arts. 60–61 allow fines of up to **CHF 250,000** for, among other things, deliberately failing to inform, giving false information in response to an access request, or breaching due-diligence rules on transfers abroad, processors or minimum security standards. The FDPIC sums it up: intent only, primarily natural persons, CHF 250,000 maximum ([FDPIC](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/strafbestimmungen)). Under Art. 64(2), where the fine would not exceed CHF 50,000 and identifying the responsible person would be disproportionate, the business itself can be ordered to pay. Cantonal prosecutors handle the cases; the FDPIC can file a complaint (Art. 65). Note that failing to report a breach under Art. 24 is not itself listed as an offence — but falling short of the minimum security requirements is (Art. 61(c)). FDPIC orders and civil claims come on top. More: [FADP fines and personal liability](/security/blog/fadp-fines-and-personal-liability).

## Do you need a data protection officer?

**No.** Private controllers *may* appoint a data protection adviser (Art. 10 FADP); unlike in Germany it is not mandatory. An adviser who meets the independence and expertise conditions, and whose contact details are published and notified to the FDPIC, lets you consult them instead of the FDPIC on high residual risk. Details: [Is a data protection adviser required in Switzerland?](/security/blog/is-a-data-protection-adviser-required-in-switzerland).

## A practical SME checklist

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>List systems that hold personal data (CRM, payroll, email, file shares, website tools)</li>
  <li>Bring your privacy notice in line with Art. 19, including countries abroad</li>
  <li>Check processor agreements with cloud, hosting, payroll and marketing vendors</li>
  <li>Role-based access; revoke it within days when someone leaves</li>
  <li>Set up and test an internal breach-reporting path</li>
  <li>Re-check the Art. 24 DPO exemption every 1 January — or keep a lean record voluntarily</li>
  <li>Screen new high-risk projects for a DPIA</li>
  <li>Brief everyone who handles personal data</li>
</ul>

**Fiduciary and advisory firms:** client files, social-security numbers and payroll call for tight access rights; the record is often optional, but access requests and disclosures need discipline. **Construction and trades:** staff files, subcontractor contacts and site cameras — keep purposes and retention clear; cameras may raise DPIA questions. **Service firms with newsletters and analytics:** US-based tools need transparency and a valid transfer safeguard under Art. 16; a cookie banner is no substitute for a privacy notice.

## The FADP is not the GDPR

If you serve EU customers, the GDPR may apply in parallel. Day-to-day Swiss differences: no general requirement of a legal basis for every private processing operation, breach notice "as soon as possible" rather than 72 hours, and fines on individuals rather than turnover-based corporate penalties. More: [FADP vs GDPR](/security/blog/swiss-fadp-vs-gdpr).


## What to do this quarter

1. Confirm who owns privacy decisions in the leadership team — fines land on people.
2. Walk through every tool that stores personal data and jot purpose plus retention.
3. Update the privacy notice if it still refers only to the pre-2023 Act.
4. Collect processor agreements for Microsoft 365, hosting, payroll and email marketing.
5. Write a one-page breach playbook: contain, assess risk, document, notify via the FDPIC portal when a high risk is likely.
6. Decide whether you rely on the Art. 24 DPO exemption or keep a voluntary lean record.
7. Schedule a short staff briefing for anyone who sends client data by email.

That sequence closes most of the gap between "we know the law exists" and "we can show what we did" if the FDPIC or a large customer asks. Pair it with [data protection training for employees](/security/blog/data-protection-training-for-employees) if the last session predates the 2023 revision.

## FAQ

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>When did the new Swiss Data Protection Act take effect?</h3>
  <p>On 1 September 2023, together with the revised Data Protection Ordinance.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Does the FADP apply to very small businesses?</h3>
  <p>Yes, as soon as you process personal data of staff, applicants or customers. Headcount only matters for the record of processing activities.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is there a 72-hour breach deadline in Switzerland?</h3>
  <p>No. Art. 24 FADP requires notice as soon as possible, and only where a high risk is likely.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Who can be fined under the FADP?</h3>
  <p>Primarily the individual who acted with intent — up to CHF 250,000. The company itself only pays in the limited cases of Art. 64(2).</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is the FDPIC my data protection officer?</h3>
  <p>No. The FDPIC is the federal supervisory authority. An in-house or external data protection adviser under Art. 10 is optional for private firms.</p>
</div>
