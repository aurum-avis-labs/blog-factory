## CEO fraud in Switzerland: what finance teams need to know

CEO fraud in Switzerland is a social-engineering scam in which someone pretends to be your chief executive or another senior manager and pushes accounts payable to make an urgent bank transfer or change payment details. The message often arrives by email or messaging app, stresses secrecy, and claims the boss is in a meeting or travelling. In Swiss reporting, this pattern sits under business email compromise and fraud. It is not a malware problem on its own; it is a process and verification problem that hits companies with 20 to 250 staff as often as larger firms, because one person in finance can still release a payment.

BACS recorded 719 CEO-fraud reports in 2024 and 971 in the full year 2025, with offenders researching targets on LinkedIn, company websites, and the commercial register, and increasingly using AI-written text; in some cases synthetic voice as well. Fraud accounted for 52 percent of reports in the second half of 2025 according to BACS. Deloitte’s 2026 study among firms under 250 employees lists CEO fraud among the attack types named most often, at 8 percent. Whether training is legally mandatory for your company is a separate question; many teams start after a near-miss, as described in our article on [whether security awareness training is mandatory in Switzerland](/security/blog/is-security-awareness-training-mandatory-switzerland).

## Who attackers contact first

Offenders pick roles that prepare or release payments, and people close to leadership. In Swiss SMEs that is often accounts payable, the finance clerk, an executive assistant, or whoever approves supplier invoices. Sometimes the target is new and does not yet know how the chief executive normally behaves. Public sources supply the attack surface: who posted about a conference on LinkedIn, who signs in the commercial register, and which email addresses appear on the website.

  <li><h3>You handle supplier payments</h3><p>A message demands a confidential wire to a «new adviser» or an emergency vendor.</p></li>
  <li><h3>You assist the executive team</h3><p>The tone feels personal and asks you to bypass the usual approver.</p></li>
  <li><h3>You are the only person for small amounts</h3><p>The sum looks «small enough» that nobody checks, until several payments follow.</p></li>

## How CEO fraud usually unfolds

The typical CEO fraud path has four stages. First, the attacker collects names, roles, and travel patterns from public sources. Second, they send a message that looks like it comes from the executive, sometimes from a look-alike domain or a compromised mailbox. Third, they ask for a confidential wire, a new supplier payment, or an exception to normal approval rules. Fourth, if nobody checks through a second channel, the payment goes out and the money is hard to recover.

Some cases add pressure: «only you can do this today», «do not call me, I am in negotiations», or a follow-up from a supposed lawyer or auditor. BACS also describes cases where offenders pose as a Swiss law firm. Others start with a compromised real account inside the firm, so the email passes technical checks and feels authentic. That variant overlaps with invoice fraud where the IBAN changes; here the focus is the fake urgency from «the boss», not maintenance of supplier master data.

  <li><strong>Research</strong><p>Names, org chart, and timing from open sources.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Contact</strong><p>Email or chat impersonating leadership.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Pressure</strong><p>Urgency, secrecy, and an exception to process.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Payment</strong><p>Transfer before anyone verifies on a known number.</p></li>
</ul>

## Stop rules before money leaves the firm

Train finance and assistants to pause when several of these appear together: a payment request only by email or chat, no purchase order or contract reference, a new beneficiary or changed bank details, instructions to bypass the usual approver, or a chief executive who never normally orders payments directly. Language may be fluent but slightly off, or perfectly polished because it was machine-generated.

BACS recommendations for business email compromise include calling back, sensitising the finance department, two-factor authentication on email accounts, and clear payment workflows. The last line is still a human who refuses to pay without verification.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Call back on a number from your own records, not from the suspicious message</li>
  <li>Do not pay when the usual approver must be skipped</li>
  <li>Treat new bank details only through your normal master-data process, not by mail</li>
  <li>Preserve the message and tell IT before you reply</li>
</ul>

## What CEO fraud is not

CEO fraud is not ordinary phishing that steals a login. It is not ransomware locking files. It is not a statutory cyberattack report to BACS for most SMEs; the federal reporting duty from 1 April 2025 applies to operators of critical infrastructure, not to a typical trading or services company with a few dozen staff. You may still report voluntarily to BACS and you should involve your bank and the police if money moved.

It is also not solved by buying another antivirus licence alone. Technical measures help, but they do not replace clear approvals and a quick chat with a colleague when something «does not fit».

## What to do first if a payment looks wrong

If someone in finance received a CEO fraud style message and has not paid yet, preserve the message, tell IT or your external IT partner, and verify with the supposed sender using your usual phone directory or an in-person check. If payment already left, contact the bank immediately to attempt a recall, document times and amounts, and file a report with the police; BACS publishes guidance on business email compromise for Swiss companies.

Internally, treat it as a security incident: short debrief with leadership, adjust approval rules if they were too thin, and consider whether staff need a focused session on payment fraud. A live security awareness session can walk through CEO fraud, invoice manipulation, and how to report odd messages without blaming the person who almost paid.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Can small Swiss companies be targeted for CEO fraud?</h3>
  <p>Yes. Attackers use public information and scale their campaigns. Size does not protect you if one person can release payments without a second check.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Must the chief executive confirm every urgent email?</h3>
  <p>No. Define which payment types need a call-back or a second approval. Real emergencies happen, but they rarely arrive only by email with no number you already hold on file.</p>
</div>
