## Dispatch planning for field technicians

Dispatch planning for field technicians means turning a request into a proposed visit that fits the skill, the area and the parts, then confirming a window with the customer. During the day, a change becomes a new message, not a silent hope. A small board can do that for a small team. Dedicated field-service software is the better buy when the problem is travel, live status and a morning that has to be rebuilt from scratch. This page says which is which. It does not rank products.

Recurring dates on service contracts, the reminder and the proposal weeks ahead, are the contract calendar in [schedule recurring maintenance](/blog/maintenance-service-reminders). This page is the day: those confirmed visits, plus breakdowns, plus the moves. After the visit, the report and the invoice are in [from daywork report to invoice](/blog/daywork-report-to-invoice). Do not build a second invoice path here.

<ol class="blog-flow">
  <li><strong>Request</strong><p>What is wrong or due, where, and how urgent.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Fit</strong><p>Skill, area, and whether the part is on the van or in the workshop.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Window</strong><p>A time the customer confirms. Then it is on the board.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Change</strong><p>A delay or an extra job updates the people who are waiting.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Close</strong><p>The visit feeds the report. The board shows it done.</p></li>
</ol>

## What the request has to say before anyone is assigned

A request that says "heating, Müller, sometime" cannot be planned. Before a name goes on the board, the office has:

- The site, and who opens the door.
- What the visit is: a confirmed maintenance, a breakdown, a quote, a return because a part was missing.
- The skill, in the words your team uses. Gas, controls, a brand you are not certified for. A job on the wrong person is a second visit.
- The part, if the symptom usually needs one. On the van, in the workshop, or to be ordered.
- How fixed the time is. A shop that stops production is not the same as a filter change that can move a day.

Write those as fields, not as a paragraph the dispatcher rereads. The phone call can fill them. The board should not depend on memory of the call.

If the part is not on the van and not in the workshop, the proposal does not promise a repair today. It proposes a look, or a day when the part can be there. Promising the fix and arriving without the part wastes the customer's morning and yours.

## A proposal, then a confirmation

Match the request to a person who has the skill and who is already in that area, or who can be without wrecking the rest of the day. For a handful of technicians with known patches, that is a rule a person can see: north, city, lakeside, and the skills on each. The board suggests. The dispatcher confirms. Do not pretend a sheet calculates the best drive across the canton. It does not. It stops the office from sending the only person with that skill to the far site while a nearer visit waits.

The customer gets a window, not a fantasy minute. "Between 10 and 12" is honest if that is what the day can hold. A single clock time is honest only when you mean it. The appointment is confirmed when they agree, the same rule as on the contract calendar. A breakdown may be "we can be there this afternoon, we will call from the road". That is still a message you can file. It is not a blank.

Parts and skill stay on the row the customer does not need to see in full. The customer sees the window, the site and what you intend to do. The dispatcher sees why this person, and what must be on the van.

## When the day moves

The day moves. A breakdown at nine, a part that is the wrong one, a visit that runs long. The board has to show the new order, and the customers whose window changed have to hear it before they wait at the door.

The flow can draft the message from the new window: who, which site, the new range, and that you will call if it moves again. A person sends it when the new time is still a guess, because a drafted "14:00" that the dispatcher has not accepted will be wrong in the customer's diary. When the new window is already decided, sending the draft without a second typing is the point.

Keep a short log on the job: original window, new window, who was told, at what time. The argument later is not about goodwill. It is about whether anyone said the time had moved. One line is enough. A call that nobody writes down does not count.

Do not reshuffle the whole week inside this flow. The move is today's board. Next week's contract visits stay on the contract calendar unless a person pulls one forward.

## When field-service software is the better buy

Buy a field-service product, or use the one your trade software already includes, when the small board is the thing that hurts.

You need it when travel is the constraint: many jobs, overlapping, and the order of the day changes the hours. A sheet that lists names does not solve the drive. A product built to plan routes does that job. Read what that product claims on its own page before you trust it. This article does not repeat vendor numbers.

You need it when the technician should see the job, the history and the parts without phoning the office, and when the office should see "on site" or "done" without a text message. That is a mobile record, not a morning email.

You need it when the team is growing and two dispatchers would otherwise keep two boards. One system, used by everyone, beats a clever flow beside a program nobody closed the jobs in.

You do not need a new product when five technicians cover patches you can draw on a notepad, the pain is "the customer did not know we moved", and a message plus a single board fixes that. You also do not need one when you already pay for a service module and the gap is that the office still plans on paper. Switch the planning onto the module you have. A second tool will not make the first one true.

## A made-up example

A made-up example: four technicians, heating and controls, one dispatcher. Monday's board starts from the confirmed maintenance visits. At 8:40 a breakdown comes in, a boiler in the same town as the morning's second visit, and the skill matches the technician already heading there. The part is a common board, and the van sheet says one is on the van. The dispatcher slots a window after that visit and the customer agrees to "late morning". At 11:00 the board on site is a different version. The repair is not promised for today. The customer gets a message that the technician looked, the part is ordered, and a window for Wednesday is proposed. Two other customers, whose windows slipped by an hour, get a draft the dispatcher sends after checking the board. The report from the morning visit goes to the invoice path the firm already uses. The firm and the times are invented.

## What to leave alone

Leave a new build alone when the dispatcher is also the only technician and the "board" is the diary in the van. A message template for delays may still help. A planning flow will not. Leave the legal content of a service contract alone. What the visit must include is the contract. The board only places a person and a time. If you cannot say which skills exist, write that list before you automate the match. A match against an empty list assigns whoever is free.
