## Why surveys don't validate an idea

A long list of «yes» answers feels like proof. It is usually sympathy. Respondents want to be helpful, they guess at a future self, and they have not paid, signed up, returned, or told a colleague. That gap is why a survey can clear a workshop and still leave you unsure whether anyone will use the product.

This piece is for founders and innovation leads in Switzerland who are tempted to send a Typeform before writing code. It does not repeat the full validation sequence in [how to validate a startup idea](/blog/how-to-validate-a-startup-idea). It explains where surveys belong, where they mislead, and what they never prove on their own.

## What a survey is actually measuring

Most idea surveys ask about importance, likelihood to buy, or feature priority. Those questions mix three different things: whether the problem sounds familiar, whether the respondent likes you, and whether they can imagine a solution. Familiarity is useful. Likeness is noise. Imagination is not a contract.

Surveys work best when you already have a defined audience and you need language. Which words do buyers use for the pain? Which alternative do they name first? Which objection appears in the free-text box? That is positioning research, not a go signal.

They also work when the risk of a wrong «no» is low. A newsletter question about topics is not the same as asking strangers whether they would pay CHF 29 per month for software that does not exist yet.

## Why positive answers inflate

Social desirability is the obvious bias. People agree because the founder is in the room, because the subject sounds worthy, or because ticking «very likely» costs nothing.

Hypothetical bias is the harder one. «Would you use this?» asks someone to simulate a Tuesday six months from now, with a tool they have never touched, next to tools they already pay for. The brain fills gaps with optimism.

Selection bias completes the picture. LinkedIn polls reach people who like polls. Mailing lists reach people who already know you. University mailing lists reach students, not budget holders. A high score in a skewed sample is not evidence about the market you need.

  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>A survey cannot show product-market fit, willingness to pay, or retention. Those need behaviour under conditions you can observe: signup, repeat use, a pre-sale, or a paid test with analytics. Treat a survey as input to the next question, not as the final answer.</p>
</aside>

## When we still send a survey

We use short surveys internally to sharpen copy before we spend on distribution. The answers change headlines and interview guides. They do not replace a deployed product with measurement.

On an internal causal-modelling tool we ran in early 2025, signups and session length told a different story than a friendly inbox would have. Eleven people registered, five stayed past four minutes, three came back, two hit a free credit cap, and nobody bought credits. A survey might still have produced encouraging words about «complex systems». The behaviour showed where friction lived and where interest was real enough to iterate.

That is the pattern: surveys shape questions; usage and conversion shape decisions.

## Who should skip the survey step

Skip a wide survey if you have fewer than ten conversations with people who match the buyer, if you cannot describe the job in one sentence without jargon, or if the next step is already cheap to test in software. In those cases, interviews and a small behavioural test beat another form.

Also skip it if the only audience you can reach is people who will say yes to support you. Kindness is not demand. The [customer interview questions](/blog/customer-interview-questions) piece and [problem validation before building](/blog/problem-validation-before-building) go deeper on conversations and problem fit; this article stays on why the spreadsheet of «likely to buy» is not enough.

## What to run instead of treating the survey as validation

You do not need a large build to learn. You need an observable action tied to the risk you fear. Sometimes that is a landing page with a clear offer and analytics. Sometimes it is a concierge version of the service. Sometimes it is a thin product with one path through the core job.

The point is not to collect more opinions. It is to see whether anyone repeats the behaviour without you in the room. Ideas as such cannot be protected; execution and evidence can still be owned. Behaviour is evidence surveys rarely supply.

## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Are surveys useless for startups?</h3>
  <p>No. They are weak as the only validation step. Use them to refine language and hypotheses after you have talked to real buyers, not as a substitute for those talks or for measured behaviour.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is a waitlist on a landing page the same as a survey?</h3>
  <p>It is closer, because someone took an action. It still does not prove payment or repeat use. Treat it as a stronger signal than a checkbox, and still not as proof of a business.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How many survey responses are enough?</h3>
  <p>There is no universal number. If the sample is not your buyer, a thousand responses still mislead. If the sample is right, a dozen thoughtful answers can be enough to rewrite your interview script, not to green-light a twelve-month build.</p>
</div>
