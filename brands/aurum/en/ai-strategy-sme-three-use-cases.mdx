## AI strategy for SMEs: what to tell the board

The board asks "what are we doing with AI?", and the reflex is to commission a strategy paper. For an SME with 5 to 80 people, a more useful AI strategy fits on one page: three concrete use cases, one owner in management, a few rules, 90 days, then a review. That gives the board something it can check in three months. A thirty-page paper usually gives it a vocabulary.

This is for owners and managing directors who have been asked the question, by the board, by a partner or by their own team, and who have no AI department to delegate it to. It is not about whether AI matters. It is about what answer changes the work.

## Why strategy papers rarely change operations

A strategy paper describes a direction without naming a task, a person or a date, so Monday stays as it was. It lists opportunities per function, perhaps a maturity model and a vision. Then it is presented, approved and filed. Nothing in it says who does what differently next week.

Meanwhile the team keeps doing what it already does: drafting and translating in whatever chat is open, while the recurring work still runs on copy and paste. We have described that gap in [everyone uses AI, operations still run on copy-paste](/blog/ai-in-operations-nothing-tangible). A paper does not close it, because the paper never touches the work.

[BDO](https://www.bdo.ch/de-ch/medienstelle/2026/ki-adoption-die-kluft-zwischen-grossunternehmen-und-kmu-wachst) described the pattern in June 2026: large companies have already built AI into their value creation, while many SMEs stop at individual applications. Their advice: instead of launching as many pilots as possible at once, choose a few concrete use cases with measurable benefit and implement them consistently. And treat AI as a strategic task for management, not as an IT topic. That is BDO's advice, and it is a good definition of a strategy that fits an SME.

## Three use cases, chosen on purpose

Pick three tasks that already happen every week, each with a person who does them today and an effect you can see within 90 days. Three is enough to learn something and few enough to finish.

A mix works better than three of the same kind:

1. **One task everyone touches.** Correspondence or translation in the approved company tool. The point is not speed alone. It moves drafting out of private accounts and under your rules.
2. **One recurring office process.** The Monday report, invoice capture, quote follow-ups. It should repeat, hurt, have a clear start and end, and use data you already have. The filter is in [which process to automate first](/blog/which-process-to-automate-first).
3. **One task close to customers or revenue.** For example first replies to standard enquiries, drafted by the tool and sent by a person. Visible to the board, and small enough to control.

For each case, write four lines: what changes, who owns the task, how you will see the effect, and by when.

## One owner and a few rules

The owner sits in management, because the decisions are about priorities and people, not about software. That person can free time for the three cases, say stop, and report to the board. An IT partner can handle settings. They should not decide which work changes.

The rules come before case one grows. Otherwise the first use case spreads private accounts through the company. A few rules means an allow-list, data classes and a human check before anything leaves the firm. The page for that is the [AI usage policy template](/blog/ai-policy-template-sme), and the tools part is [how to approve AI tools](/blog/approve-ai-tools-allow-list).

## 90 days, then a review

After 90 days each case gets one of three verdicts: keep it, change it, or drop it. Measure the work, not the logins. How long does the task take now, how often does it need rework, does it still run when the usual person is on holiday?

Counting logins is a common trap. The [adesso GenAI Impact Report 2026 for Switzerland](https://www.adesso.ch/adesso-ch/whitepaper/genai-impact-report-2026-ch.pdf) found that 45 percent of the Swiss companies surveyed measure GenAI success by usage rate, almost as many as measure process efficiency (47 percent). Their figures. A rising usage rate tells the board people open the tool. It does not tell it whether any work got better.

The board then gets one page: three cases, three verdicts, the next three. Repeat each quarter. That page is your AI strategy, and it stays current because it is rewritten every 90 days.

## When three use cases are not enough

Some situations need more than one page. If you sell into the EU, have an establishment there, or use software that decides about people, such as screening applications, you need legal advice alongside the use cases. If a bank or an investor asks for a formal strategy document, write it, and put the three cases on page one.

And if your firm is far beyond 80 people with its own IT and data teams, this format is a starting point for one department, not for the company.
