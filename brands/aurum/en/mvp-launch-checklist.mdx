## MVP launch checklist

An **MVP launch checklist** is the short pass you run before strangers can sign up, before paid ads point at a live URL, or before you ask a pilot customer to invite colleagues. You are not polishing for a Series A demo. You are making sure the product can be used, measured, and supported without obvious gaps that turn every metric into a story about broken software.

This is for founders and innovation leads in Switzerland who already have something deployable, not a clickable mock-up. If you are still testing demand with a landing page only, most items below are premature. For why we treat deploy quality as part of validation, see [why we build production-quality MVPs](/blog/why-we-build-production-quality-mvps).

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>Run this checklist when</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Real users outside friends and family can create accounts or submit data</li>
      <li>You plan paid acquisition or a store listing in the next few weeks</li>
      <li>A B2B pilot expects a stable URL, a privacy notice, and someone to email when something breaks</li>
      <li>You need two weeks of behaviour data you can compare week on week</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Skip or shorten it when</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>You are running a fake-door or waitlist test with no product login</li>
      <li>Only you use a local build and no personal data leaves your machine</li>
      <li>Legal counsel already signed off a regulated launch pack this month</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

## What finishing the list does and does not prove

Ticking every box means you removed launch blockers you could find in an afternoon or two, and you wrote down what is still open. It does not prove product-market fit, a repeatable acquisition channel, or that your price is right. A clean launch only means the signal is more likely to be about the offer, not about a missing password reset or a blank analytics tag.

  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>This list is engineering and go-to-market hygiene, not legal advice. Health data, employment records, or children’s products need counsel before you widen access, even if the app «works».</p>
</aside>

## The checklist before you go live

Work top to bottom. If an item is not applicable, note why in one line so a contractor next month does not assume you forgot it.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li><strong>One core job, end to end.</strong> A new user can complete the main promise without you in the room: sign up, do the thing, see a result. Secondary screens can wait; the path you will measure must not depend on a hidden admin toggle.</li>
  <li><strong>Production host, not a dev URL.</strong> The link in your ad or email is the same environment you tested. HTTPS works, sessions survive a refresh, and you know who can deploy and roll back.</li>
  <li><strong>Analytics and session context.</strong> Page views alone are thin for a product. Configure the events you will judge the test on (signup, activation, key action, drop-off step) and confirm they fire in a private browser test. Session recordings help explain where people stall; turn them on only with a clear notice if your traffic is in Switzerland.</li>
  <li><strong>Privacy and contact.</strong> Publish who processes data, for what purpose, and how to reach you. Match what the landing page claims about data use. Swiss law does not require a cookie banner for every site, but profiling, certain transfers, and cross-site tracking need consent; otherwise inform users and give a way to object. When in doubt, ask counsel rather than copying a German template.</li>
  <li><strong>Errors visible to you, not only to users.</strong> Trigger a failure on purpose. Check that your team gets an alert or a log entry without reading the user’s screen. Raw stack traces in the browser are a leak; fix or mask them before scale.</li>
  <li><strong>Support path.</strong> One monitored inbox or form for «it broke». Someone is named internally for the first 48 hours after launch, even if that person is the founder.</li>
  <li><strong>Promise match.</strong> Headline, store screenshot, and first screen inside the app describe the same job. A surprise upsell or a different persona on day one pollutes interview and retention data.</li>
  <li><strong>Data you cannot lose.</strong> If users store content or messages, state whether backup and export exist and whether you tested restore. «The host backs up» is not the same as «we tried a restore».</li>
  <li><strong>Mobile store readiness, if you ship a wrap.</strong> Apple’s guideline 4.2 expects more than a repackaged website; 4.2.2 rejects pure marketing shells. Plan app-like function before review. For Google Play, new personal developer accounts created after 13 November 2023 must run a closed test with at least 12 testers for 14 continuous days before production. Details sit in [publish an app on the App Store](/blog/publish-an-app-on-the-app-store) and [App Store guideline 4.2](/blog/app-store-guideline-4-2).</li>
  <li><strong>Written success criteria.</strong> One paragraph: what you will look at for the next two to four weeks, what would count as «stop», and what you will not conclude from a small sample. Without that, every spike feels like traction and every quiet day feels like failure.</li>
  <li><strong>Launch log.</strong> Date, version, who deployed, what was checked, known open risks. You will need it when the first bug report arrives at midnight.</li>
</ul>

## A made-up example: a B2B roster tool on a Wednesday

A made-up example: a team of two ships a shift-roster web app for small care homes in German-speaking Switzerland. No payments in v1; managers invite staff by email. They plan a modest LinkedIn test the following Monday.

On Wednesday they run the checklist. Signup and invite work on mobile Safari, but the «publish week» button silently fails when the session is older than an hour; they fix token refresh before any ad spend. Google Analytics events were missing on the invite step; they add one named event and verify in debug view. The privacy page still mentions a product they dropped in beta; they rewrite the purpose line. They decide not to submit an iOS wrap yet because the product is still browser-first and guideline 4.2 would be a gamble. They write success criteria: ten activated homes with at least one published week within 21 days, otherwise pause ads and interview five managers who bounced on invite. The launch log is a shared note with version 0.9.3 and «restore untested».

The pass took a day. It did not prove homes will pay. It stopped them from buying clicks while invites were broken.

## Common gaps that look like «no demand»

Broken email delivery, wrong locale on the login screen, and analytics that double-count signups each produce graphs that look like rejection. So does a store rejection you discover only after announcing a launch date. Build store lead time into the calendar when mobile is in scope, not the night before press.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should we launch on web first and add stores later?</h3>
  <p>Often yes, when the hypothesis is workflow and retention in a browser. Stores add review time and policy risk. Add a wrap when mobile use is part of the test or when buyers expect an icon on the home screen.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is a checklist enough before enterprise buyers?</h3>
  <p>It is a floor. Larger customers may ask for security questionnaires, data location, and subprocessors. Finish the list, then decide whether a structured review or a production build is the next step.</p>
</div>
