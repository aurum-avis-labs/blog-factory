## Callback phishing explained

Callback phishing is an e-mail or PDF that lists a phone number so you «confirm» a payment, unlock an account, or cancel a subscription. The number reaches the attacker, not your bank, Microsoft, or IT. On the line they harvest MFA codes or install remote software. In Swiss offices the lure often mimics Microsoft 365, a cloud invoice, or HR payroll. The text is for staff in firms of about 20 to 250 people. The attack starts in the mailbox, not at the handset.

## What callback phishing is not

A calendar invite with the support number you already stored. Your MSP calling from the number on their website after you opened a ticket. A genuine bank letter with a hotline you have used for years, not a surprise PDF attachment.

## HR and payroll lures

Callback numbers appear on fake «payslip error» mail, often in January. HR should tell staff that payroll changes only happen in the portal they already use, never via a number in an unexpected PDF. Anyone asked to «quickly confirm» gets the same caution as in [verifying identity on the phone](/security/blog/verifying-identity-on-the-phone).

## Premium-rate numbers and phone bills

Some callbacks route to expensive premium lines. The scam earns on the dial even if you hang up early. If finance sees odd phone bills, check whether someone dialled a mail-supplied number. That is not a data erasure request; [right to erasure under the FADP](/security/blog/right-to-erasure-under-the-fadp) is unrelated to callback numbers.

## Microsoft 365 and the admin portal

Callback mail often claims the tenant is «locked» and only the hotline in the message helps. Admins open the admin portal via a bookmark or ask the MSP through the ticket system. No password and no MFA code on the phone, even when the caller says «Microsoft partner in Zurich». AI text can polish the mail; the test stays the number from your master data.

## Shared mailboxes and reception

`info@` and reception sometimes forward callback mail to finance before anyone checks the number. Rule: do not forward «call immediately» lines unchecked. Reception can say: «We call customers and authorities from our list, not numbers in unexpected mail.»

## Finance and PDF footers

PDF footers show support lines. Finance should never dial them without checking vendor master data. Same rule as IBAN changes: use the number you already have, as with [invoice fraud and a changed IBAN](/security/blog/invoice-fraud-changed-iban).

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>No callbacks to numbers from unexpected mail or PDFs</li>
  <li>IT ticket first, then the contract number</li>
  <li>No MFA codes on the phone, even to «unlock»</li>
  <li>Forward suspicious mail to the internal phishing mailbox</li>
</ul>

## After you dialled: first steps

If you already called and read codes, treat it like clicking phishing: tell IT, reset passwords and sessions, check whether payments left. Do not stay quiet out of embarrassment; early IT notice limits damage. That is not an FADP erasure case for customer data; still document internally what happened.

## Mail filters and headers

IT can block domains from callback mail once someone forwards it. That does not replace a «do not dial» rule, but it stops a second wave. Train that PDF attachments may carry the same number as the message body. Macro-free PDFs are therefore not a trust signal.

## Four-eyes and payment processes

Callback callers often target people who may pay alone. If your firm uses four-eyes for transfers, that still applies after the callback: the caller cannot «override» the rule. Finance should log odd cases such as IBAN requests that cite only a mail number, not ERP master data. Repeat briefly in the monthly meeting when January payroll mail returns.

## Overlap with smishing

Some campaigns list a number by SMS instead of mail. The same rule applies: use the official channel, not the number in the message. Parcel smishing is a separate pattern; callback stays the case where you dial because post or mail suggested it.
