## How to spot phishing emails

You spot phishing emails by checking whether the story matches how your company really works: unexpected parcels, tax messages, bank alerts, or Microsoft 365 sign-in warnings that push you to act in minutes. Look at the sender address, the link destination (hover without clicking), and whether anyone inside the firm already announced the same theme. In Swiss offices, imitations of postal services, rail tickets, cantonal e-tax, banks, and Microsoft 365 are routine.

This guide is for employees and team leads in a Swiss SME with 20 to 250 staff. It complements what to do after a click. Payment fraud with a changed IBAN follows a different playbook, described in [invoice fraud with a changed IBAN](/security/blog/invoice-fraud-changed-iban).

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Does the sender domain match the organisation it claims to be?</li>
  <li>Does the link go to the real site when you hover?</li>
  <li>Is the tone unusually urgent or personal for your role?</li>
  <li>Would this process normally arrive by app, letter, or a known portal?</li>
</ul>

## Sender, display name, and link destination

The visible name in Outlook or Apple Mail may say «Swiss Post» while the address behind it reads `@post-ch-customs.net`. Read the domain right to left: `login-microsoft-secure.ch` is not Microsoft. Short links and QR codes in mail hide the same destination.

On a laptop, hover over the link without clicking. On a phone, long-press the link and read the URL, or open the official app instead of the message. If the mail asks for a password on a website you never use otherwise, stop, even when the layout looks familiar.

## Swiss-looking themes that appear often

**Parcel and delivery notes.** A message claims customs fees or a failed delivery and offers a link to «reschedule». Real carriers usually contact you through the channel you chose at purchase, not a button in unexpected mail.

**Rail and mobility.** Refund or ticket problems are copied often around travel seasons. Check tickets in the SBB app or at sbb.ch, not through the mail link.

**Cantonal e-tax and e-government.** Tax offices use defined portals. They do not ask for your banking password in e-mail. If a canton moves you to eTax, the announcement normally comes through official channels you already use with the administration.

**Banking and TWINT.** Your bank may send alerts, but login pages belong in the banking app or a bookmark you set yourself. TWINT warnings appear in the TWINT app, not as a link from an unfamiliar domain.

**Microsoft 365 and shared documents.** «Shared invoice» or «voice message» links are frequent in business inboxes. Open documents from the OneDrive or Teams interface you already use, or ask the colleague on Teams chat.

<ul class="blog-scenarios">
  <li><h3>Finance and admin</h3><p>A mail «from the CEO» demands an urgent transfer or a new IBAN. Stop rule: never change payment details from mail alone; call back on a number you already know.</p></li>
  <li><h3>Reception and shared mailboxes</h3><p>«Parcel could not be delivered» with a payment link. Stop rule: track shipments only in the shop or carrier the customer chose at purchase.</p></li>
</ul>

## A made-up example

A made-up example: On a Monday morning a mail arrives at `info@` for a fiduciary firm in Lucerne. Sender «Microsoft Security», link «account locked in 24 hours». The receptionist does not open the banking app, forwards to IT, and flags the mail as phishing. IT sees three colleagues received the same template, blocks the domain in the filter, and warns on Teams. Nobody clicked. The company name is invented; the pattern is typical.

## What is no longer proof on its own

A spelling mistake alone does not prove a fake. Legitimate marketing can also come from unfamiliar domains. Context matters: would finance really change supplier bank details by e-mail only? That overlap with [invoice fraud](/security/blog/invoice-fraud-changed-iban) is why finance and admin need their own rules.

## Attachments and internal forwards

Attachments with macros or «enable content» warnings belong in the report folder, not on a shared drive. If a PDF asks for a password «to view the invoice», treat it like any other unexpected attachment. Loading remote images can tell the sender that the address is active. That is reason to report, not to reply.

Internal mailing lists sometimes forward real alerts from IT. Ask once a year whether your IT team sends links or only tells you to open the admin portal yourself. Knowing that pattern removes false alarms and makes fakes stand out.

## Who should not rely on this page alone

IT administrators who tune spam filters need technical documentation, not this article. Private individuals can use consumer advice from police and BACS; here the focus is a shared company mailbox and an IT partner.

## Report instead of debating

When unsure, forward to your internal phishing mailbox or IT partner using the process your employer defined. Early reports help block the same mail for colleagues. External reporting to BACS is covered in a separate how-to article.

Managers should take reports seriously. [Deloitte’s 2026 cyber study](https://www.allnews.ch/sites/default/files/ckfinder/userfiles/Deloitte%20Cyber%20Studie%202026_0.pdf) of firms under 250 employees found 86&nbsp;% consider cyber training helpful. Regular training is reported by 65&nbsp;% of small and 82&nbsp;% of medium firms in that survey. Phishing remains common anyway. Spotting mail is a skill you refresh, not a one-off memo.

</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
