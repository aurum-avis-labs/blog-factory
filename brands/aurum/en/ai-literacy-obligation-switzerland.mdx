**The short answer:** The AI literacy obligation sits in Article 4 of the EU AI Act. Companies that provide AI systems or use them at work must take measures to build their staff's AI literacy. The duty has applied since 2 February 2025. On 27 July 2026 the so-called AI Omnibus watered it down: a "sufficient level" of literacy is no longer required. A Swiss company is caught only if it has an EU link, for example when AI output is used in the EU or an EU subsidiary uses AI. Switzerland itself has no AI law with a training duty. As of October 2026; not legal advice.

This guide is for owners, HR and IT leads in Swiss SMEs. It answers three questions: does this apply to us, what exactly is required, and what does training look like that you can evidence later?

## What Article 4 says today

The EU's Artificial Intelligence Regulation (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, the AI Act) calls it "AI literacy". In German-speaking Switzerland you will mostly hear "KI-Schulungspflicht" or "KI-Kompetenz". Since the amendment by Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, paragraph 1 reads, per the [consolidated text on the European Commission's AI Act Service Desk](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-4):

> "Providers and deployers of AI systems shall take measures to support the development of AI literacy of their staff and other persons dealing with the operation and use of AI systems on their behalf […]. This obligation does not require providers or deployers to guarantee any specific level of AI literacy of any individual."

The measures must take into account staff's technical knowledge, experience, education and training, the context of use, and the people the AI is used on. A "deployer" is anyone using an AI system under their own authority in a professional capacity (Article 3(4)). If your team uses ChatGPT, Microsoft Copilot or an AI translation tool at work, you are a deployer.

### What the AI Omnibus changed

Most pages ranking for this topic still quote the old wording. The difference matters:

| | Until 26 July 2026 | Since 27 July 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Core duty | Measures to ensure "to their best extent" a "sufficient level of AI literacy" | Measures to "support the development of AI literacy" |
| Literacy level | "Sufficient", undefined | Explicitly no specific level guaranteed |
| EU and member states | Not addressed | Must support companies, SMEs in particular, and publish practical examples |
| Enforcement | Duty applied, but market surveillance not yet active | National market surveillance authorities since 2 August 2026 |

The change comes from [Regulation (EU) 2026/1744](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj) of 8 July 2026, the "Digital Omnibus on AI". It was published in the Official Journal on 24 July 2026 and has applied since 27 July 2026. In its [announcement](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-omnibus-enters-force), the Commission sums it up: the previous AI literacy requirement for companies is simplified, with the Commission and member states taking a stronger role. The duty was not repealed. It is still an obligation for providers and deployers, just without a minimum level.

High-risk AI is stricter, for example systems that screen job applications. Deployers must assign human oversight to people with "the necessary competence, training and authority" ([Article 26(2)](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-26)). After the Omnibus, those rules apply from 2 December 2027, and from 2 August 2028 for AI built into products such as machinery ([Article 113](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-113)).

## Does the AI literacy obligation apply to Swiss companies?

Switzerland is not in the EU, so the AI Act does not apply here directly. It can still reach a Swiss company through [Article 2](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2). The Commission's [AI literacy Q&A](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/ai-literacy-questions-answers) confirms this explicitly for Article 4: the Act applies to actors outside the EU "as long as the AI system is placed on the Union market, used in the Union or its use has an impact on people located in the EU."

For a Swiss SME, run through four questions:

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Does your group have a subsidiary, branch or establishment in the EU that uses AI tools? That entity is an EU deployer and Article 4 covers its staff.</li>
  <li>Is your AI output used in the EU, such as AI-drafted quotes, translations, analyses, or a chatbot serving customers in Germany, Austria or France? That is Article 2(1)(c).</li>
  <li>Do you sell a product with AI features into the EU? Then you are a provider.</li>
  <li>Do you use AI to make decisions about people in the EU, for example screening applications from cross-border commuters? Then the high-risk question comes up too, and you need a lawyer.</li>
</ul>

If every answer is no, Article 4 does not apply to you. The Swiss federal [SME portal](https://www.kmu.admin.ch/de/bis-anfang-2027-soll-ein-vorentwurf-zur-ki-regulierung-ausgearbeitet-werden) reads it the same way: providers and deployers from third countries can fall under the AI Act if results generated by an AI system are used in the EU.

<ul class="blog-scenarios">
  <li><h3>Fiduciary firm in Winterthur, Swiss clients only</h3><p>ChatGPT Business for drafts, no EU clients. Article 4 does not apply. A one-page data rule and a short training still make sense, because of the Swiss Data Protection Act, not the EU.</p></li>
  <li><h3>Machine builder in Thurgau selling into southern Germany</h3><p>Sales answers German enquiries with AI help, support runs an AI chatbot on the website. The output is used in the EU. Comply with Article 4 and keep records. Since August 2026 the chatbot also has to make clear to customers that they are talking to an AI.</p></li>
  <li><h3>Group with a GmbH in Konstanz</h3><p>The German entity uses Copilot, so Article 4 applies to it directly. The simplest route is one training programme for both sites.</p></li>
</ul>

Whether the AI Act affects your SME more broadly is a separate question, covered in [The EU AI Act and Swiss SMEs](/prozess-check/blog/eu-ai-act-swiss-smes). This guide stays with training.

## What applies in Switzerland itself

As of October 2026 there is no Swiss AI act. According to the [federal SME portal](https://www.kmu.admin.ch/de/bis-anfang-2027-soll-ein-vorentwurf-zur-ki-regulierung-ausgearbeitet-werden), Switzerland has no specific cross-sector AI rules. Existing law applies, above all data protection.

The federal timeline:

- **12 February 2025:** The Federal Council [comes out in favour](https://www.news.admin.ch/de/nsb?id=104110) of ratifying the Council of Europe AI Convention. Changes should be sector-specific where possible, and the Convention mainly covers state actors. Non-binding measures such as industry solutions are planned alongside.
- **27 March 2025:** Federal Councillor Albert Rösti [signs](https://www.bakom.admin.ch/de/nsb?id=104646) the Convention in Strasbourg.
- **Spring 2027:** The consultation draft was originally due by the end of 2026. According to the SME portal (September 2026), it will now be prepared in spring 2027, covering transparency, data protection, non-discrimination and oversight.

None of this announces an EU-style training duty for companies. Whether one will come is open.

What already applies: the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner ([FDPIC](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/ki-und-datenschutz)) states that the Data Protection Act (FADP) applies directly to AI-based processing. Under [Article 8 FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de), companies must ensure data security appropriate to the risk through suitable technical and organisational measures. The word "training" is not there. But if staff paste customer data into AI tools without anyone explaining the limits, you will struggle to show you met that duty. More in [ChatGPT and customer data](/prozess-check/blog/chatgpt-customer-data-swiss-dsg).

## What the obligation does not require

Plenty of courses sell an "AI Act certificate". The Commission's Q&A is clear:

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>Required</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>Measures matched to role, risk and prior knowledge</li>
      <li>Covering everyone using AI on your behalf, including freelancers and contractors</li>
      <li>Flagging specific risks, such as hallucinations in ChatGPT output</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Not required</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>A certificate: "There is no need for a certificate"</li>
      <li>An AI officer or governance board</li>
      <li>A test measuring each person's knowledge</li>
      <li>Any particular course format</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

The Commission also warns that just asking staff to read the instructions for use is often ineffective, and that penalties become more likely after an incident caused by a lack of training.

<aside class="blog-callout">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Note</span>
  <p>Article 4 is not on the fine list in <a href="https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-99">Article 99(4)</a>. Member states set penalties for Article 4 in national law and must consider SMEs' interests. Claims like "up to EUR 15 million for missing training" have no basis in the AI Act.</p>
</aside>

## Building AI literacy: a five-step plan

The Commission lists four things every programme should cover at minimum: a general understanding of AI, your role (provider or deployer), the risks of the systems you use, and measures tailored to prior knowledge and context. For an SME, that becomes:

  <li data-step="3"><h3>Group people</h3><p>Not everyone needs the same thing. The table below splits by role.</p></li>

What people should be able to do afterwards: <a href="/prozess-check/blog/ai-training-for-employees">AI training for employees</a>.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Record and refresh</h3><p>Log who learned what, when. Include AI in onboarding. Retrain when tools or use cases change.</p></li>

### Who needs what

| Group | Typical people | Content | Effort (guide) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everyone using AI | Admin, sales, accounting | What AI can do and where it fails, hallucinations, allow-list, data rules, review duty | Half a day, plus a short yearly update |
| Heavy users | Marketing, project leads, support | Prompt and context patterns, checking sources, multi-tool workflows | Half to full day |
| Management | Executive team, board | Your role under the AI Act, EU exposure, liability, approvals | Two hours |
| People deciding about staff | HR, team leads | Limits for applications and appraisals, when to call a lawyer | Case by case, with a specialist |
| External parties | Freelancers, agencies | Your rules and data limits, in writing in the contract | Short briefing |

The effort figures are our own guide values, not a legal requirement. Germany's Bundesnetzagentur also recommends a [staged approach](https://www.bundesnetzagentur.de/EN/Areas/Digitalisation/AI/07_Literacy/start.html). It has no say in Switzerland, but it is a useful benchmark.

## Documentation: what evidence looks like

You do not need a certificate. The Commission says an internal record of training and other measures is enough. For each measure, note:

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Date, format (workshop, e-learning, briefing) and duration</li>
  <li>Participants and their roles, including external people</li>
  <li>Content, and which version of the AI policy or allow-list was covered</li>
  <li>Which tools and use cases were included</li>
  <li>Who owns the programme and when the next refresher is due</li>
  <li>Filed materials: agenda, slides or recording, attendance list</li>
</ul>

A SharePoint list or one folder per year does the job. What matters is that someone keeps it up to date. The same record also evidences your organisational measures under the Swiss FADP.

## FAQ

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is AI training mandatory in Switzerland?</h3>
  <p>Not explicitly under Swiss law. The AI literacy duty exists only in the EU AI Act (Article 4), which applies to Swiss firms only with an EU link. If you process personal data with AI, Article 8 FADP still requires appropriate organisational measures, and training is the obvious one.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Did the AI Omnibus abolish the AI literacy obligation?</h3>
  <p>No. Since 27 July 2026, providers and deployers must "support the development" of AI literacy instead of ensuring a "sufficient level". No specific level is required any more, but the duty to take measures remains.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Who enforces Article 4?</h3>
  <p>National market surveillance authorities in EU member states, since August 2026. The Commission's AI Office does not. There is no Article 4 authority in Switzerland; the FDPIC handles data protection.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Do we need a certificate?</h3>
  <p>No. The Commission states plainly that no certificate is needed. An internal training record is enough.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is an online course enough?</h3>
  <p>A generic course covers the basics, but it knows neither your tools nor your data rules. Article 4 asks for measures matched to role and context, so pair a course with an internal session on your own rules.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">

Add internal time: roughly half a day per person, plus preparing the tool inventory and rules. That time is often a bigger cost than the course itself.</p>
</div>
