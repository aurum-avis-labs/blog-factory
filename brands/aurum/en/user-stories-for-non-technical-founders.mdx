<p class="blog-lede">User stories for non-technical founders are short sentences that describe who needs something, what they are trying to do, and why it matters, so a builder can scope one slice of work without reading your entire pitch deck. They are a briefing tool, not a contract, and they work best when each story covers a single outcome you could demo in a few minutes.</p>

This is for founders who have a clear problem, little or no technical co-founder, and a first build or agency conversation ahead. You do not need Jira fluency. You need a shared list of jobs the product must do in v1, written so a developer can ask sensible questions. If you are still arguing what an MVP is allowed to omit, read [what is an MVP](/blog/what-is-an-mvp) first. User stories sit one level below that: they turn "the MVP" into negotiable pieces.

## User stories for non-technical founders: a workable format

The classic shape is: "As a [type of user], I want [action or capability], so that [outcome]." Founders often over-fill the middle clause with product vision. Keep the action concrete. "Upload a supplier invoice" beats "manage documents intelligently".

Stories should be small enough to finish and test in isolation. Teams often use the INVEST checklist (independent, negotiable, valuable, estimable, small, testable) as a smell test. You do not need the acronym on your wall. You need stories that do not secretly contain five features.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Name one real user type</h3><p>Not "everyone". Pick the person who must succeed for v1 to mean anything: the restaurant manager, the tenant, the recruiter.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>State one job, not a roadmap</h3><p>If the sentence contains "and" twice, split it. Login, password reset, and profile edit are three stories, not one.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Add two to five acceptance criteria</h3><p>What must be true when this is done? Include a failure case if it matters for trust or compliance.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Mark what is out of scope</h3><p>"No payment in this story" or "admin only" saves weeks of silent assumptions.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Order by risk, not by screen order</h3><p>Put the story that tests your riskiest assumption near the top of the backlog, not the settings page because it is easy to describe.</p></li>
</ol>

## What belongs in the story, and what belongs elsewhere

A user story carries intent. It does not replace data models, security rules, or API choices. Put those in a short technical note or leave them for the build conversation. If you are hiring, attach criteria the way you would attach a sketch: enough that nobody builds the wrong happy path.

## A made-up example: supplier orders for a small kitchen

A made-up example: a founder plans software for independent restaurant kitchens in Zurich, not a client. They write one epic in the notes ("order from suppliers") but only three stories for the first build.

Story 1: As a head chef, I want to see our usual suppliers in a list, so that I do not retype names every week. Acceptance: list shows name and last order date; empty state if none; no editing supplier legal data in v1.

Story 2: As a head chef, I want to draft an order from past line items, so that Monday morning reordering takes minutes. Acceptance: pick a previous order as a template; change quantities; save as draft without sending.

Story 3: As a head chef, I want to send the draft as an email to the supplier, so that we keep our current process. Acceptance: preview email body; send from a fixed company address; log sent time. Out of scope: payments, stock levels, multi-site permissions.

The founder brings twelve stories in a slide deck. The builder asks which three prove whether chefs will repeat the flow. They cut to these three. That is the point of stories at MVP stage: negotiable scope, not completeness.

## What user stories do not prove

Writing crisp stories does not validate demand. It does not show that strangers will pay, that your unit economics work, or that a vibe-coded prototype is safe to scale. Stories align build work; they are not market research.

They also do not force good architecture. A story that says "user can log in" can still be implemented with brittle auth if nobody discussed sessions, roles, or Swiss hosting. When the codebase already exists in Cursor or Lovable, stories help you describe the next slice to refactor or harden, not a substitute for a review.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>A backlog of user stories is not a specification for regulated software, financial products, or medical devices. If liability, audit trails, or professional secrecy matter, add explicit requirements with your adviser and your builder. Stories stay human-readable; they do not replace compliance work.</p>
</aside>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>How many user stories does an MVP need?</h3>
  <p>Enough to cover one complete loop for one user type, usually roughly five to fifteen small stories, not fifty. If the list reads like a three-year roadmap, you are describing a product suite, not an MVP.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Should non-technical founders write stories in English?</h3>
  <p>Use the language your builder and your users share. For a Swiss team, German or English is fine if everyone uses the same terms for roles and objects. Mixing languages inside one backlog creates silent mismatches.</p>
</div>
