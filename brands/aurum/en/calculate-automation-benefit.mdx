## How to calculate the benefit of an automation

You calculate the benefit of an automation in three moves. Put a yearly value on what it removes: the hours saved, priced at your internal hourly cost, plus the errors it prevents. Subtract what it costs to run each year. Then divide the one-off cost by what is left, and you have the payback in months. If nothing is left after running costs, or the payback is longer than the limit you set beforehand, the answer is "don't".

This is for owners and office leads who want to test a decision on paper before they spend anything. [When automation pays off](/prozess-check/blog/when-automation-pays-off-small-business) gives the rough threshold and the reasons behind it. This article is the sheet you fill in. Where the price ranges come from is in [what automation costs in Switzerland](/prozess-check/blog/what-automation-costs-switzerland).

## The template: six numbers to collect

Six numbers give you the whole calculation, and you can count all of them within a week. Wherever you are unsure, write a low and a high case instead of one figure.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Runs per year</h3><p>Count the runs of the last four weeks and multiply, instead of trusting memory.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Minutes by hand, before and after</h3><p>Time three real runs and add up everyone who touches the task, including questions and corrections. Then estimate what stays by hand afterwards, usually a check of the exceptions. Saved minutes are the difference, not the whole run.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Your internal hourly cost</h3><p>Yearly gross pay plus the employer's social charges, divided by the hours a person really works in a year. Your payroll shows the actual percentage. This is a cost, not the rate you bill customers.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Errors the automation would prevent</h3><p>How many were there last year, and what did a typical one cost in correction time: a credit note, a phone call, a document sent twice? Count only errors that come from the manual step.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Waiting and single-person risk, in words</h3><p>Who waits for the result, and for how long? What happens when the one person who knows the task is away? Write one sentence for each. Put a number on it only if you can name the cost.</p></li>
  <li data-step="6"><h3>Costs: one-off and running</h3><p>One-off is the build plus the hours your own people spend explaining the task and testing it. Running is hosting and operation every month, plus AI service costs by usage if a model is involved. Keep the two apart.</p></li>
</ol>

For orientation only: the Swiss earnings structure survey puts the [gross median monthly wage at CHF 7'024](https://www.admin.ch/en/newnsb/CzozNgBTCJOrNyvwsItyp) for a full-time position in 2024. Your payroll is the source for step 3, not a median.

Then put the numbers together in this order:

- Hours saved per year = runs per year × saved minutes ÷ 60
- Benefit per year = hours saved × hourly cost + errors avoided × cost per error
- Net benefit per year = benefit per year − running cost per year
- Payback in months = one-off cost ÷ net benefit per year × 12

## A worked example, line by line

A clearly hypothetical example: a maintenance company with about 25 people sends around 60 recurring invoices a month for service contracts. Today the office copies the contract data into an invoice template, adds the month's extras, exports a PDF and mails it. That takes 10 minutes per invoice, plus 5 more for the office lead's check. After automation, drafts appear from the contract list, and 3 minutes per invoice remain to check what the workflow flagged.

Three more assumptions. About three invoices a month go out wrong, and each costs 30 minutes to fix with a credit note and a new invoice. The automation halves that. The hourly cost is CHF 72'000 gross plus 15 percent employer charges, so CHF 82'800, divided by 1'800 working hours: CHF 46. All of these are assumptions. Replace them with yours.

The task fits our Small package: one clearly bounded workflow with few systems, 2–3 days, guide price CHF 3'200–4'800. Hosting and operation add CHF 50–150 a month. There are no AI service costs here, because filling a template from a list needs rules, not a model. Ten hours of the office's own time for explaining and testing count as one-off cost. The CHF 99 Process Check is not a separate line, because it is credited in full if you book the package.

| Line | Calculation | Low end | High end |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A. Hours saved per year | 60 × 12 = 720 runs × (15 − 3) minutes ÷ 60 | 144 h | 144 h |
| B. Value of those hours | 144 × CHF 46 | CHF 6'624 | CHF 6'624 |
| C. Value of avoided errors | 18 fewer errors (half of 36) × 0.5 h × CHF 46 | CHF 414 | CHF 414 |
| D. Benefit per year | B + C | CHF 7'038 | CHF 7'038 |
| E. Running cost per year | 12 × CHF 50 or CHF 150 | CHF 600 | CHF 1'800 |
| F. Net benefit per year | D − E | CHF 6'438 | CHF 5'238 |
| G. One-off cost | Small package plus 10 h × CHF 46 | CHF 3'660 | CHF 5'260 |
| H. Payback | G ÷ F × 12 | about 7 months | about 12 months |

The example pays back in 7 to 12 months. Over three years it is roughly CHF 21'100 of benefit against CHF 5'500–10'700 of cost.

Two things stay outside the number. Invoices leave on the 7th instead of the 1st, so every customer pays about a week late. And only the office lead knows which contracts have extras. Both would make a yes easier. Neither is needed for this one. These are guide prices. The fixed price for a real task is confirmed in writing after the Check.

## When the number says don't

The number says don't when nothing is left after running costs, or when the payback is longer than your limit. Set that limit before you start, for example 12 or 24 months, so the result cannot bend it.

Take the same company with 8 recurring invoices a month instead of 60. That is 96 runs a year, and 96 × 12 minutes ÷ 60 gives 19.2 hours, or CHF 883. Errors are rare and left out. Against CHF 600–1'800 of running cost, the net benefit is CHF 283 at the low end and minus CHF 917 at the high end. At the low end, the CHF 3'660 pays back in about 13 years. At the high end it never does, because the automation costs more to run than the work it replaces.

Here a written instruction and a second person who have done the task twice cost less, and they close the holiday gap as well. A good result also needs the saved hours to go somewhere. If nobody waits for the result and the person has slack, saved minutes are not saved money, as [when automation pays off](/prozess-check/blog/when-automation-pays-off-small-business) explains.

<ul class="blog-scenarios">
  <li><h3>If the payback is within your limit at both ends</h3><p>The numbers support it. Ask for a fixed price for your task and check it against the same lines.</p></li>
  <li><h3>If your limit falls inside the range</h3><p>The range is too wide to decide on. A fixed price for the real task narrows it. A soft factor you can put a cost on can also decide, such as a payment that arrives a week late every month.</p></li>
  <li><h3>If nothing is left after running costs</h3><p>Do not automate. Fix the task with a checklist, a template or a second person, and count again if the volume grows.</p></li>
</ul>
