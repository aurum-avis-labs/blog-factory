## How to calculate the benefit of an automation

You calculate the benefit of an automation in three moves. Put a yearly value on what it removes: hours saved at your internal hourly cost, plus errors it prevents. Subtract yearly running cost. Divide one-off cost by what is left for payback in months. If nothing remains after running cost, or payback exceeds the limit you set beforehand, the answer is "don't".

Owners and office leads who want to test a decision on paper before spending anything are the audience. [When automation pays off](/prozess-check/blog/when-automation-pays-off-small-business) gives the rough threshold. This article is the sheet you fill in. Build and running price ranges are in [what automation costs in Switzerland](/prozess-check/blog/what-automation-costs-switzerland).

## The template: six numbers to collect

Six numbers give you the whole calculation. You can gather all of them within a week. Where you are unsure, write a low and a high case instead of one figure.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Runs per year</h3><p>Count runs in the last four weeks and scale up, instead of trusting memory.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Minutes by hand, before and after</h3><p>Time three real runs and add everyone who touches the task, including questions and corrections. Estimate what stays manual afterwards, usually checking exceptions. Saved minutes are the difference, not the whole run. Subtracting the full old path overstates the benefit.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Your internal hourly cost</h3><p>Yearly gross pay plus employer social charges, divided by hours a person really works in a year. Payroll shows the percentage. This is cost, not your customer rate.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>Errors the automation would prevent</h3><p>How many last year, and what did a typical one cost in correction time? Count only errors from the manual step.</p></li>
  <li data-step="5"><h3>Waiting and single-person risk, in words</h3><p>Who waits for the result, and for how long? What happens when the one person who knows the task is away? Put a figure only if you can name the cost.</p></li>
  <li data-step="6"><h3>Costs: one-off and running</h3><p>One-off: build plus your people's hours explaining and testing. Running: hosting and operation each month, plus AI usage if a model is involved.</p></li>
</ol>

For orientation only: the Swiss earnings structure survey puts the [gross median monthly wage at CHF 7'024](https://www.admin.ch/en/newnsb/CzozNgBTCJOrNyvwsItyp) for a full-time position in 2024. Payroll is the source for step 3.

Set a payback limit before step 1, for example 18 months. Compare the table result to that limit, not to a gut feeling afterwards.

Then combine the numbers in this order:

- Hours saved per year = runs per year × saved minutes ÷ 60
- Benefit per year = hours saved × hourly cost + errors avoided × cost per error
- Net benefit per year = benefit per year − running cost per year
- Payback in months = one-off cost ÷ net benefit per year × 12

## A worked example, line by line

A clearly hypothetical example: a maintenance company with about 25 people sends around 60 recurring invoices a month for service contracts. Today the office copies contract data into a template, adds monthly extras, exports a PDF and mails it. That takes 10 minutes per invoice, plus 5 for the office lead's check. After automation, drafts come from the contract list; 3 minutes per invoice remain to check what the workflow flagged.

Further assumptions: about three invoices a month go out wrong, each costing 30 minutes to fix. The automation halves that. Hourly cost: CHF 72'000 gross plus 15 percent employer charges, divided by 1'800 working hours: CHF 46. Replace with your own figures.

The task fits our Small package: one bounded workflow with few systems, 2–3 days, guide price CHF 3'200–4'800. Hosting and operation add CHF 50–150 a month. No AI service costs, because a list filling a template needs rules, not a model. Ten hours of office time for explaining and testing count as one-off cost. | A. Hours saved per year | 60 × 12 = 720 runs × (15 − 3) minutes ÷ 60 | 144 h | 144 h |
| B. Value of those hours | 144 × CHF 46 | CHF 6'624 | CHF 6'624 |
| C. Value of avoided errors | 18 fewer errors (half of 36) × 0.5 h × CHF 46 | CHF 414 | CHF 414 |
| D. Benefit per year | B + C | CHF 7'038 | CHF 7'038 |
| E. Running cost per year | 12 × CHF 50 or CHF 150 | CHF 600 | CHF 1'800 |
| F. Net benefit per year | D − E | CHF 6'438 | CHF 5'238 |

| H. Payback | G ÷ F × 12 | about 7 months | about 12 months |

The example pays back in 7 to 12 months. Over three years it is roughly CHF 21'100 of benefit against CHF 5'500–10'700 of cost.

Two factors sit outside the table. Invoices leave on the 7th instead of the 1st, so customers pay about a week late. Only the office lead knows some extras on certain contracts. Both would make a yes easier. You need neither for this yes.

If you want a soft factor in francs, count conservatively: a week of payment delay on the average invoice times 720 invoices a year, or the hours the office lead spends on questions during a holiday week. If step 5 only says "important" with no figure, leave the line blank. The table stays more honest than invented precision.

## When the number says don't

The number says don't when nothing is left after running costs, or when payback exceeds your limit. Set that limit before you start, for example 12 or 24 months.

The same company with 8 recurring invoices a month: 96 runs a year, 96 × 12 minutes ÷ 60 gives 19.2 hours, or CHF 883. Errors stay out. Against CHF 600–1'800 running cost, net benefit is CHF 283 at the low end and minus CHF 917 at the high end. At the low end, CHF 3'660 pays back in about 13 years. At the high end it never does, because running cost exceeds the manual work.

Here a written instruction and a second person who has done the task twice often cost less. That closes the holiday gap without tying up CHF 3'660 in a workflow that runs rarely. Still check whether the volume doubles within two years; then a new sheet with the same six numbers is worth it. Saved minutes become money only if someone waits for the result, as [when automation pays off](/prozess-check/blog/when-automation-pays-off-small-business) explains.
