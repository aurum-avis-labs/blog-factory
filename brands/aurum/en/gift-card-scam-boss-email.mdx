## Gift card scam boss email: vouchers instead of wire transfers

A gift card scam boss email asks an assistant or finance clerk to buy Apple, Google, or retail vouchers "for a surprise for staff" and send the codes immediately by reply. The message impersonates the chief executive, stresses secrecy, and avoids phone calls. It is business email compromise: the payout is redeemable codes, not a new IBAN. The [Federal Office for Cybersecurity (BACS)](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/26w4-de) groups such cases with CEO fraud trends; overall CEO fraud reports reached 971 in 2025 (external statistic).

It differs from [CEO fraud in Switzerland](/security/blog/ceo-fraud-switzerland) that pushes bank wires, and from [deepfake CEO voice call fraud](/security/blog/deepfake-ceo-voice-call-fraud) on the phone. Codes sent by mail or chat from a personal phone tie to [BYOD security](/security/blog/byod-security-for-a-small-business).

## Warning signs in the message

Multiple voucher types in one request, unusually large totals for your firm size, payment by personal card with promised reimbursement, or a Gmail address that almost matches your real domain. Attackers may follow up if you delay. Some threads start from compromised real mailboxes, which makes headers look legitimate.

Variations include photos of card backs, split purchases across stores, "reimbursement later", or requests via Teams chat instead of mail. Smaller amounts sometimes test your process first; still stop and report.

## What to do first

Do not buy cards. Preserve the message or chat, alert IT and the real executive, and report internally as in [internal incident reporting](/security/blog/internal-incident-reporting). If cards were already sent, contact your insurer and police with codes, times, and receipts. Voluntary NCSC reporting is optional for most SMEs; steps are in [how to report phishing in Switzerland](/security/blog/how-to-report-phishing-in-switzerland).

## How this differs from wires and voice fraud

No IBAN change, yet the same CEO fraud pattern: impersonation, pressure, bypass of normal approvals. Use the same verification as for [CEO fraud in Switzerland](/security/blog/ceo-fraud-switzerland). The [four-eyes principle for payments](/security/blog/four-eyes-principle-for-payments) applies in spirit: no voucher purchase without a second person or call-back, even when the amount looks "small".

## If codes were already sent

Contact the issuer immediately with proof of fraud; recovery is uncertain. Preserve mail headers for police. File online via cybercrime.ch with screenshots; document internally in parallel. Finance should check whether further payments are queued from the same thread.

## Rules for reception and assistants

Reception should not silently forward "urgent CEO mail"; ask for the approval process instead. Assistants and finance agree a stop word ("call-back") and practise it in training. Attackers often name specific chains; the stop rule covers all brands, not only those mentioned.

## Technical follow-up

IT should review forwarding rules and compromised mailboxes. Context on Microsoft 365 hardening: [Microsoft 365 and Swiss data protection](/security/blog/microsoft-365-and-swiss-data-protection). That does not replace a call-back on the known executive number.

## Why attackers prefer gift cards to wires

Codes resell quickly and are harder to recover than a stopped bank payment. The mail can feel "less financial" than CEO fraud with an IBAN change even when the loss is real. The share of pure gift-card cases among all CEO fraud reports in Switzerland is not cleanly broken out in public statistics; your signal is the concrete message, not an industry ratio.

## Approval in finance

Define in writing: no voucher purchase without a second person or call-back, whatever the amount. Assistants may say "no" without asking the executive by reply mail. Finance sees aggregated reports, not naming individuals. That aligns with the [four-eyes principle for payments](/security/blog/four-eyes-principle-for-payments) even when no bank account changes.

## After the attempt

IT checks whether the executive or assistant mailbox has forwarding rules. Reset passwords if the mail came from a real account. Send a short internal note to reception and assistants without panic. Police and insurer only per your internal checklist; voluntary NCSC reporting if you want to flag a pattern.

## What you do not need to do

You do not need to set a "trap" or identify the attacker yourself. No counter-purchase "to test". Do not reply with insults; just stop and report. Legal classification stays with management and its internal or external contacts.

## Typical phrases in the scam message

"I need this within the next two hours", "confidential team event", "reply only to this address", or "I cannot take calls right now" appear often. The combination of urgency and secrecy is the real warning sign, not any single word. If the wording genuinely does not sound like your executive, that is one more reason to call back. Store suspicious mails in the internal ticket system, not only in an assistant's private folders. IT needs the originals.
