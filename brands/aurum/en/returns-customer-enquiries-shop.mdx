## Handle returns and customer enquiries in an online shop

You handle returns and customer enquiries in an online shop by sorting each message into one of four types, drafting the reply from that order, and sending only the remainder to a person. The four types are: where is my order, a return label, the status of a refund, and an exchange. Money, a promise you did not mean, and a damaged item stay with a person.

This is for the small team that reads the shop inbox, not for a complaints desk in a factory. What the shop system already sends, and where AI does not belong, is in [AI for small retailers and online shops](/blog/ai-for-retail-ecommerce). Sorting a mixed company inbox in general is in [sorting a shared inbox](/blog/shared-inbox-sort-emails). Here the point is that the answer has to match one order, and that a return follows rules you wrote down.

## Step 1: Four types, and a pile for the rest

Read the message for the job, not for the mood. Most shop mail is one of four jobs.

- Where is my order, or can I change the address before it ships.
- I want to send it back: a label, a deadline, what happens to the postage.
- Where is my refund, after a return you already accepted.
- I want a different size or colour, an exchange rather than money.

Everything else goes to a second pile: the item arrived broken, the customer says you promised a date, there is no order number, or the message is not about an order at all. [ch.ch](https://www.ch.ch/en/safety-and-justice/returning-or-exchanging-goods) is clear that Switzerland has no statutory right to return or exchange goods, online or in a store. Each shop sets its own rules. A model that has read other countries will offer 14 days unless you tell it your rule. Do not let it.

<ul class="blog-scenarios">
  <li><h3>If the order number is in the mail</h3><p>Open that order. Draft from its status. Do not ask the customer to repeat it.</p></li>
  <li><h3>If the number is missing</h3><p>Ask for it, or for the email used at checkout. Do not search three systems by a first name and guess.</p></li>
  <li><h3>If the goods are said to be faulty</h3><p>That is not a change-of-mind return. Put it on the pile a person handles. Warranty is a different job.</p></li>
</ul>

## Step 2: Where is my order, from the shipment you already have

If the shop or the carrier record shows a status, the draft says that status, the date it changed, and where the customer can see it. If the status is "not shipped", the draft says that, and it does not invent a delivery day. If two parcels went out, the draft names which one the question is about, or asks which, rather than describing the wrong parcel.

The person sending the mail checks that the draft matches the order in front of them. A sentence that sounds helpful and is not on the order is the sentence you delete. Address changes before shipment can be a draft too, but only if you have not yet handed the parcel to the carrier. After that, the draft says what the record shows, and a person decides whether a stop is still possible.

## Step 3: A return label only when your terms say yes

Write the return rule down once, in fields a workflow can check: how many days from delivery, which articles are excluded, who pays postage, whether you want the item back before you refund, and whether an exchange is allowed. Then the check is the same every time. Inside the window, the right article, not excluded: the draft confirms the return and, if you use labels, that a label will follow. Outside the window, or an excluded article such as a hygiene item you have listed: the draft says no, in the words you approved, and a person still sees it before it goes.

A voluntary window of 14 days, if you offer one, is your term. It is not the rule for every Swiss online order. The 14-day cancellation [ch.ch](https://www.ch.ch/en/safety-and-justice/returning-or-exchanging-goods) describes applies to door-to-door sales and to contracts agreed on the telephone, when the goods are worth more than CHF 100. An ordinary order on your website is not automatically that case. Do not copy that deadline into your shop mail unless it is actually your term, or unless that rare case really is what the customer concluded. This is a practical reading, not legal advice.

The label itself is a file from the carrier or from your shop, attached or sent when the rule passes. The workflow prepares it. A person confirms it when the case was flagged.

## Step 4: Refund status is a fact, not a tone

"Where is my money?" is answered from the refund record, not from a wish to calm the customer. If the refund is marked as sent, the draft says the date and the amount. If the return has arrived but the refund has not been released, the draft says that, and it does not say the money is on the way. If you have no return in the system, the draft says so and asks for the tracking of the return parcel, rather than promising to look "as soon as possible" with no date.

Exchange is the same discipline. The draft offers the size or colour only if that variant is the one you allow on the rule, and only if the order shows the original item. A different product, a goodwill gesture, or a price difference is a person's decision. The workflow can show the price difference. It should not waive it.

## Step 5: What never gets an automatic answer

Keep a short list of messages that always go to a person, with no draft sent on its own:

- The item is damaged, incomplete, or described as dangerous.
- The customer quotes a promise, a phone agreement, or a price that is not on the order.
- There is a threat of a complaint to an authority, a lawyer, or a review that includes personal data of your staff.
- The order cannot be found, or two customers seem to share one number.
- The message is not in a language your team checks. A draft you cannot read is not a draft you should send. Replies across languages, once a person will check them, are covered in [multilingual customer messages](/blog/multilingual-customer-communication).

Sorting the inbox still matters. The shared-inbox article is the general method. The shop adds the order as the source of the draft, and your terms as the source of yes or no.

## A made-up example: forty messages, four types

A made-up example: a Swiss online shop for household goods, two people on the inbox, about 40 customer messages on a busy day. Roughly half ask where the parcel is. The rest are returns, refund questions, and a few exchanges. Before, both people opened the shop, searched the name, and wrote from scratch. On a Monday the same sender wrote twice, and the second reply contradicted the first.

In the changed route, each message is marked as one of the four types, or as "person". Order number present: the draft quotes the status already stored. Order number missing: the draft asks for the checkout email and does not look up a surname. Return inside the 30 days this shop has written into its terms, article not on the exclusion list: the draft confirms and a label is prepared. Day 41: the draft declines, in the sentence the shop approved. Three messages skip the draft entirely: a broken glass, a claim that someone on the phone promised free return postage, and a refund question where the payment record shows nothing sent. One person works that list. The other sends the drafts that match the order. What stays is the judgement. What goes is searching for an order you already have a number for. How much time that is depends on how many messages you get and how long one takes today.

## When to leave it

Leave it if a handful of messages a week are cleared by the person who also packs the parcels. A shared folder and your terms printed next to the screen will cost less. Leave it too if your terms are not written down. A workflow cannot apply a rule that lives in one person's head. Write the window, the exclusions and the postage first. Use them for a few weeks. Then automate the check.
