## Paying a development partner in equity

Paying a development partner in equity means trading a slice of your company for product work instead of (or alongside) cash invoices. It can align incentives when cash is tight and the partner will stay involved after the first release. It does not remove the need for a clear scope, a written assignment of intellectual property, and honest answers about what the build is meant to prove. Treat equity as a payment method with governance consequences, not as a discount on an undefined project.

This is for founders and innovation leads in Swiss companies who have a serious product hypothesis but cannot or will not fund a full agency bill in cash alone. If you are still comparing labels, the difference between a studio, an agency, and an accelerator is set out in [venture studio vs agency](/blog/venture-studio-vs-agency). How fixed scope interacts with price, and what an app costs in Switzerland, are separate budget questions.

## What equity is buying

Equity pays for judgment and labour over time, not for an idea on its own. Under Swiss law, ideas as such are not protectable; what you can contract for is the work product and the rights to use it. If the partner is an employee, employer ownership of software follows employment rules. If they are external, copyright typically stays with the author until you have a written transfer or licence. Equity without a signed IP and confidentiality package is how founders discover later that they do not own the repository.

Before anyone talks about shares, agree in writing:

- What will be built in the first phase, and what is explicitly out of scope
- Who owns code, designs, data exports, and documentation when invoices or grants are settled
- How changes are handled when the product direction shifts (change process, not silent scope creep)
- Whether the partner has a board seat, observer rights, or only economic shares

None of that is exotic. It is the same discipline you would apply to a cash fixed-price engagement, with cap-table paperwork added.

## Cash, equity, or a mix

Most serious partners accept cash, equity, or a mix. The split is discussed in a discovery conversation, not in a blog post. What you should decide internally first is how much risk each side carries.

Cash reduces ambiguity: you pay, you own the deliverables per contract, the relationship can end when the statement of work ends. Equity shifts risk to the partner and ties them to your fundraising, your governance, and your future rounds. A mix often reflects uneven cash on hand while still giving the partner skin in the game.

<ul class="blog-facts">
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Cash</span><strong>Clear exit</strong><p>The vendor relationship ends when the contract ends, subject to support terms.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Equity</span><strong>Shared upside</strong><p>The partner wins if the company wins; dilution and voting follow company law.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Mix</span><strong>Balanced risk</strong><p>Lower immediate burn with aligned incentives if governance is clean.</p></li>
</ul>

Equity does not magically fix a weak hypothesis. It pays for execution and partnership attention; it does not replace measurement. If the open question is whether anyone will use the product or pay for it, you still need a validation plan, not only a generous grant of shares.

## What an equity deal does not prove

Granting shares to a builder does not show product-market fit. It shows that two parties agreed on risk sharing. A polished MVP funded with equity can still fail acquisition tests. Conversely, a cash project can produce excellent code that nobody needs.

Equity also does not replace legal and tax advice. Share issuances touch company form (for example GmbH minimum capital rules), shareholder agreements, and often tax timing for founders and the partner. This article is product and contracting context, not counsel.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>Do not sign an equity-for-development term sheet because cash is uncomfortable. If you cannot describe the first release, the IP transfer, and what happens when you pivot, pause and get advice before you issue shares.</p>
</aside>

## Who equity fits, and who should keep cash

<div class="blog-pair">
  <div>
    <h3>Equity can fit</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>You have a defined first product phase and a partner who will challenge scope, not only implement tickets</li>
      <li>Both sides want long-term alignment and can document IP and governance</li>
      <li>Cash would delay a validation window you are willing to measure with analytics and acquisition tests</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
  <div>
    <h3>Keep cash or walk away</h3>
    <ul>
      <li>You only need a brochure site or a slide deck dressed as an app</li>
      <li>The partner refuses written IP assignment or treats equity as a substitute for a statement of work</li>
      <li>You are uncomfortable explaining the cap table to your next investor</li>
    </ul>
  </div>
</div>

Studios that take equity usually do so because they invest product judgment, not because they undercut hourly shops. Agencies optimised for spec execution may prefer cash entirely. Neither model is wrong; mismatch is.

<dl class="blog-terms">
  <div>
    <dt>Vesting</dt>
    <dd>Shares or options earned over time or milestones, so a partner cannot walk away on day two with full ownership.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>IP assignment</dt>
    <dd>Written transfer of copyright and related rights in the software and designs delivered under the project.</dd>
  </div>
  <div>
    <dt>Dilution</dt>
    <dd>Future funding rounds reduce each holder's percentage even when the number of shares stays the same.</dd>
  </div>
</dl>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Can we pay in equity if we only have a GmbH and little cash?</h3>
  <p>Forming a company is not the same as having a clean share register. Equity for services still needs documented issuance, IP transfer, and usually tax input. Low cash is a reason to discuss a mix, not to skip paperwork.</p>
</div>

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Does equity mean the partner becomes a co-founder?</h3>
  <p>Only if you design it that way. Economic shares without roles, vesting, and decision rights are a common source of conflict. Name the role, the deliverables, and the exit paths in writing.</p>
</div>
