## Reporting a data breach in Switzerland: the short answer

In Switzerland, you report a data breach to the Federal Data Protection and Information Commissioner (FDPIC, EDÖB in German) when it is likely to result in a high risk to the people affected. You file "as soon as possible" through the portal [databreach.edoeb.admin.ch](https://databreach.edoeb.admin.ch). Swiss law sets no fixed deadline like the GDPR's 72 hours. Reported or not, you record every breach internally and keep that record for at least two years.

This guide is for management, IT and whoever handles data protection in a Swiss SME. It is based on [Art. 24 of the Federal Act on Data Protection (FADP)](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de), [Art. 15 of the Data Protection Ordinance (DPO)](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/568/de) and the [FDPIC guide](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/leitfaden-data-breach), version 1.2 of 23 April 2025 (as of October 2026). It is not legal advice for your specific case.

## Which authority do you report to?

The most common mix-up starts with the authority. Searches for "report a data breach" often surface cantonal data protection offices or EU regulators. A private company in Switzerland falls under federal law, so the FDPIC is responsible.

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Your situation</th><th>Report to</th><th>Deadline</th><th>Legal basis</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Private company, association, foundation</td><td>FDPIC, DataBreach portal</td><td>As soon as possible</td><td>Art. 24 FADP</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Cantonal or municipal public body (school, hospital, municipality)</td><td>Cantonal data protection authority</td><td>Under cantonal law</td><td>Cantonal data protection act</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Critical infrastructure operator, cyberattack</td><td>Also the BACS</td><td>24 hours after discovery</td><td>Information Security Act (ISA)</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Processing also falls under the GDPR</td><td>Also the competent EU authority</td><td>Without undue delay, where feasible within 72 hours</td><td>Art. 33 GDPR</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

These duties run in parallel. A report to the Federal Office for Cyber Security (BACS) does not replace the FDPIC report, and vice versa. According to the [BACS](https://www.bacs.admin.ch/de/25-mp-de), the 24-hour duty has applied since 1 April 2025 to critical infrastructure operators such as energy and drinking water suppliers, transport companies and cantonal and municipal administrations. Fines for failing to report have been possible since 1 October 2025. On request, the BACS form can forward the report to the FDPIC. Most SMEs in trade, crafts or services are not covered by the BACS duty. The FDPIC duty applies to everyone.

## What counts as a data breach

The law speaks of a "breach of data security". Under [Art. 5(h) FADP](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/491/de), it occurs when personal data is accidentally or unlawfully lost, deleted, destroyed or altered, or disclosed or made accessible to unauthorised persons. That covers three kinds of incident:

- **Confidentiality:** data reaches the wrong people, for example through an email to the wrong recipient, a hacked mailbox or a cloud folder shared publicly.
- **Integrity:** data is changed without authorisation, such as bank details in accounts payable.
- **Availability:** data is gone or encrypted, for example after ransomware or a failed backup.

Incidents without personal data are outside Art. 24 FADP. The FDPIC guide states that even serious breaches of trade or manufacturing secrets are not reportable as long as no personal data is involved. A stolen engineering drawing is a security problem, but not a case for the FDPIC.

## Report or not? The decision table

Not every breach is reportable, only one that is "likely" to result in a high risk. The FDPIC guide lists the criteria: how sensitive the data is, how easily people can be identified, who has the data now, how much data per person is affected and whether vulnerable people are involved. What counts is the person most severely affected, not the average.

Two points from the guide are often missed. First, measures you only plan or announce after the incident do not count in the risk assessment. Immediate measures that demonstrably worked before you reported can count, for example if you can rule out access to the data within hours based on logs. Second, trust is not enough. According to the guide, an unsubstantiated, subjective trust in the good intentions of unknown recipients does not rule out a high risk.

Three examples from everyday SME life:

- **Fiduciary firm:** an employee sends the salary certificates of 40 clients to the wrong external address. Salary, AHV number and address are now with a stranger. High risk is likely, so you report. How to handle the first minutes is covered in [Email sent to the wrong recipient](/security/blog/email-sent-to-the-wrong-recipient).
- **Construction company:** a site foreman loses a tablet with site plans and subcontractors' phone numbers. The device is encrypted with BitLocker and locked via MDM. The data counts as anonymous, so you record it internally.
- **Medical practice:** after someone clicks a phishing link, an attacker gets into the practice mailbox containing medical certificates. Health data is sensitive personal data, so you report. What to do right after the click is in [Clicked a phishing link at work](/security/blog/clicked-a-phishing-link-at-work).

## "As soon as possible" instead of 72 hours

Art. 24(1) FADP requires a report "as soon as possible". The law names no number of hours. That does not mean you have more time than in the EU. The FDPIC guide is clear: if you see a likely high risk and cannot rule it out with certainty quickly, you must report. You may not wait for lengthy investigations. For ransomware, this explicitly applies even when it is unclear what the attackers will do with the data, before or after any ransom payment.

<ul class="blog-facts">
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">Switzerland (FADP)</span><strong>As soon as possible</strong><p>From the moment you know. Missing details may follow later.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">EU (GDPR)</span><strong>72 hours</strong><p>Without undue delay and where feasible within 72 hours, otherwise with reasons for the delay.</p></li>
  <li><span class="blog-kicker">BACS (ISA)</span><strong>24 hours</strong><p>Critical infrastructure only. 14 days to complete the report.</p></li>
</ul>

In practice: once you can describe the incident in broad terms, report it. The forensic report can follow. [Art. 15(2) DPO](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2022/568/de) explicitly allows this, and the portal supports follow-up reports. Note in your internal log when you learned what. It lets you show later that you acted promptly.

## What goes into the report: template

The portal asks for the details listed in Art. 15(1) DPO. With these prepared, the report takes little time. Copy this template into your incident handbook:

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li><strong>a. Nature of the breach:</strong> what happened? For example "misdirected email", "unauthorised mailbox access", "ransomware with data exfiltration".</li>
  <li><strong>b. Time and duration:</strong> start, discovery, end (as far as known).</li>
  <li><strong>c. Data affected:</strong> categories and approximate number of records, e.g. "about 1,200 customer records with name, address, order history".</li>
  <li><strong>d. People affected:</strong> categories and approximate number, e.g. "customers, employees".</li>
  <li><strong>e. Consequences and risks:</strong> what could happen to those affected? Phishing, identity misuse, exposure, financial loss.</li>
  <li><strong>f. Measures:</strong> what have you done and what is planned to close the gap and reduce the consequences?</li>
  <li><strong>g. Contact person:</strong> name, role, phone and email of someone who can be reached.</li>
</ul>

After submitting, you can download a confirmation with the details you reported. It also records when you reported. File it with your internal log.

## Informing the people affected

Informing those affected is a separate duty and does not depend on high risk. Under Art. 24(4) FADP, you inform them if it is necessary for their protection or if the FDPIC requests it. According to the guide, this is mainly the case when people can or must act themselves: change passwords, block credit cards, check bank statements or watch out for phishing emails. Conversely, a high risk usually also points to informing them.

The message must be in simple and clear language and include at least: what happened, the consequences and risks, what you have done or plan to do, and a contact person with contact details (Art. 15(3) DPO). There is no required format. As a rule, though, you inform people directly and individually. A public announcement is the exception, for instance when informing each person is impossible or would take disproportionate effort.

> On 12 October, an unauthorised person gained access to our customer portal. Your name, email address and order history were affected, but not your payment data. We have blocked the access and reset all passwords. Please choose a new password the next time you log in and be careful with emails sent in our name. If you have questions, contact Ms Muster on 044 000 00 00.

## What happens after you report

According to the guide, the FDPIC briefly checks whether your measures are suitable, sufficient and timely. It may ask for clarification, call for additional measures or order you to inform those affected. If you do not comply, it can open a formal investigation.

Four points SMEs should know:

- **No penalty for failing to report as such.** The guide states that ignoring the duty to report is not a criminal offence under the FADP. The FDPIC can, however, order you to report after the fact (Art. 51(3)(f) FADP). A fine of up to CHF 250,000 can apply to anyone who wilfully disregards the minimum data security requirements (Art. 61(c) FADP). Such fines are generally imposed on the responsible individual; the company itself only exceptionally, up to CHF 50,000 (Art. 64 FADP).
- **Protection in criminal proceedings.** A report may only be used in criminal proceedings against the person who reported with their consent (Art. 24(6) FADP). According to the guide, this also applies to voluntary reports.
- **Freedom of information.** Reports count as official documents and are, in principle, accessible under the Freedom of Information Act. The FDPIC assesses restrictions case by case. Write factually and avoid speculation.
- **Voluntary reports.** You can report even without a high risk, for example when many people are affected and the media may take an interest. According to the guide, voluntary reports are made outside the portal, for example via the [contact form for controllers](https://www.edoeb.admin.ch/de/kontakt-fuer-verantwortliche).

With your consent, the FDPIC can also forward the report to the BACS for analysis (Art. 24(5bis) FADP).

## When the breach happens at your IT provider

Many SMEs have third parties process personal data: IT providers, cloud services, payroll bureaus. These processors must report every breach to you "as soon as possible", whether or not the risk is high (Art. 24(3) FADP, guide section 1.2). The report to the FDPIC remains your job as controller. Check your data processing agreements: do they say whom the provider notifies, how fast and with what details? Without that, you lose time when it matters.

The same principle applies internally. Employees need to know whom to tell about an incident straight away, even if it is embarrassing. How to set up that channel is covered in [Internal incident reporting](/security/blog/internal-incident-reporting).

## Who does what in your business

Most SMEs have no data protection department, so decide before an incident who submits the report, who informs management and who drafts the notice to the people affected. IT supplies the technical facts, HR knows the staff lists, and finance knows where payment data sits. Keep the portal link and these roles in your incident handbook, along with your company name, address and UID. For the wider picture of FADP duties, see [Swiss FADP obligations for SMEs](/security/blog/swiss-fadp-obligations-for-smes).

Reporting to the FDPIC does not replace other notifications. If you have cyber insurance, your policy will usually require its own, often very early, claim notification, so check the terms. For theft, hacking or extortion, also file a criminal complaint with the police.

## Record it, even without a report

Under Art. 15(4) DPO, you must document breaches: the facts, the effects and the measures taken. The ordinance requires you to keep this for at least two years from the report. For incidents you do not report, we recommend counting the two years from your decision not to report. In that case, record your reasoning: which data, why no high risk, who decided and when? If the FDPIC, your insurer or your auditor asks, this entry is your evidence.

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li>Date and time of the incident and its discovery</li>
  <li>Who reported it internally, who handled it?</li>
  <li>Systems, data and groups of people affected</li>
  <li>Risk assessment with reasons (report: yes or no)</li>
  <li>Measures with dates</li>
  <li>FDPIC confirmation and a copy of the notice to those affected</li>
</ul>

## A fictional example: ransomware at a builder

A construction firm with 70 employees in the canton of Aargau. The example is made up. Overnight into Monday, ransomware encrypts the file server. On Monday morning, IT finds a ransom note. The attackers claim to have copied HR files and customer data.

- **Monday, 08:00:** IT disconnects the server and informs management. The external IT partner secures the logs.
- **Monday, 11:00:** management assesses: HR files with AHV numbers and some medical certificates, exfiltration not ruled out. High risk is likely.
- **Monday, 15:00:** report via the FDPIC portal with the known details, noting "forensics ongoing". Confirmation filed.
- **Wednesday:** forensics confirms one folder was exfiltrated. Follow-up report with the number of people affected. Letter to employees, email to customers with a phishing warning.
- **Following week:** log closed, measures agreed: multi-factor authentication, separate backups, training.

The company reported before it had certainty. That is exactly what the guide requires.

## Frequently asked questions

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>When do I have to report a data breach in Switzerland?</h3>
  <p>When the breach is likely to result in a high risk to the people affected, and as soon as possible after you become aware of it. If the risk is low, you only record the incident internally.</p>
  <h3>Does the 72-hour deadline apply in Switzerland?</h3>
  <p>No. The FADP requires a report "as soon as possible". The 72 hours come from Art. 33 GDPR and only apply if your processing also falls under the GDPR. In that case, you report to both authorities.</p>
  <h3>Where do I report a data breach in Switzerland?</h3>
  <p>Private companies report to the FDPIC via databreach.edoeb.admin.ch. Cantonal and municipal bodies report to their cantonal data protection authority. Critical infrastructure operators also report cyberattacks to the BACS within 24 hours.</p>
  <h3>What happens if I do not report a data breach?</h3>
  <p>Failing to report is not a criminal offence under the FADP. The FDPIC can still order the report, open an investigation and impose further measures. If minimum data security requirements were wilfully ignored, a fine of up to CHF 250,000 is possible.</p>
  <h3>Do I have to report a cyberattack without data loss?</h3>
  <p>To the FDPIC, only if personal data is affected and the risk is high. Critical infrastructure operators report cyberattacks to the BACS regardless. All other companies can report to the BACS voluntarily.</p>
</div>
