## Deadlines and incoming mail in a law firm

<p class="blog-lede">Deadlines from incoming mail in a law firm are handled in four steps: recognise court and authority mail, put it on the right file, propose the date with the page it came from, and enter it only after a lawyer confirms.</p>

The calculation of the legal time limit stays with the lawyer. A workflow may copy a date it can see. It may not decide what the procedure allows. This is office orientation, not legal advice.

This is for the secretariat and the partners of a small Swiss firm. The wider question of what secrecy allows, including why this mail does not belong in a free chat account, is in [AI for Swiss law firms](/prozess-check/blog/ai-for-law-firms). This page is only the path from the envelope to the deadline register.

## What counts as this mail

Not every letter has a deadline. The stream that matters here is mail from a court, prosecutor, authority or opposing lawyer that refers to a proceeding you already have, or that opens one. Invoices from the court cashier, journals and event invitations are not this job, even if they mention a date.

The letter may arrive on paper or as a message in the mailbox the firm uses for service. How paper becomes a file is its own work. Once the letter is a document the secretariat can open, the deadline steps are the same. The workflow looks for a case reference, a party name and a date or a phrase that a time limit is running. If it cannot see those, it does not invent them. The item goes to a person with the reason: no reference, no date, or more than one date.

Commercial notice periods, leases, insurance and software licences are a different register. That job is in [tracking contract notice periods](/prozess-check/blog/contract-deadlines). Do not mix a lease renewal into the court list. The consequence of a wrong date is not the same kind of work, and the person who checks it is not the same person.

<ol class="blog-steps">
  <li data-step="1"><h3>Mark court and authority mail</h3><p>Separate it from invoices, journals and marketing. Unclear items stay in a queue with a reason.</p></li>
  <li data-step="2"><h3>Propose the file</h3><p>Match the reference on the letter to the matter number you already use. Several matches, or none, go to the secretariat.</p></li>
  <li data-step="3"><h3>Propose the date, with the page</h3><p>Copy the date that is written, and keep the page beside it. Do not calculate. The field is a proposal, not the firm's deadline.</p></li>
  <li data-step="4"><h3>The lawyer confirms</h3><p>Only a confirmed date enters the register. Reminders use the intervals the firm chose, and critical dates get a second look the firm itself required.</p></li>
</ol>

## Assign the letter to the file before anyone trusts the date

A date without a file is a note on a desk. The reference on the letter, the case number the court prints, is what the firm already stores on the matter. A workflow can propose that match: exact reference first, then party name only if the firm has one open matter for that party. Two open matters for the same name, or a reference that does not exist, are not a guess. They wait.

The proposal should show why it matched, in plain words: "reference on page one equals matter 2024-118" is the sort of line a person can check in a second. A match that only says "likely" is not ready.

[Art. 13 para. 2 of the Lawyers Act](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2002/153/de#art_13) requires lawyers to see that assistants keep professional secrecy. Filing this mail is the assistants' work, so the workflow runs in the system the firm already uses for the file. It does not send the letter out to be read in an account the firm does not control. That limit is the point of the secrecy page linked above, not a second essay here.

## The date is a proposal until the lawyer confirms

Many letters print a date. Some print a period and leave the counting to the reader. Some print several dates: a hearing, a submission date, a date the decision was sent. A workflow can list every date it sees, each with the sentence around it, and point at the page. It stops there.

[Art. 12 let. a BGFA](https://www.fedlex.admin.ch/eli/cc/2002/153/de#art_12) says lawyers practise carefully and conscientiously. Confirming the firm's deadline is part of that care. The lawyer decides which date controls, whether anything is added or not, and what the register should say. This page does not describe that calculation. No day-count from a statute belongs here, because a wrong one would be worse than none.

The register entry, after confirmation, needs four fields the secretariat can trust: the matter, the date, what it is in the firm's own words ("submission", "hearing", "internal check"), and who confirmed it. A proposal that was rejected stays visible as rejected, so the next letter is not silently dropped.

Reminders are the firm's choice. A common pattern is a note a set number of office days before, and a morning list for the week. Critical matters may add a second person on the reminder as a house rule, not a statutory duty.

## A made-up example

A made-up example: a three-person firm, one secretariat. On a Tuesday a PDF arrives from an address already treated as a court mailbox. The document, invented for this story, shows a case reference that equals one open matter, and two dates in the text: a hearing on a Thursday in April, and a sentence that a submission is due earlier in April. No numbers here are a real case.

The workflow proposes the matter because the reference matches exactly, and it lists both dates with the sentences. It does not pick one. The lawyer opens the PDF, keeps the submission date as the firm's deadline, treats the hearing as a separate entry, and changes the submission date by two office days for a reason she writes on the entry. Only then does the secretariat's register show them, with her name as the person who confirmed.

A second letter the same day has no reference and two possible client names. It stays in the queue. The reminder for the confirmed submission is the one the firm uses for every confirmed court date: a note five office days before, to the lawyer and to the secretariat. Five days is this firm's setting in the story, not a legal period.

## When to leave it, and when the Process Check fits

Leave it if the firm receives a handful of court letters a month and the lawyer already reads each one the hour it arrives. A register on top of a habit that already works adds a place for two dates to diverge. Leave the calculation in the software if your practice system already forces a confirmation and your people use it. The gap is the letter that sits in a personal inbox until someone remembers.
