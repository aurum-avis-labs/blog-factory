<p class="blog-lede">A go, pivot or stop decision is a written choice at the end of a validation window: continue on the current promise, change one big assumption, or end the experiment before more calendar and cash disappear. It is not a mood, a board vote dressed up as data, or a spreadsheet that invents certainty.</p>

## Go, pivot or stop decision

You reach a **go, pivot or stop decision** when you have run the test you said you would run, with analytics you trust enough to argue about, and you can answer what would have falsified the idea. **Go** means the original learning goal is still the right bet and the numbers or behaviour support spending the next phase on depth, not on redefining the product. **Pivot** means one named assumption failed or weakened while something else stayed interesting: segment, channel, pricing band, core job, or onboarding. **Stop** means the evidence is clear enough that the current path does not deserve more build or acquisition spend, and you document why so the team does not relitigate the same scope in three months.

This is for founders and innovation leads in Switzerland who already shipped a narrow MVP or finished a structured validation engagement and must decide what happens in quarter two. Product-market fit is a longer pattern than one test; how those patterns show up in behaviour is in [product-market fit: how to find it](/blog/product-market-fit-how-to-find-it). Whether you should have run a fake door instead of a product loop is a different read: [fake door test vs real MVP](/blog/fake-door-test-vs-mvp).

<ul class="blog-checklist">
  <li><strong>Hypothesis on paper.</strong> The user, the core path, and the success rule you wrote before launch, with dates.</li>
  <li><strong>Evidence ledger.</strong> Sessions, activation, cost per lead, conversion steps, and qualitative notes, each with who supplied the traffic.</li>
  <li><strong>Sample honesty.</strong> Separate friends and family from strangers; note if one channel or one post dominated the spike.</li>
  <li><strong>Pivot log.</strong> What you changed in scope, copy, or segment during the window, and what moved after each change.</li>
  <li><strong>Residual unknowns.</strong> What you still did not test: payments, a second language, operations at volume, or a corporate buyer.</li>
  <li><strong>Named outcome.</strong> One line: go, pivot, or stop, plus the single assumption that drove the call.</li>
</ul>

## What each outcome commits you to

**Go** is not «everyone is excited». It is «the falsification we agreed on did not happen, and the next spend tests scale or depth, not a new idea every fortnight». Typical go moves: widen acquisition within the same segment, harden the core path, add one adjacent feature that the same user asked for repeatedly, or prepare a board update with the same metrics continued for another cycle.

**Pivot** keeps one thread. You name the thread: «students will post jobs» failed but «employers will post shifts» got inbound; «self-serve onboarding» failed but «white-glove setup for three firms» produced repeat use. A pivot without a named change is usually go with extra features, which burns runway without learning.

**Stop** is legitimate when the measure missed by a margin you defined in advance, when strangers never reach the core path despite honest traffic, or when the economics of acquisition and delivery cannot close even in an optimistic model. Stop should still produce a short memo: what you tried, what you learned, what you will not fund again. That memo is how you avoid quietly restarting the same MVP with a new logo.

<ol class="blog-flow">
  <li><strong>Measure</strong><p>Weeks of live usage, funnels, and paid tests on the promise you shipped.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Compare</strong><p>Outcomes against the pre-written rule, not against hope or a competitor's press release.</p></li>
  <li><strong>Choose</strong><p>Go, pivot, or stop, with explicit limits on what the data still do not prove.</p></li>
</ol>

## What the decision does not prove

Even a disciplined go does not prove product-market fit, a fundable round, or that your first channel will scale nationwide. A pivot does not prove the new assumption; it only earns the right to test it. Stop does not prove the idea was worthless in every market; it proves this segment, promise, and scope did not earn the next cheque under the rules you set.

Internal builds show the same split: interest in one metric and silence in another, without any need for a magic score. The useful read is which assumption stayed open after honest traffic.

<aside class="blog-callout blog-callout--limit">
  <span class="blog-kicker">Limit</span>
  <p>There is no universal scoring formula that replaces judgment. If someone sells you a weighted matrix that outputs «go» without sample size, traffic mix, and a falsification rule, treat it as theatre. The decision is only as good as the experiment you ran and the honesty of the ledger.</p>
</aside>

## A made-up example: week twelve in Zurich

A made-up example: a two-founder team in Zurich, not a client. They validated a B2B tool that turns meeting notes into tasks for agencies. The rule was four agencies outside their network each completing the import path twice without a founder on the call within ten weeks. Paid LinkedIn ran in weeks five to nine. By week twelve only three agencies finished once, two via an investor, and cost per qualified signup sat above the band in their brief. Activation fell when notes came from a client folder instead of paste. They chose **pivot**: paste-only input and a human review queue, with a new rule of eight stranger reviews in six weeks. **Go** would have cleared the four-agency import bar; **stop** would have meant fewer than two strangers finishing the paste path after honest spend. Residual unknowns: pricing, seats, and payment without an integration. One page to the board, no score.

<div class="blog-faq">
  <h3>Is stop a failure?</h3>
  <p>It saves budget for a better bet. Failure is funding the same scope after the measure missed without updating the hypothesis.</p>
</div>
